Assigning an arbitrary penalty to an orbit that fails to reach its destination can cause orbital optimization to return such an orbit as the minimizer, depending on the size of the penalty.
Here we restrict the problem to a verifiable two-body setting and optimize using a variable that represents only reachable orbits.
We do not consider gravity assists using a third body's gravity; integration of the three-body problem in a rotating frame is covered in [Events and conserved quantities in the restricted three-body problem](../orbital-mechanics/).
Optimization involving the encounter phase with a planet, finite-duration thrust, or collision avoidance cannot be solved from these two-body results alone.

This page derives the vis-viva equation from energy conservation and expresses the sum of two velocity changes as a function of the transfer orbit's aphelion radius.
We minimize this function over an interval using `ScalarMinimizer` and compare with the analytical value to confirm that the minimizer is the Hohmann transfer.
Finally, we integrate the optimized ellipse over half a period with `DormandPrince853` to check its aphelion radius by a separate computational route.

## Problem setup and nondimensionalization

We treat the spacecraft as a point mass subject only to the central body's gravity (assuming the Sun), giving a two-body problem.
The departure and target orbits are coplanar circles with radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ ($r_2>r_1$).
Velocity changes occur through instantaneous burns (impulses); we neglect mass changes due to propellant consumption and the planets' own gravity.
The cost is the sum $\Delta v=\lvert\Delta\mathbf v_1\rvert+\lvert\Delta\mathbf v_2\rvert$ of the magnitudes of the two velocity changes.
For constant exhaust velocity, the rocket equation determines the propellant mass ratio solely from $\Delta v$, so minimizing $\Delta v$ is equivalent to minimizing propellant consumption.

Let the gravitational constant be $\mu=GM$, and choose $r_1$ as the unit of length and $\sqrt{r_1^3/\mu}$ as the unit of time.
In these units, $\mu=1$, the departure circular orbit has radius and speed both equal to 1, and its period is $2\pi$.
The speed unit $\sqrt{\mu/r_1}$ is the speed of the departure circular orbit.
The only remaining input is the target radius ratio $R=r_2/r_1$.

For an orbit around the Sun with $r_1=1\,\mathrm{au}$, the speed unit is approximately $29.78\,\mathrm{km/s}$, and the time unit is $1/(2\pi)$ of a year.
These are educational values that approximate planetary orbits as circles: Mars corresponds to $R\approx1.524$ and Jupiter to $R\approx5.203$.
They are not values for actual flight planning based on an ephemeris.

The nondimensional equations of motion for position $\mathbf r=(x,y)$ and $r=\lvert\mathbf r\rvert$ are

$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\frac{\mathbf r}{r^3}
$$


The state components are ordered as $(x,y,v_x,v_y)$.

## The vis-viva equation

The elliptical-orbit relations used here follow from two conserved quantities.
The equations of motion give zero time derivatives for the specific energy $\varepsilon=v^2/2-1/r$ and specific angular momentum $h=\lvert\mathbf r\times\mathbf v\rvert$.

Let the perihelion distance be $r_p$ and the aphelion distance be $r_a$.
At these two points, velocity is perpendicular to the radius vector, so $h=r_pv_p=r_av_a$.
Substituting $v_a=r_pv_p/r_a$ into energy conservation $v_p^2/2-1/r_p=v_a^2/2-1/r_a$ and solving for $v_p$ gives

$$
v_p^2=\frac{2r_a}{r_p(r_p+r_a)}
$$

follows.
Substituting this back into $\varepsilon$ gives $\varepsilon=-1/(r_p+r_a)$.
Using the semimajor axis $a=(r_p+r_a)/2$ gives $\varepsilon=-1/(2a)$, and at any point on the orbit,

$$
v^2=\frac2r-\frac1a
$$


This is the vis-viva equation.
Kepler's third law gives the half-period as $\pi a^{3/2}$.

## A variable that satisfies the arrival condition

We accelerate tangentially at departure to enter an elliptical orbit (the transfer orbit) with perihelion 1 and aphelion $a_p$.
The condition for reaching the target radius $R$ is $a_p\ge R$, which we use as the variable's domain.
The objective then represents a reachable orbit at every trial point, eliminating the need to penalize unreachable orbits.

The transfer orbit's semimajor axis is $a=(1+a_p)/2$.
Substituting $r_p=1$ and $r_a=a_p$ into the expression for $v_p^2$ above gives the specific angular momentum $h=v_p=\sqrt{2a_p/(1+a_p)}$.
At the target radius, angular momentum conservation gives the tangential velocity $v_t=h/R$, and the vis-viva equation gives the squared total speed $v^2=2/R-1/a$.
Putting the terms of the squared radial velocity $v_r^2=v^2-v_t^2$ over a common denominator gives

$$
v_r^2=\frac2R-\frac{2}{1+a_p}-\frac{2a_p}{R^2(1+a_p)}
=\frac{2(R-1)(a_p-R)}{R^2(1+a_p)}\ge0
$$


The factored right-hand side remains nonnegative after rounding when $a_p\ge R$.
Computing the original three-term form causes three terms of magnitude roughly $2/R$ to cancel when $a_p$ is close to $R$, leaving an absolute error of order $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$ in $v_r^2$.
Here $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$ is the double-precision machine epsilon $2^{-52}\approx2.2\times10^{-16}$.
Computing $v_r$ itself amplifies the error through the square root to order $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}$, but the objective below adds $v_r^2$ to a positive term inside the square root, so either form keeps the objective error to order $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$.
Also, when $R\le a_p\le2R$, Sterbenz's lemma ensures that the difference $a_p-R$ is computed without rounding error.

## Velocity increments for the Hohmann transfer

The departure circular orbit has speed 1, and we accelerate tangentially to $h$ at perihelion, giving the first velocity increment $h-1$.
Upon reaching the target radius, the velocity is $(v_r,v_t)$, while the target circular orbit has tangential velocity $R^{-1/2}$.
The second burn cancels this vector difference, so the total velocity increment is

$$
J(a_p)=h-1+\sqrt{v_r^2+\left(v_t-R^{-1/2}\right)^2},\qquad a_p\in[R,\infty)
$$


At $a_p=R$, we have $v_r=0$, and the transfer orbit is tangent to the target circular orbit.
This is the Hohmann transfer, in which both burns are tangential.
Substituting $h=\sqrt{2R/(1+R)}$ and $v_t=h/R$ gives the independent reference value

$$
\Delta v_H=\sqrt{\frac{2R}{1+R}}-1+
\frac1{\sqrt R}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac2{1+R}}\right)
$$


At $R=2$, the increments are $\Delta v_1\approx0.154701$ and $\Delta v_2\approx0.129757$, totaling $\Delta v_H\approx0.284457$.
For Mars at $R=1.524$, the total is approximately $5.60\,\mathrm{km/s}$, and the transfer duration (half-period) is about 259 days.

The positive right derivative at the endpoint shows that $a_p=R$ is at least a local minimizer.
Setting $D=R^{-1/2}-h/R\gt0$ and using $v_r^2=0$ at $a_p=R$ gives

$$
J'(R)=h'\left(1-\frac1R\right)+\frac{(v_r^2)'}{2D},\qquad
h'=\frac{1}{h(1+R)^2},\qquad (v_r^2)'=\frac{2(R-1)}{R^2(1+R)}
$$

and both terms are positive for $R\gt1$.
Its value is $J'(R)\approx0.690$ at $R=2$, about 1.52 at $R=1.2$, and about 0.158 at $R=5$.
Placing aphelion beyond the target increases the first burn and requires the second burn to cancel radial velocity as well; both increase the cost.
For $R=1.2,2,3,5$, sampling $[R,3R]$ in $10^4$ equal subdivisions shows that $J$ increases monotonically over the sampled range, and the minimum over this interval is also at $a_p=R$.

This establishes optimality within the family of two-burn transfers that are coplanar and depart from perihelion.
The Hohmann transfer is also known to minimize the cost among all two-burn transfers between circular orbits, but this page does not cover that proof.
Bi-elliptic transfers using three burns lie outside this family.
They first enter an ellipse with aphelion $r_b\gt R$, raise perihelion to $R$ at $r_b$, and finally decelerate into a circular orbit at $R$.
For $R\gt11.94$, the limiting bi-elliptic transfer as $r_b\to\infty$ has a smaller $\Delta v$ than the Hohmann transfer; for $R\gt15.58$, the bi-elliptic transfer is smaller for every value of $r_b\gt R$.
The Hohmann transfer's $\Delta v_H$ itself reaches a maximum of approximately 0.536 at $R\approx15.58$.
Over the cell's input range $1.2\le R\le5$, the Hohmann transfer is preferable to any bi-elliptic transfer, and the cell does not compute bi-elliptic transfers.
Bi-elliptic transfers take much longer.

## Bounded scalar minimization

The optimization problem minimizes $J(a_p)$ over the closed interval $[R,3R]$ in one variable.
The upper bound $3R$ keeps the search interval finite; the monotonicity check shows that the interval contains the minimizer.
The minimizer is the lower endpoint rather than an interior local minimum, so a three-point bracket around the minimizer never exists.

Without using derivatives, `ScalarMinimizer` shrinks an interval containing the minimizer using only comparisons of function values ([Scalar minimization](oxref:optimize-scalar-minimization)).
Starting with `ScalarMinimumStart::Bounded` evaluates three points, the endpoints and midpoint, and places the point with the smallest value in the center.
Here the lower endpoint $a_p=R$ has the smallest value, so the three points become $[R,R,3R]$.

Golden-section search places the trial point on the longer side of the current best point, at $(3-\sqrt5)/2\approx0.382$ times that side's width.
For an interior minimizer, convergence is linear: each evaluation multiplies the bracket width by $(\sqrt5-1)/2\approx0.618$.
In the present problem with a minimum at the lower endpoint, the trial value is always greater than the lower endpoint's value, and the interval shrinks to $[R,u]$.
The new width is 0.382 times the previous width, so it shrinks faster than with an interior minimizer.

[Brent's method](oxref:optimize-brent-method) proposes the vertex of the parabola through the best point and the two other previously evaluated points with the smallest values.
It falls back to the golden-section point if the candidate lies outside the bracket or its distance from the best point is at least half the preceding step.
Parabolic interpolation gives superlinear convergence at an interior local minimum of a smooth function.
Here the best point remains at the endpoint and $J$ increases monotonically, so the parabola's vertex lies outside the bracket or fails the safeguard conditions.
Brent's method therefore uses the same trial points as golden-section search and offers no advantage for this endpoint minimum.
The cell's table lets you check that the two methods use equal numbers of evaluations.

Set both absolute and relative position tolerances to $\tau_x$ and both absolute and relative objective tolerances to $\tau_f$.
For the final lower bound $a$, upper bound $b$, and best point $x$, termination occurs with

$$
\lvert b-a\rvert\le\tau_x\left(1+\max(\lvert a\rvert,\lvert b\rvert)\right),\qquad
\lvert f(a)-f(x)\rvert,\ \lvert f(b)-f(x)\rvert\le\tau_f\left(1+\max(\lvert f(a)\rvert,\lvert f(x)\rvert,\lvert f(b)\rvert)\right)
$$

the status `Converged` when both conditions hold simultaneously.
For the default `ScalarMinimumOptions`, $\tau_x=\tau_f=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}\approx1.49\times10^{-8}$ (the same absolute and relative values), with a limit of 1000 iterations and 2000 evaluations.
At $R=2$, we need to reduce the width $2R=4$ to approximately $4.5\times10^{-8}$ or less; after $k$ reductions by 0.382, $k\ge\ln(4/4.5\times10^{-8})/\ln(1/0.382)\approx19.0$, giving $k=20$ reductions.
The initial three evaluations plus about 20 reductions give an evaluation count in the low twenties.
The ratio of the initial width $2R$ to the stopping threshold $\tau_x(1+R)$ is of order $1/\tau_x$ independently of $R$, so changing $R$ barely changes the evaluation count.

The returned minimizer is exactly the endpoint evaluated initially.
The method `x()` returns the point with the smallest observed value, breaking ties in favor of the point evaluated first.
Thus the returned $a_p$ equals $R$, and the difference between the objective and $\Delta v_H$ is of the order of rounding error.
The reduced interval width indicates that the neighborhood of the endpoint has been searched to the stopping tolerance; it does not prove that no smaller value exists within the interval.

## Verification by orbit integration

We numerically integrate the equations of motion to check whether an orbit given speed $h$ at perihelion really has aphelion radius $a_p$.
Starting at perihelion $(1,0)$ with velocity $(0,h)$ and integrating for the half-period $\pi a^{3/2}$ takes the exact solution to aphelion $(-a_p,0)$.
This check does not use the formula for $J$; it verifies that the vis-viva equation and Kepler's third law agree with the equations of motion.

The method `DormandPrince853` is a 12-stage explicit Runge–Kutta method of order 8.
It estimates local error by combining embedded estimates of orders 5 and 3 ([Local error estimation with embedded pairs](oxref:ode-embedded-pairs)) and adjusts the step size automatically.
From the second step onward, each step evaluates the right-hand side 12 times.
Both absolute and relative tolerances are $10^{-11}$; a step is accepted if every component's local error estimate $e_i$ satisfies $\lvert e_i\rvert\le10^{-11}\left(1+\max(\lvert y_{n,i}\rvert,\lvert y_{n+1,i}\rvert)\right)$ for the values $y_{n,i}$ and $y_{n+1,i}$ before and after the step (maximum norm, [Error normalization and acceptance](oxref:ode-error-normalization)).
High-order methods need fewer steps for the smooth, nonstiff two-body problem when tight tolerances are imposed.

The local error tolerance does not directly bound the global error, but the table confirms that for an integration over about half a period, the global error also remains close to the tolerance in magnitude.
At aphelion, $\dot r=0$, so a time offset $\delta t$ affects the radius only to order $\delta t^2$.
The radius difference therefore measures the error in the orbit's shape (energy and angular momentum), while the phase error appears to first order in the table's $y$ component.
Examining both checks the orbit's shape and passage time.

## Objective error and minimizer accuracy

The objective on this page is in closed form, with only rounding error of order $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$.
In orbital problems, objective values are also often computed by an inner numerical ODE integration.
In that case, the objective has an additional error $\delta$ whose magnitude depends on the integration tolerance.
This error is not smooth in $a_p$: it jumps whenever the step sequence changes and therefore acts as noise for the minimization method.

The effect of noise varies greatly with the type of minimum.
For an endpoint minimum with slope $s=J'(R)\gt0$, as in this problem, points whose values differ by at most $\delta$ cannot be distinguished, giving the position error

$$
\delta x\approx\frac{\delta}{s}
$$


At $R=2$, if $\delta=10^{-8}$, then $\delta x\approx1.4\times10^{-8}$.
At a smooth interior local minimum, $J(x^*+\delta x)-J(x^*)\approx\tfrac12J''(x^*)\delta x^2$, so

$$
\delta x\approx\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{J''(x^*)}}
$$

and the position error grows with the square root of $\delta$.
For example, $J''=1$ and $\delta=10^{-8}$ give $\delta x\approx1.4\times10^{-4}$.
The error in the minimum value itself, however, remains of order $\delta$ in either case.
Noise has little effect if only the value of $\Delta v$ is needed, but has a larger effect when the optimal orbit's shape is needed.

Noise also affects stopping decisions and method selection.
If the objective tolerance $\tau_f$ is smaller than the noise, the value-difference condition may be difficult to meet, causing the interval to shrink to floating-point resolution and stop with `Stagnation`, or reach the evaluation limit.
In that case, use `with_function_tolerance` to set $\tau_f$ above the noise magnitude.
Brent's parabola estimates curvature from differences at three nearby points, so it becomes more sensitive to noise as the points approach one another.
Golden-section search uses only value comparisons, so noise does not change how the interval shrinks; however, if noise reverses a comparison, the method may retain the side that does not contain the minimizer.

Determining arrival through an inner integration also creates a difficulty specific to the optimal point $a_p=R$.
The orbit at $a_p=R$ only touches the target radius at aphelion, and $r-R$ does not change sign.
Event detection based on sign changes may therefore miss arrival precisely at the optimal point.
This page avoids both this difficulty and noise by encoding the arrival condition in the variable's domain and evaluating the objective in closed form.

## Optimization and orbit verification

For the input target radius $R$, the following cell minimizes $J$ over $[R,3R]$ using golden-section search and Brent's method, then integrates the optimal ellipse to check its aphelion radius.
All input and output lengths and speeds are dimensionless: the length unit is the departure circular orbit's radius, and the speed unit is its speed.

In the optimization part, the objective is the closure `|apo: f64| Ok(…)`, which implements `ScalarObjective` as `Fn(f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError>`.
Trial points that return nonfinite values are rejected and treated as larger than any finite value.
An error returned by the objective stops the search and is returned directly from `minimize`.
The constructor `Interval::new(radius, 3.0 * radius)` creates a closed interval, returning `NonFinite` for nonfinite endpoints and `InvalidInput` if the lower endpoint exceeds the upper endpoint.
Since `Interval` is `Copy`, the same interval can be reused for both methods.
The object `ScalarMinimizer::new(method, ScalarMinimumOptions::default())` holds the method and default settings, and `minimize(&objective, start)` solves the problem while borrowing the objective.
The workspace consists solely of three internal fixed-size points; users need not provide one.
Results are returned as `Ok` even when an iteration or evaluation limit is reached, and the termination status indicates that the search did not succeed.

In the result, `x()` and `fun()` are the best point and value, `fun_evals()` counts evaluations including the initial three, and `termination()` is `OptimizeTermination`.
The value `samples()` contains the final lower bound, best point, and upper bound; the final interval width in the table is the difference between its upper and lower bounds.
Since the minimizer is an endpoint, `bracket()` is `None` and is unused in the cell.
The method `is_success()` is true only for `Converged`.
Success means a local minimum satisfying the stopping criteria, rather than a proof of a global minimum.

In the orbit integration part, `OdeFn::new(4, …)` wraps a four-dimensional right-hand-side function.
The right-hand-side closure takes time, a borrowed state `y`, and the destination `dy`; the components are ordered as $(x,y,v_x,v_y)$.
The problem `OdeProblem::new(&system, end)` borrows the right-hand side and holds the final time, without events.
The default tolerances of `OdeOptions::new()` are absolute $10^{-6}$ and relative $10^{-3}$ (root-mean-square norm), so we replace them with `with_error_tolerance` and `ErrorTolerance::scalar(1e-11, 1e-11, NormSelection::Infinity)`.
The constructor `OdeState::from_slice` creates a state holding the initial time and a copy of the initial values.
The constructor `workspace` allocates the workspace, and `advance` overwrites `state` with the state at the final time.
The sink `&mut ()` discards intermediate output, and `ExecutionContext::sequential()` is an execution context that runs sequentially on the calling thread.
The report's `termination()` returns `Option<OdeTermination>` (always `Some`), and `ReachedEnd` indicates that the final time was reached.
Stopping at a step limit or similar condition also returns `Ok`, so the cell checks `is_success()` to set the outcome to `partial`.

```rust
//| id: app-orbital-transfer
//| ref: app-orbital-transfer
//| title: "Minimize the velocity increments of a reachable ellipse"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-optimize, scioxide-ode]
//| inputs:
//|   radius: {"type":"range","label":"Target radius / departure radius [-]","min":1.2,"max":5,"step":0.2,"value":2}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"near","label":"R = 1.2","description":"The target is close, and the endpoint slope J′(R) is large.","inputs":{"radius":1.2}}
//|   - {"id":"far","label":"R = 5","description":"A ratio close to Jupiter's orbit. Integration needs more steps.","inputs":{"radius":5}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"optimization","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"max":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"optimization","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":2,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"orbit","path":"/rows/0/2","range":{"max":1e-8}}
//|       - {"artifact":"orbit","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"min":-1e-8,"max":1e-8}}
//|       - {"path":"/outputs/2/state","equals":"satisfied"}
//|   - case: near
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"optimization","path":"/rows/1/3","range":{"max":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"orbit","path":"/rows/0/2","range":{"max":1e-8}}
//|   - case: far
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"optimization","path":"/rows/1/3","range":{"max":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"orbit","path":"/rows/0/2","range":{"max":1e-8}}
//|       - {"path":"/outputs/2/state","equals":"satisfied"}
use scioxide_core::{ErrorTolerance, ExecutionContext, Interval, NormSelection};
use scioxide_ode::{DormandPrince853, OdeFn, OdeMethod, OdeOptions, OdeProblem, OdeState};
use scioxide_optimize::{
    ScalarMinimizer, ScalarMinimumMethod, ScalarMinimumOptions, ScalarMinimumStart,
};
let sig6 = serde_json::json!({"significantDigits": 6});
// J(a_p) = (h − 1) + √(v_r² + (h/R − R^{−1/2})²). Compute v_r² in factored form.
let objective = |apo: f64| {
    let h = (2.0 * apo / (1.0 + apo)).sqrt();
    let radial2 = 2.0 * (radius - 1.0) * (apo - radius) / (radius * radius * (1.0 + apo));
    Ok(h - 1.0 + (radial2 + (h / radius - 1.0 / radius.sqrt()).powi(2)).sqrt())
};
// Closed form for the Hohmann transfer Δv. An independent reference using f64 inherent methods.
let exact = (2.0 * radius / (1.0 + radius)).sqrt() - 1.0
    + (1.0 - (2.0 / (1.0 + radius)).sqrt()) / radius.sqrt();
let bounds = Interval::new(radius, 3.0 * radius).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut rows = Vec::new();
let mut best = None;
let mut successful = true;
for method in [
    ScalarMinimumMethod::GoldenSection,
    ScalarMinimumMethod::Brent,
] {
    let result = ScalarMinimizer::new(method, ScalarMinimumOptions::default())
        .minimize(&objective, ScalarMinimumStart::Bounded(bounds))
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    successful &= result.is_success();
    rows.push((
        format!("{method:?}"),
        result.x(),
        result.fun(),
        (result.fun() - exact).abs(),
        result.fun_evals(),
        // The width between the final lower and upper bounds. With an endpoint minimum, bracket() is None.
        result.samples().map(|[low, _, high]| high.x() - low.x()),
        result.termination().to_string(),
    ));
    best = Some(result.x());
}
let apo = best.ok_or("No optimization result")?;
// Depart from perihelion (1, 0) with velocity (0, h) and examine aphelion (−a_p, 0) after the half-period π a^{3/2}.
let semi = (1.0 + apo) / 2.0;
let end = core::f64::consts::PI * semi.powf(1.5);
// Component order: (x, y, v_x, v_y); x'' = −x/r³, y'' = −y/r³.
let system = OdeFn::new(4, |_: f64, y: &[f64], dy: &mut [f64]| {
    let r3 = y[0].hypot(y[1]).powi(3);
    dy.copy_from_slice(&[y[2], y[3], -y[0] / r3, -y[1] / r3]);
    Ok(())
})
.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let problem = OdeProblem::new(&system, end);
let method = DormandPrince853::new();
let options = OdeOptions::new().with_error_tolerance(
    ErrorTolerance::scalar(1e-11, 1e-11, NormSelection::Infinity).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,
);
let mut state = OdeState::from_slice(0.0, &[1.0, 0.0, 0.0, (2.0 * apo / (1.0 + apo)).sqrt()])
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut ws = method
    .workspace(&problem, &options)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let report = method
    .advance(
        &problem,
        &options,
        &mut state,
        &mut (),
        &mut ws,
        ExecutionContext::sequential(),
    )
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let termination = report.termination().ok_or("No integration termination status")?;
let final_radius = state.values()[0].hypot(state.values()[1]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    serde_json::json!([
        {"key": "0", "label": "Method", "type": "string", "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "1", "label": "Aphelion radius [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "2", "label": "Δv [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "3", "label": "Analytic error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "4", "label": "Evaluations", "type": "number", "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "5", "label": "Final interval width [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "6", "label": "Termination reason", "type": "string", "nullText": "Undefined"}
    ]),
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({"id": "optimization"})
);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    serde_json::json!([
        {"key": "0", "label": "Radius after integration [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "1", "label": "Ellipse aphelion [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "2", "label": "Absolute difference [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"},
        {"key": "3", "label": "y after integration [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": "Undefined"}
    ]),
    vec![(
        final_radius,
        apo,
        (final_radius - apo).abs(),
        state.values()[1],
    )],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({"id": "orbit"})
);
let outcome = if successful && report.is_success() {
    Outcome::satisfied("Optimization and orbit integration completed")
} else {
    Outcome::partial("At least one computation is incomplete")
};
emit_outcome!(outcome.status(format!("{termination:?}")).diagnostic(
    "Accepted steps",
    report.accepted_steps() as f64,
    None
));
```

The optimization table has one row per method, showing the minimizer's aphelion radius, $\Delta v$, its difference from the analytical value $\Delta v_H$, the evaluation count, the final interval width, and the termination reason.
For the default $R=2$, both methods return aphelion radius 2 and $\Delta v$ approximately 0.284457, differing from the analytical value by the order of rounding error, and stop with `Converged`.
The evaluation count and final interval width (about $1.7\times10^{-8}$) agree between the two methods, consistent with Brent's parabolic candidates never being used.
The orbit table shows the difference between the radius after integration and aphelion $a_p$, and the $y$ component after integration; both are of order $10^{-11}$ by default.
The outcome diagnostic is the number of accepted steps, about 30 by default.
Including rejected steps gives approximately 420 right-hand-side evaluations.
ODE integration accounts for most of the computation time; the twenty-odd objective evaluations are negligible.

Changing the input shows the following.
Reducing $R$ to 1.2 lowers $\Delta v_H$ to approximately 0.0869 and increases the endpoint slope $J'(R)$ to approximately 1.52.
Increasing $R$ to 5 gives $\Delta v_H$ approximately 0.480 and increases the accepted step count to about 37 because the half-period is longer.
The evaluation count barely changes with $R$ because the interval shrinks toward the endpoint by a factor of 0.382 independently of $R$.
The final interval width is the first width below the stopping threshold $\tau_x(1+R)$, so it lies between 0.38 and 1 times that threshold and is not monotone in $R$ (about $2.7\times10^{-8}$ at $R=1.2$ and $4.4\times10^{-8}$ at $R=5$).

Problems appear in the tables as follows.
A termination reason other than `Converged` means that the best point was returned but the stopping criteria were not met.
An aphelion radius away from $R$ indicates an error in the objective formula or interval settings.
If the difference from the analytical value greatly exceeds $10^{-12}$, suspect a mismatch between the objective and reference formula.
If the radius difference after integration or the $y$ component exceeds $10^{-8}$, suspect the tolerance or integration-time settings.
Changing the cell's tolerance $10^{-11}$ to $10^{-6}$ makes the radius difference at $R=2$ approximately $3\times10^{-7}$; the radius difference and $y$ component grow roughly in proportion to the tolerance.

## Summary and further reading

Encoding the arrival condition $a_p\ge R$ in the variable's domain makes the problem a bounded scalar minimization without introducing penalties for unreachable orbits.
The minimum is the interval endpoint $a_p=R$; the endpoint slope $J'(R)\gt0$ and monotone increase over the interval show that the Hohmann transfer is optimal within this family.
At an endpoint minimum, golden-section search and Brent's method have the same evaluation count, and each evaluation shrinks the interval by a factor of 0.382.
The difference from the analytical expression $\Delta v_H$ and the aphelion radius and $y$ component obtained by orbit integration independently check the objective and the orbit's shape and phase, respectively.
When evaluating an objective through an ODE, estimate the position error from noise of magnitude $\delta$ as $\delta/J'$ for an endpoint minimum and $\sqrt{2\delta/J''}$ for an interior local minimum, and set $\tau_f$ larger than the noise.
