How do stripes and spots on an organism's surface form spontaneously during development?
In his 1952 paper, *The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis*, Alan Turing showed that interactions between the reactions and diffusion of just two chemical species can produce spatial patterns from a uniform state.
A uniform state stable without diffusion becomes unstable to fluctuations at particular wavelengths when diffusion is added.
This is called diffusion-driven instability (Turing instability).
Since diffusion smooths concentration differences, the conclusion that it can destabilize a system is counterintuitive.
The mechanism lies in a large difference between the two species' diffusion rates.

[`scioxide-pde` example: Two-dimensional Turing pattern formation](../../scioxide-pde/examples/turing-pattern-formation/) computes patterns through nonlinear time evolution, while [Turing instability dispersion relations and discrete modes](../../scioxide-pde/examples/turing-reaction-diffusion/) covers dispersion relations and mode counting on a square.
This page uses several crates to analyze the bifurcation preceding pattern formation.

1. Derive the critical diffusion-coefficient ratio $d_c$ that destabilizes the uniform state, find it with Brent's method in `scioxide-rootfind`, and compare it with the closed form.
2. Find the discrete wavenumbers allowed by a finite-interval grid, integrate in time from a single cosine mode using `StructuredFdmPlan`, `MethodOfLinesPlan`, and `RosenbrockW`, and measure the growth rate from amplitudes extracted by the DCT in `scioxide-fft`.
3. Compute all eigenvalues of the semidiscrete Jacobian using the QR method in `scioxide-linalg` and compare them with discrete-mode eigenvalues predicted by the dispersion relation.

The three computations have different error sources.
The first includes root-finding termination and rounding errors, the second spatial discretization and time integration errors, and the third only eigenvalue-computation rounding errors.
Comparing each with the same dispersion-relation reference lets us distinguish the stages producing each error.

## Model and nondimensionalization

### Schnakenberg reaction system

The Schnakenberg system models two species, activator $U$ and inhibitor $V$, following these elementary reactions.
Precursors supply $U$ and $V$ at constant rates $k_1$ and $k_4$; $U$ degrades at rate $k_2 U$, and the termolecular reaction $2U + V \to 3U$ converts $V$ into $U$ at rate $k_3 U^2 V$.
By the law of mass action, the reaction–diffusion system on the one-dimensional interval $0 \le X \le \ell$ is

$$
\partial_T U = D_U \partial_X^2 U + k_1 - k_2 U + k_3 U^2 V, \qquad
\partial_T V = D_V \partial_X^2 V + k_4 - k_3 U^2 V
$$


Here $T$ is time, $X$ position, and $D_U$ and $D_V$ the two diffusion coefficients.
The $U^2 V$ term increases $U$, so $U$ grows autocatalytically (activation); the same term consumes $V$, so $V$ inhibits the increase of $U$.
We assume spatially uniform reaction-rate constants and that uniform supply is the only external addition of material.

### Nondimensionalization

Measure time in units of the $U$ degradation time $1/k_2$, length in units of the distance $\sqrt{D_U/k_2}$ over which $U$ diffuses during that time, and concentration in units of $\sqrt{k_2/k_3}$.

$$
t = k_2 T, \quad x = X\sqrt{\frac{k_2}{D_U}}, \quad u = U\sqrt{\frac{k_3}{k_2}}, \quad v = V\sqrt{\frac{k_3}{k_2}}
$$

This substitution gives the dimensionless system

$$
\partial_t u = \partial_x^2 u + f(u, v), \qquad \partial_t v = d\, \partial_x^2 v + g(u, v), \qquad
f = a - u + u^2 v, \quad g = b - u^2 v
$$


Three parameters remain: $a = (k_1/k_2)\sqrt{k_3/k_2}$ and $b = (k_4/k_2)\sqrt{k_3/k_2}$ are dimensionless supply rates, and $d = D_V/D_U$ is the diffusion-coefficient ratio.
After nondimensionalization, $D_u = 1$ and $D_v = d$; we retain general $D_u$ and $D_v$ in the derivation and set $D_u = 1$ in numerical calculations.
Murray's textbook (J. D. Murray, *Mathematical Biology II*, 3rd ed., Springer, 2003, Chapter 2) covers the Schnakenberg system and its nondimensionalization.

We impose zero-flux Neumann conditions $\partial_x u = \partial_x v = 0$ at both endpoints.
No material crosses the endpoints; only reactions change the total amount.

Matching the pde example, we use $a = 0.1$, $b = 0.9$, default diffusion ratio $d = 20$, and interval length $L = 20$ (measured in length units $\sqrt{D_U/k_2}$).
These educational values are chosen from a regime exhibiting Turing instability; they are not fitted to measurements of a particular organism or chemical reaction.
All quantities are dimensionless.

## Uniform steady state and linearization

### Uniform steady state

A spatially uniform, time-independent solution satisfies $f(u^*, v^*) = g(u^*, v^*) = 0$.
Adding the two equations gives $a + b - u^* = 0$, hence $u^* = a + b$; then $g = 0$ gives $v^* = b/(u^*)^2 = b/(a + b)^2$.
This is the only positive solution.
For $a = 0.1$ and $b = 0.9$, $u^* = 1$ and $v^* = 0.9$.

### Jacobian

Writing deviations from the uniform steady state as $\mathbf{w} = (u - u^*, v - v^*)^{\mathsf T}$ and expanding $f$ and $g$ to first order gives $\partial_t \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{J}_0 \mathbf{w} + \operatorname{diag}(D_u, D_v)\,\partial_x^2 \mathbf{w}$.
The matrix $\mathbf{J}_0$ is the reaction-term Jacobian; substituting $u^* v^* = b/(a + b)$ into the partial derivatives $f_u = -1 + 2u^* v^*$, $f_v = (u^*)^2$, $g_u = -2u^* v^*$, and $g_v = -(u^*)^2$ gives

$$
\mathbf{J}_0 = \begin{pmatrix} f_u & f_v \\ g_u & g_v \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{b - a}{a + b} & (a + b)^2 \\ -\frac{2b}{a + b} & -(a + b)^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{J}_0) = (a + b)^2 > 0
$$


For $a = 0.1$ and $b = 0.9$, $f_u = 0.8$, $f_v = 1$, $g_u = -1.8$, and $g_v = -1$, with $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) = -0.2$ and $\det(\mathbf{J}_0) = 1$.
The sign $f_u > 0$ means that $u$ promotes its own increase (self-activation), while $g_v < 0$ means that $v$ suppresses its own increase (self-inhibition).
The terms $f_v > 0$ and $g_u < 0$ describe cross-effects: $v$ increases $u$, and $u$ decreases $v$.

### Stability without diffusion

Without diffusion, $\partial_t \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{J}_0 \mathbf{w}$, and solutions are superpositions of $e^{\mu t}$ for eigenvalues $\mu$ of $\mathbf{J}_0$.
The eigenvalues of a 2×2 matrix are roots of the characteristic equation $\mu^2 - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0)\,\mu + \det(\mathbf{J}_0) = 0$; their sum is the trace and their product the determinant.
Both roots have negative real parts if and only if $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) < 0$ and $\det(\mathbf{J}_0) > 0$.
A positive product makes the real parts have the same sign (or coincide for a conjugate pair), and a negative sum fixes that sign as negative.
Since the Schnakenberg system always has $\det(\mathbf{J}_0) > 0$, the condition for uniform-state stability without diffusion reduces to

$$
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) = \frac{b - a}{a + b} - (a + b)^2 < 0
$$


With the defaults, the roots are $-0.1 \pm 0.995i$, and deviations decay while oscillating.

## Deriving the dispersion relation

### Decomposition into cosine modes

The linearized equations have constant coefficients, so cosines $\cos(kx)$ satisfying zero-flux conditions are spatial eigenfunctions.
For $\partial_x \cos(kx) = -k\sin(kx)$ to vanish at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ requires $\sin(kL) = 0$, or equivalently

$$
k = \frac{n\pi}{L}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
$$


Since $\partial_x^2 \cos(kx) = -k^2 \cos(kx)$, substituting $\mathbf{w} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}(t)\cos(kx)$ shows that the amplitude $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfies the two-component ODE

$$
\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{w}}}{dt} = \mathbf{J}_k \hat{\mathbf{w}}, \qquad \mathbf{J}_k = \mathbf{J}_0 - k^2 \operatorname{diag}(D_u, D_v)
$$


Modes with different $n$ do not couple, so a general perturbation evolves as a sum of independently evolving mode amplitudes.
Setting $\hat{\mathbf{w}}(t) = \mathbf{w}_0 e^{\sigma t}$ makes the growth rates $\sigma$ the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}_k$.
The relationship between $k$ and $\sigma$ is called the dispersion relation; we denote the eigenvalue with larger real part by $\sigma(k)$.

### Trace and determinant

The trace and determinant of $\mathbf{J}_k$ are

$$
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_k) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) - (D_u + D_v)k^2, \qquad
h(k^2) \equiv \det(\mathbf{J}_k) = D_u D_v k^4 - (D_v f_u + D_u g_v) k^2 + \det(\mathbf{J}_0)
$$


The eigenvalues are

$$
\sigma_\pm(k) = \frac{1}{2}\left( \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_k) \pm \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_k)^2 - 4h(k^2)} \right)
$$

and form a complex conjugate pair when the radicand is negative.
Since $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) < 0$, the trace is negative for every $k$.
Applying the preceding stability criterion to $\mathbf{J}_k$ shows that a mode is unstable (has an eigenvalue with positive real part) exactly when $h(k^2) < 0$.
The two eigenvalues are then real, one positive and one negative because their product is negative.
Diffusion-driven instability therefore appears as a real eigenvalue giving monotone growth without oscillation.

## Turing conditions and critical diffusion ratio

### Four conditions

The determinant $h$ is quadratic in $k^2$, with $h = \det(\mathbf{J}_0) > 0$ at $k^2 = 0$.
For $h$ to become negative at positive $k^2$, its minimum must first lie on the positive side, requiring $D_v f_u + D_u g_v > 0$.
The minimum of $h$ occurs at $k_m^2 = (D_v f_u + D_u g_v)/(2 D_u D_v)$ and equals $\det(\mathbf{J}_0) - (D_v f_u + D_u g_v)^2/(4 D_u D_v)$.
Combining the condition for this value to be negative with stability without diffusion gives the following four Turing-instability conditions.

$$
f_u + g_v < 0, \qquad f_u g_v - f_v g_u > 0, \qquad d f_u + g_v > 0, \qquad (d f_u + g_v)^2 > 4 d \det(\mathbf{J}_0)
$$

Here $d = D_v/D_u$.
Comparing the first and third requires $d \ne 1$; for a system with $f_u > 0 > g_v$, this gives $d > -g_v/f_u > 1$.
The inhibitor must diffuse faster than the activator.
For the defaults, $-g_v/f_u = 1.25$, but this is only a necessary condition.
Intuitively, a local increase in activator produces a rapidly spreading inhibitor that suppresses activator growth nearby, arranging increased and suppressed regions at regular intervals.

### Deriving the critical diffusion ratio

Under $d f_u + g_v > 0$, the third and fourth conditions are equivalent to $d f_u + g_v > 2\sqrt{d \det(\mathbf{J}_0)}$.
Setting $s = \sqrt{d} > 0$ gives the quadratic inequality in $s$

$$
f_u s^2 - 2\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}_0)}\, s + g_v > 0
$$


The two roots of the left-hand side are $s_\pm = \bigl(\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}_0)} \pm \sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}_0) - f_u g_v}\bigr)/f_u$.
Since $f_u > 0$ and $g_v < 0$, we have $\det(\mathbf{J}_0) - f_u g_v > \det(\mathbf{J}_0)$, hence $s_- < 0 < s_+$.
The inequality is equivalent to $s > s_+$; if $s > s_+$, then $f_u s^2 + g_v > 2\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}_0)}\, s > 0$, so the third condition is automatically satisfied as well.
The critical diffusion ratio $d_c = s_+^2$ and critical wavenumber $k_c$ that first becomes unstable there are

$$
d_c = \left( \frac{\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}_0)} + \sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}_0) - f_u g_v}}{f_u} \right)^2, \qquad k_c^2 = \frac{1}{D_u}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\mathbf{J}_0)}{d_c}}
$$


Here $k_c^2$ is the minimizer $k_m^2$ of $h$ at $d = d_c$, obtained by substituting the zero-minimum condition $(D_v f_u + D_u g_v)^2 = 4D_u D_v \det(\mathbf{J}_0)$ for $h$ into the expression for $k_m^2$.
With the defaults, $d_c = ((1 + \sqrt{1.8})/0.8)^2 \approx 8.5676$ and $k_c^2 \approx 0.3416$.

### Unstable band

For $d > d_c$, $h(k^2) = 0$ has two positive roots $k_1^2 < k_2^2$, and modes with wavenumbers $k_1 < k < k_2$ between them grow.
This range is called the unstable band.
The roots are $k_{1,2}^2 = \bigl(\beta \mp \sqrt{\beta^2 - 4d\det(\mathbf{J}_0)}\bigr)/(2d)$ ($D_u = 1$, $\beta = d f_u + g_v$).
At $d = 20$, $\beta = 15$, giving the unstable band $k^2 \in (0.0740,\ 0.6760)$.
The wavenumber $k_{\max}$ maximizing $\sigma$ within the unstable band coincides with neither the minimizer of $h$ nor $k_c$.
This is because $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_k)$ also varies with $k$.
[Critical diffusion ratio and critical wavenumber](oxref:pde-turing-critical-ratio) explains the distinction between $k_{\max}$ and $k_c$.

## Finding the critical diffusion ratio

### Condition function and bracket construction

Even with a closed form, numerically finding the zero of the condition function

$$
\varphi(d) = d f_u + g_v - 2\sqrt{d \det(\mathbf{J}_0)}
$$

provides a procedure that remains applicable when changing the reaction terms removes the available formula.
We have $\varphi(0) = g_v < 0$, and if $f_u > 0$, then $\varphi \sim d f_u \to +\infty$ at $d \to \infty$.
Also, $\varphi$ is quadratic in $\sqrt{d}$, so $(0, \infty)$ has only the zero $d_c$.
Doubling the upper bound as $1, 2, 4, \ldots$ until $\varphi > 0$ therefore produces a sign-changing bracket $[0, d_{\mathrm{up}}]$ in finitely many evaluations.
With the defaults, $\varphi(8) < 0 < \varphi(16)$, so five evaluations establish the bracket $[0, 16]$.
These search evaluations are not included in the root-finding report's count.

### Brent's method

The method `Brent` is Brent–Dekker.
Each iteration proposes inverse quadratic interpolation through the latest three points (a secant step if only two are distinct), accepting it only if it lies within the interval and its step is smaller than half the step two iterations earlier; otherwise, it uses bisection.
Each iteration evaluates $f$ once.
Near a simple root, inverse quadratic interpolation gives superlinear convergence (order about 1.84), while fallback to bisection guarantees convergence for a continuous function.
Maintaining a sign-changing bracket throughout prevents loss of the root.
[Brent–Dekker interpolation and safeguards](oxref:rootfind-brent) gives algorithmic details.

### Stopping conditions and error

The default stopping condition for `ScalarRootOptions` tests the interval width, $b - a \le \tau_a + \tau_r \max(\lvert a \rvert, \lvert b \rvert)$, with $\tau_a$ and $\tau_r$ both defaulting to $4\varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon$ is machine epsilon).
The cell uses `with_step_tolerance(StepTolerance::new(0.0, 1e-14))` to set $\tau_a = 0$ and $\tau_r = 10^{-14}$, and `with_residual_tolerance(ResidualTolerance::new(1e-13, 0.0))` to add the residual test $\lvert \varphi \rvert \le 10^{-13}$.
Passing the residual test gives termination reason `ResidualTolerance`; passing the interval-width test gives `BracketTolerance`.

The derivative $\varphi'(d_c) = f_u - \sqrt{\det(\mathbf{J}_0)/d_c}$ at the zero converts residuals into root-error estimates.
With the defaults, $\varphi'(d_c) \approx 0.458$, so $\lvert \varphi \rvert \le 10^{-13}$ corresponds to error about $\lvert d - d_c \rvert \lesssim 2.2 \times 10^{-13}$, or relative error about $2.6 \times 10^{-14}$.
Rounding error in evaluating $\varphi$ itself is of order $\varepsilon(\lvert d f_u \rvert + \lvert g_v \rvert + 2\sqrt{d\det(\mathbf{J}_0)}) \approx 3 \times 10^{-15}$, corresponding to root relative error of order $10^{-15}$.
Since $\varphi'(d_c)$ is bounded away from zero, this is a well-conditioned simple root.

### Cell inputs and outputs

The following cell takes supply rates $a$ and $b$ and a diffusion ratio $d$ for plotting.
It computes the entries of $\mathbf{J}_0$ from $a$ and $b$, checks $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) < 0$, expands the bracket, obtains $d_c$ with `Brent`, and compares it with the closed form.
The function `ScalarFn::new` turns a closure returning a value wrapped in `Ok` into a root-finding function; `Interval::new(0.0, upper)` constructs a closed interval, returning `NonFinite` for NaN or infinite endpoints and `InvalidInput` when the lower endpoint exceeds the upper.
The function `Brent.solve` returns `InvalidInput` if the endpoint values have the same sign.
From the `RootResult` returned by `Brent.solve`, `is_success()` indicates success, `point()` gives the root, `termination()` the termination reason, and `counts().provider(EvaluationRole::Function)` the function evaluation count.
The table columns are Brent's $d_c$, closed-form $d_c$, their relative difference, $k_c^2$, the function evaluation count, and the termination reason.

The plot shows the maximum growth rate $\max \operatorname{Re}\sigma(k)$ ($D_u = 1$) at each $k^2$, overlaying a solid curve for the selected $d$ and a dashed curve for $d = d_c$, with reference lines at $\sigma = 0$ and $k_c^2$.
Growth rates use the closed-form eigenvalues $\sigma_+ = (\operatorname{tr} + \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}^2 - 4h})/2$ of $\mathbf{J}_k$ (real part $\operatorname{tr}/2$ if the radicand is negative).
With negative trace and $\sigma_+$ near zero, addition in this expression suffers cancellation, giving absolute error of order $\varepsilon\lvert\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_k)\rvert$.
This is accurate enough to read the plot; the definitions block below shows a form for cases requiring relative accuracy.
For inputs whose uniform state is unstable without diffusion ($\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) \ge 0$), the cell displays that the Turing-instability prerequisite is unmet and ends with outcome `failed`.

```rust
//| id: app-turing-critical-ratio
//| title: "Brent root finding for the critical diffusion ratio and the dispersion relation"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-rootfind]
//| inputs:
//|   supply_a: {"type":"range","label":"Supply rate a [-]","description":"Precursor supply rate for activator u.","min":0.02,"max":0.3,"step":0.02,"value":0.1}
//|   supply_b: {"type":"range","label":"Supply rate b [-]","description":"Precursor supply rate for inhibitor v.","min":0.5,"max":1.5,"step":0.1,"value":0.9}
//|   diff_ratio: {"type":"range","label":"Diffusion ratio d = Dv/Du [-]","description":"Diffusion ratio used to plot the dispersion relation.","min":2,"max":40,"step":1,"value":20}
use scioxide_core::{EvaluationRole, Interval, RealScalar, ResidualTolerance, StepTolerance};
use scioxide_rootfind::{Brent, ScalarFn, ScalarRootOptions, ScalarRootSolver};

// Entries of Jacobian J₀ at the uniform equilibrium (u*, v*) = (a+b, b/(a+b)²).
let s = supply_a + supply_b;
let (fu, fv, gu, gv) = (
    (supply_b - supply_a) / s,
    s * s,
    -2.0 * supply_b / s,
    -s * s,
);
let det = fu * gv - fv * gu;
// φ(d) = d f_u + g_v − 2√(d det J₀). The uniform state becomes unstable for d greater than d_c.
let phi = |d: f64| d * fu + gv - 2.0 * RealScalar::sqrt(d * det);
// With D_u = 1, the largest real part among eigenvalues of J_k = J₀ − k² diag(1, d) (closed form for plotting).
let growth = |d: f64, k2: f64| {
    let (tr, dk) = (
        fu + gv - (1.0 + d) * k2,
        (fu - k2) * (gv - d * k2) - fv * gu,
    );
    let disc = tr * tr - 4.0 * dk;
    if disc >= 0.0 {
        0.5 * (tr + RealScalar::sqrt(disc))
    } else {
        0.5 * tr
    }
};
if fu + gv >= 0.0 {
    emit_text!(&format!(
        "tr J₀ = {:.4} ≥ 0: the uniform state is unstable even without diffusion, so the Turing-instability prerequisite is unmet.",
        fu + gv
    ));
    emit_outcome!(Outcome::failed(
        "The prerequisite of equilibrium stability without diffusion is unmet"
    ));
} else {
    // φ(0) = g_v < 0. Since f_u > 0, doubling the upper bound makes φ > 0 in finitely many evaluations.
    let mut upper = 1.0_f64;
    while phi(upper) <= 0.0 {
        upper *= 2.0;
    }
    let options = ScalarRootOptions::default()
        .with_residual_tolerance(ResidualTolerance::new(1e-13, 0.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?)
        .with_step_tolerance(StepTolerance::new(0.0, 1e-14).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?);
    let bracket = Interval::new(0.0, upper).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let result = Brent
        .solve(&ScalarFn::new(|d: f64| Ok(phi(d))), bracket, &options)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    if !result.is_success() {
        return Err(format!(
            "Brent's method was not accepted: {}",
            result.termination()
        ));
    }
    let d_root = result.point();
    // Independent check: the positive root of the quadratic in √d, f_u s² − 2√det s + g_v = 0.
    let s_c = (RealScalar::sqrt(det) + RealScalar::sqrt(det - fu * gv)) / fu;
    let d_closed = s_c * s_c;
    let sig12 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 12 });
    let sig6 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 6 });
    let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
    emit_table_with_columns!(
        serde_json::json!([
            { "key": "0", "label": "d_c (Brent) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig12 },
            { "key": "1", "label": "d_c (closed form) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig12 },
            { "key": "2", "label": "Relative difference [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
            { "key": "3", "label": "k_c² [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
            { "key": "4", "label": "Function evaluations [count]", "type": "number" },
            { "key": "5", "label": "Termination reason", "type": "string" }
        ]),
        vec![(
            d_root,
            d_closed,
            (d_root - d_closed).abs() / d_closed,
            RealScalar::sqrt(det / d_closed),
            result.counts().provider(EvaluationRole::Function),
            result.termination().to_string()
        )]
    );
    let curve = |d: f64| -> Vec<[f64; 2]> {
        (0..=200)
            .map(|i| {
                let k2 = 1.5 * i as f64 / 200.0;
                [k2, growth(d, k2)]
            })
            .collect()
    };
    let unstable = curve(diff_ratio)
        .iter()
        .filter(|point| point[1] > 0.0)
        .count();
    let series = vec![
        serde_json::json!({ "name": format!("d = {diff_ratio}"), "points": curve(diff_ratio) }),
        serde_json::json!({
            "name": "d = d_c",
            "points": curve(d_root),
            "style": { "lineType": "dashed" }
        }),
    ];
    let k_line = RealScalar::sqrt(det / d_root);
    emit_line_chart!(
        &series,
        options = &serde_json::json!({
            "xLabel": "Squared wavenumber k² [-]",
            "yLabel": "Maximum growth rate max Re σ [-]",
            "yMin": -1.0,
            "yMax": 0.6,
            "annotations": [
                { "kind": "line", "axis": "y", "value": 0.0, "label": "σ = 0" },
                {
                    "kind": "line",
                    "axis": "x",
                    "value": k_line,
                    "label": "k_c²",
                    "style": { "lineType": "dotted" }
                }
            ]
        })
    );
    emit_text!(&format!(
        "Grid points in k² with σ > 0 at d = {diff_ratio}: {unstable} of 201"
    ));
    emit_outcome!(
        Outcome::satisfied("Critical diffusion ratio root finding completed")
            .status(result.termination().to_string())
    );
}
```

With the defaults, Brent's method passes the residual test after around 10 function evaluations, and $d_c = 8.567627\ldots$ agrees with the closed form to relative difference of order $2 \times 10^{-16}$.
This difference is smaller than the preceding error estimate, showing that the two computations agree to within rounding error.
The dashed curve ($d = d_c$) touches $\sigma = 0$ at $k_c^2 \approx 0.342$ and is negative at all other $k^2$.
The solid curve for $d = 20$ is positive in the band $k^2 \approx 0.074$–$0.676$, with 81 of the 201 grid points positive.
Only modes with wavenumbers in this band grow.

Changing inputs shifts the bifurcation.
Reducing $d$ below $d_c$ makes the solid curve negative everywhere, with zero positive grid points.
Increasing $a$ to $0.3$ lowers $f_u = 0.5$ and weakens self-activation, increasing $d_c \approx 28.5$, so the uniform state remains stable even at $d = 20$.
Increasing $b$ to $1.5$ ($a = 0.1$) gives $d_c \approx 18.8$, so $d = 20$ is slightly above critical and a narrow unstable band appears (29 positive grid points).
For $a = 0.02$ ($b = 0.9$) or $b = 0.5$ ($a = 0.1$), $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) \ge 0$, and a prerequisite-failure message replaces the table.
For these two inputs, the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}_0$ form a complex conjugate pair with positive real parts, making the uniform state oscillatory unstable even without diffusion (beyond a Hopf bifurcation).
This is distinct from diffusion-driven instability.

## Discrete wavenumbers on a finite interval

### Rectangular grid and cosine modes

We now fix $a = 0.1$ and $b = 0.9$ and examine how a finite interval and grid change the growth rates.
The constructor `StructuredFdmPlan` uses conservative vertex-centered finite differences to discretize the two-dimensional rectangle $[0, 20] \times [0, 1]$ with spacing $h = 1$.
The long side has 20 subdivisions (21 columns), and the short side one subdivision (two rows), giving 42 vertices, two components $u$ and $v$, and 84 unknowns.
There are only two modes along the short side: one constant in $y$ and one with opposite signs in the two rows.
The latter's squared discrete wavenumber $4$ lies above the unstable band $k^2 < 0.676$, so only modes independent of $y$ grow, making the system effectively a one-dimensional problem on $[0, 20]$.
The eigenvalue computation below verifies this.

On the continuous interval $[0, L]$, zero-flux modes permit only wavenumbers $k = n\pi/L$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$).
Even with an open unstable band, the uniform state remains stable if none of the $k$ fall inside it.
At $L = 20$, adjacent $k^2$ become farther apart with increasing $n$, and four modes $n = 2, 3, 4, 5$ fall within the unstable band $(0.0740,\ 0.6760)$ for $d = 20$.

### Grid eigenvalues

Let $A$ be the conservative finite-difference operator and $M$ the lumped mass (a diagonal matrix of dual control-volume areas at vertices).
For a uniform grid of spacing $h$, an interior vertex has $(M^{-1}Au)_j = -(u_{j-1} - 2u_j + u_{j+1})/h^2$.
Boundary vertex masses are half the interior masses, so boundary rows coincide with central differences using reflected ghost values $u_{-1} = u_1$.
The vertex cosine $\cos(kx_j)$ is symmetric about $x = 0$ and $x = L$, satisfying this boundary relation and forming an eigenvector of $M^{-1}A$.
The eigenvalue for diffusion coefficient 1 is

$$
k_h^2 = \frac{4}{h^2}\sin^2\frac{kh}{2} = k^2 - \frac{h^2 k^4}{12} + O(h^4 k^6)
$$


The leading error term $-h^2k^4/12$ shows that $k_h^2$ approximates $k^2$ to second order; since $\sin x < x$ at $x > 0$, it is smaller than $k^2$ for $k > 0$.
The “Discrete Neumann modes” section of [Turing instability dispersion relations and discrete modes](../../scioxide-pde/examples/turing-reaction-diffusion/) covers the same property on a two-dimensional square and checks eigenvectors using the Plan operator.

The linearized semidiscrete growth rate is $\lambda_h = \sigma(k_h)$, replacing $k^2$ in the dispersion relation with $k_h^2$.
The mode values for $d = 20$ are as follows ($n = 0, 1$ has complex conjugate eigenvalues, and the table shows their real parts).

| Mode number $n$ | $k^2$ | $k_h^2$ | Continuous-system $\sigma(k)$ | Spatially discrete $\lambda_h$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | $-0.1$ | $-0.1$ |
| 1 | 0.0247 | 0.0246 | $-0.3591$ | $-0.3585$ |
| 2 | 0.0987 | 0.0979 | 0.1194 | 0.1166 |
| 3 | 0.2221 | 0.2180 | 0.2624 | 0.2618 |
| 4 | 0.3948 | 0.3820 | 0.2075 | 0.2147 |
| 5 | 0.6169 | 0.5858 | 0.0487 | 0.0735 |
| 6 | 0.8883 | 0.8244 | $-0.1851$ | $-0.1281$ |
| 7 | 1.2090 | 1.0920 | $-0.4819$ | $-0.3721$ |

The relative deviation in $k_h^2$ is 1.8% at $n = 3$ and 9.7% at $n = 7$, increasing with $n$.
To the right of the fastest-growing wavenumber, $\sigma$ slopes downward, so discrete modes $k_h < k$ have larger growth rates than the continuous system.
The fastest-growing discrete mode is $n = 3$ (wavelength $2L/n \approx 13.3$).

### Shared definitions

The following definitions block shares the reaction-term Jacobian, diffusion coefficients, rectangular finite-difference Plan, linearized right-hand side, discrete wavenumbers, and eigenvalues of $2 \times 2$ matrices between the next two cells on this page.
The implementation `StructuredFdmCoefficients::diffusion` returns diffusion coefficients by axis and component: component 0 is $u$ and component 1 is $v$.
The condition `BoundaryCondition::natural` specifies the outward conormal flux $\mathbf{n} \cdot D\nabla u$; combined with `BoundaryData::zero()` it gives zero flux, applying to both components because none is singled out.
The constructor `StructuredBoundaries::new(&mesh, 2, conditions)` creates a two-component boundary, and `StructuredFdmPlan::new` takes ownership of the grid to construct the Plan.
Unknowns are grouped by component (42 vertices for $u$ followed by 42 for $v$); within each component, the long-side axis varies fastest.

The implementation `linearized_rhs` computes the physical right-hand side $M\mathbf{J}_0\mathbf{y} - A\mathbf{y}$.
The method `apply_into` writes $A\mathbf{y}$ into `out` using caller-supplied workspace `scratch`.
Subtracting this from the reaction term multiplied by lumped mass $M$ expresses diffusion and reaction with the same dual control-volume weights.

The function `mode_eigenvalues` returns the two eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}_0 - \kappa\operatorname{diag}(1, d)$ as $[\operatorname{Re}, \operatorname{Im}]$ in descending real-part order.
Unlike the first cell, it computes the larger real eigenvalue as $\sigma_+ = h/\sigma_-$ from the product relation $\sigma_+ \sigma_- = h$.
Because the trace is negative, $\sigma_- = (\operatorname{tr} - \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}^2 - 4h})/2$ is a sum of terms with the same sign and avoids cancellation.
This form keeps relative errors of the order of rounding even for modes with $\sigma_+$ near zero.

```rust
//| id: app-turing-strip-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Rectangular finite-difference Plan, linearized right-hand side, discrete wavenumbers, and 2×2 eigenvalues"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-pde, scioxide-spatial]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, LinearOperator, RealScalar};
use scioxide_pde::{
    BoundaryCondition, BoundaryData, StructuredBoundaries, StructuredFdmCoefficients,
    StructuredFdmPlan,
};
use scioxide_spatial::{AxisSide, AxisTopology, BoundarySet, CoordinateAxis, Grid2d, QuadMesh};

/// Jacobian J₀ = [[f_u, f_v], [g_u, g_v]] at uniform steady state (1, 0.9) (a = 0.1, b = 0.9).
pub(crate) const JACOBIAN: [[f64; 2]; 2] = [[0.8, 1.0], [-1.8, -1.0]];

/// Subdivisions along the long side [0, 20]. Vertices are x = 0, 1, …, 20, with cosine mode numbers n = 0, …, 20.
pub(crate) const CELLS: usize = 20;

/// Diffusion coefficients D_u = 1 and D_v = d. Component 0 is u and component 1 is v, with identical values along both axes.
pub(crate) struct Diffusion(pub(crate) f64);
impl StructuredFdmCoefficients<f64, f64, 2> for Diffusion {
    fn diffusion(&self, _: usize, component: usize, _: [f64; 2]) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
        Ok([1.0, self.0][component])
    }
}

/// Discretize [0, 20] × [0, 1] with a vertex grid of spacing 1 (21 × 2 vertices), imposing zero flux on all four sides.
pub(crate) fn strip_plan(ratio: f64) -> Result<StructuredFdmPlan<f64, f64, 2>, SciOxideError> {
    let long = CoordinateAxis::uniform(0.0, CELLS as f64, CELLS + 1)?;
    let short = CoordinateAxis::uniform(0.0, 1.0, 2)?;
    let mesh = QuadMesh::new(Grid2d::new([long, short], [AxisTopology::Bounded; 2])?)?;
    let mut conditions = Vec::new();
    for axis in 0..2 {
        for side in [AxisSide::Start, AxisSide::End] {
            let region = BoundarySet::axis_side(&mesh, axis, side)?;
            conditions.push(BoundaryCondition::natural(region, BoundaryData::zero()));
        }
    }
    let boundaries = StructuredBoundaries::new(&mesh, 2, conditions)?;
    StructuredFdmPlan::new(mesh, &Diffusion(ratio), &boundaries)
}

/// Physical right-hand side of the linearized semidiscrete system: out = M J₀ y − A y.
///
/// y and out contain all u vertices followed by all v vertices; `mass` is the lumped mass in the same order.
pub(crate) fn linearized_rhs(
    plan: &StructuredFdmPlan<f64, f64, 2>,
    mass: &[f64],
    y: &[f64],
    out: &mut [f64],
    scratch: &mut [f64],
) -> Result<(), SciOxideError> {
    plan.apply_into(y, out, scratch)?;
    let nodes = mass.len() / 2;
    let [[fu, fv], [gu, gv]] = JACOBIAN;
    for i in 0..nodes {
        let (u, v) = (y[i], y[nodes + i]);
        out[i] = mass[i] * (fu * u + fv * v) - out[i];
        out[nodes + i] = mass[nodes + i] * (gu * u + gv * v) - out[nodes + i];
    }
    Ok(())
}

/// Squared discrete wavenumber 4 sin²(mπ/2L) for cosine mode m on a side of length L with grid spacing 1.
pub(crate) fn discrete_wavenumber2(mode: usize, length: f64) -> f64 {
    let s = RealScalar::sin(mode as f64 * core::f64::consts::PI / (2.0 * length));
    4.0 * s * s
}

/// Return both eigenvalues [Re, Im] of J₀ − κ diag(1, d) in descending real-part order. κ is k² or k_h².
pub(crate) fn mode_eigenvalues(kappa: f64, ratio: f64) -> [[f64; 2]; 2] {
    let [[fu, fv], [gu, gv]] = JACOBIAN;
    let trace = fu + gv - (1.0 + ratio) * kappa;
    let det = (fu - kappa) * (gv - ratio * kappa) - fv * gu;
    let disc = trace * trace - 4.0 * det;
    if disc >= 0.0 {
        // Since tr < 0, the smaller root avoids cancellation. Obtain the larger root from the product det.
        let small = 0.5 * (trace - RealScalar::sqrt(disc));
        [[det / small, 0.0], [small, 0.0]]
    } else {
        let im = 0.5 * RealScalar::sqrt(-disc);
        [[0.5 * trace, im], [0.5 * trace, -im]]
    }
}
```

## Measuring growth rates by time integration

### Semidiscrete system and the method of lines

Discretizing only space gives the ODE with unknowns $\mathbf{y}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{84}$

$$
M\frac{d\mathbf{y}}{dt} = M(\mathbf{J}_0 \otimes I)\mathbf{y} - A\mathbf{y}
$$


Here $\mathbf{J}_0 \otimes I$ applies $\mathbf{J}_0$ componentwise at each vertex.
This is called the method of lines.
The Plan `MethodOfLinesPlan::new(84, Some(mass), None)` normalizes a first-order system with positive diagonal mass $M$ to $\mathbf{y}' = \mathbf{J}_0\mathbf{y} - M^{-1}A\mathbf{y}$ by dividing both sides by $M$.
The third argument specifies state scaling; `None` means no scaling.
The method `workspace()` creates workspace, and `bind(&model, &mut binding)` borrows the physical right-hand side `model` and workspace to create the `OdeSystem` passed to the integrator.
This normalization is necessary because `RosenbrockW` accepts only identity-mass systems and does not handle mass matrices directly.

The cell integrates the linearized system without nonlinear terms.
Amplitude magnitude therefore does not affect the result, and measured growth always remains in the linear regime.
For the same measurement in the nonlinear system, the ratio of quadratic to linear terms in the expansion of $u^2 v$ is approximately proportional to amplitude, so use only a time interval over which the amplitude is sufficiently small.
The initial amplitude $10^{-5}$ is chosen with nonlinear-system use in mind; for $d = 20$ and $n = 3$, it grows about 14-fold to $1.4 \times 10^{-4}$ by $t = 10$.

### Initial values and mode extraction

The initial state is the long-side cosine $\cos(kx_j)$ ($k = n\pi/20$) multiplied by the eigenvector for the larger eigenvalue $\lambda_h$ of $\mathbf{J}_0 - k_h^2\operatorname{diag}(1, d)$.
The eigenvector's first-row equation $(f_u - k_h^2 - \lambda_h)w_u + f_v w_v = 0$ gives $w_v/w_u = (\lambda_h - f_u + k_h^2)/f_v$, approximately $-0.32$ for $d = 20$ and $n = 3$.
This initial state is a semidiscrete eigenvector, so the exact solution is proportional to $e^{\lambda_h t}$; other modes appear only through time-integration errors.
A general initial state requires waiting for the component of the smaller eigenvalue $\lambda_- \approx -5.04$ at the same $n$ to decay as $e^{-(\lambda_h - \lambda_-)t}$, on a timescale of approximately $1/(\lambda_h - \lambda_-) \approx 0.19$.

At each output time, apply `dct(..., DctType::I, Normalization::Unscaled)` to the 21 values of $u$ at $y = 0$.
The unnormalized DCT-I is $c_m = x_0 + (-1)^m x_{20} + 2\sum_{j=1}^{19} x_j \cos(\pi jm/20)$; discrete orthogonality of the vertex cosines $\cos(\pi nj/20)$ makes $c_m$ extract only the amplitude of mode $m$.
For $1 \le n \le 19$, a mode of amplitude $\alpha$ gives $c_n = 20\alpha$, but this constant does not affect the growth rate because the latter is a logarithmic slope.

### Fitting the growth rate

Compute $\ell_i = \ln\lvert c_n(t_i) \rvert$ at 21 output times $t_i$ and fit the line $\ell = \ell_0 + \hat\sigma t$ by least squares.
The estimated slope is

$$
\hat\sigma = \frac{\sum_i (t_i - \bar t)(\ell_i - \bar\ell)}{\sum_i (t_i - \bar t)^2}
$$

where $\bar t$ and $\bar\ell$ are means.
The maximum fit residual $\max_i \lvert \ell_i - \bar\ell - \hat\sigma(t_i - \bar t) \rvert$ measures deviation of the amplitude from a single exponential.
It increases if the initial state is not an eigenvector or other modes are mixed in.
The input range excludes $n \le 2$.
The eigenvalues for $n = 0, 1$ are complex even at the default $d$, and the amplitude $\lvert c_n \rvert$ decays with oscillation, so a straight-line fit cannot measure the growth rate ($n = 2$ is also complex at $d \le 10$).

### Stiffness and Rosenbrock-W

The semidiscrete eigenvalues have their largest magnitudes for the shortest-wavelength mode.
At $d = 20$, this magnitude (the spectral radius) is about 161, about 600 times the growth rate 0.26.
The explicit classical four-stage Runge–Kutta method (RK4) has a real-axis stability interval of about 2.79, restricting the step to $\Delta t \lesssim 2.79/161 \approx 0.017$ and requiring at least about 580 steps to reach $t = 10$.
Halving the grid spacing in both directions increases the spectral radius approximately fourfold and makes this restriction four times stricter.
This property, in which explicit-method stability imposes stricter limits than accuracy requirements, is called stiffness.

The method `RosenbrockW::new(RosenbrockMethod::Ros34Pw2)` is Rang and Angermann's ROS34PW2, a four-stage third-order Rosenbrock-W method with an embedded second-order error estimate.
Each stage solves one linear system with matrix $I - h\gamma\mathbf{J}$ ($h$ is the time step, $\gamma \approx 0.436$ the method's diagonal coefficient, and $\mathbf{J}$ an approximate Jacobian), without Newton iteration.
With an exact Jacobian it is L-stable, so accuracy rather than stability determines the step size.
W methods retain their order with approximate Jacobians; since this cell supplies no analytical Jacobian to `OdeFn`, it uses forward differences, last in the default preference order of analytical, automatic differentiation, then forward differences.
For 84 unknowns, one difference Jacobian requires 84 right-hand-side evaluations, accounting for most of those evaluations.

The error tolerances are `ErrorTolerance::scalar(1e-12, 1e-7, NormSelection::Infinity)`, with absolute tolerance $10^{-12}$, relative tolerance $10^{-7}$, and the maximum-component norm.
The error weight for component $i$ is $10^{-12} + 10^{-7}\lvert y_i \rvert$; at vertices of amplitude order $10^{-5}$, the terms are comparable, while near cosine nodes the absolute tolerance determines the weight.
Controlling local error at this level is expected to give relative global amplitude error of order $10^{-7}$ at $t = 10$, implying growth-rate error in $\ln(c(T)/c(0))/T$ of order $10^{-7}/T \approx 10^{-8}$.
Values at output times specified by `OdeOutputSchedule::times` come from each step's cubic Hermite interpolation (dense output).

### Cell inputs and outputs

The following cell takes diffusion ratio $d$ (8–24) and cosine mode number $n$ (3–7), outputting 21 times at intervals of 0.5 from $t = 0$ to $10$.
The constructor `OdeState::from_slice(0.0, &initial)` creates the initial state, and `method.workspace(&problem, &options)` creates the integrator workspace.
The function `advance` advances state `state` in place to $t = 10$, writes outputs to `OdeTrajectory`, and returns `OdeSolveReport`.
The methods `OdeTrajectory::times()` and `states()` return output times and states in matching order.
The method `report.accepted_steps()` gives accepted steps, `report.counts().provider(EvaluationRole::RightHandSide)` and `provider(EvaluationRole::Jacobian)` give right-hand-side and Jacobian evaluation counts, and `termination()` gives the termination reason.
We use `RefCell` to borrow the $A\mathbf{y}$ workspace inside the right-hand-side closure.
This is necessary because the right-hand side of `OdeFn` is an `Fn` closure and cannot directly capture mutable workspace.

The plot shows DCT amplitude $\lvert c_n(t) \rvert$ on a logarithmic axis; linear growth gives a straight line.
The table columns are continuous-system growth rate $\sigma(k)$, spatially discrete growth rate $\lambda_h$, fitted growth rate $\hat\sigma$, $\lvert \hat\sigma - \lambda_h \rvert$, maximum fit residual, accepted steps, and right-hand-side and Jacobian evaluation counts.
The outcome is `satisfied` if integration reaches the endpoint, or `partial` if it stops early, with the termination reason in `status`.

```rust
use scioxide_core::{
    ErrorTolerance, EvaluationRole, ExecutionContext, LinearOperator, NormSelection, RealScalar,
};
use scioxide_fft::{DctType, Normalization, dct};
use scioxide_ode::{
    OdeFn, OdeMethod, OdeOptions, OdeOutputSchedule, OdeProblem, OdeState, OdeTrajectory,
    RosenbrockMethod, RosenbrockW,
};
use scioxide_pde::{MethodOfLinesPlan, SpatialDiscretization};
use std::cell::RefCell;

let plan = strip_plan(ratio).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let nodes = plan.unknowns() / 2;
let mass = plan.lumped_mass();
let scratch = RefCell::new(vec![
    0.0;
    plan.apply_workspace_len().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?
]);
let model = OdeFn::new(2 * nodes, |_: f64, y: &[f64], out: &mut [f64]| {
    linearized_rhs(&plan, mass, y, out, &mut scratch.borrow_mut())
})
.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
// Divide both sides by lumped mass to give y' = J₀ y − M⁻¹A y.
let mol = MethodOfLinesPlan::new(2 * nodes, Some(mass), None).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut binding = mol.workspace().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let system = mol.bind(&model, &mut binding).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
// Initial values are cos(kx) multiplied by the eigenvector of the larger eigenvalue λ_h of J₀ − k_h² diag(1, d).
let n = mode as usize;
let k = n as f64 * core::f64::consts::PI / CELLS as f64;
let kh2 = discrete_wavenumber2(n, CELLS as f64);
let lambda = mode_eigenvalues(kh2, ratio)[0][0];
let v_over_u = (lambda - JACOBIAN[0][0] + kh2) / JACOBIAN[0][1];
let initial: Vec<_> = (0..2 * nodes)
    .map(|j| {
        let factor = if j < nodes { 1.0 } else { v_over_u };
        factor * 1e-5 * RealScalar::cos(k * (j % nodes % (CELLS + 1)) as f64)
    })
    .collect();
let mut state = OdeState::from_slice(0.0, &initial).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let method = RosenbrockW::<f64>::new(RosenbrockMethod::Ros34Pw2).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let options = OdeOptions::new()
    .with_error_tolerance(
        ErrorTolerance::scalar(1e-12, 1e-7, NormSelection::Infinity)
            .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,
    )
    .with_output_schedule(OdeOutputSchedule::times(
        (0..=20).map(|i| i as f64 / 2.0).collect(),
    ));
let problem = OdeProblem::new(&system, 10.0);
let mut ws = method
    .workspace(&problem, &options)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut path = OdeTrajectory::new();
let report = method
    .advance(
        &problem,
        &options,
        &mut state,
        &mut path,
        &mut ws,
        ExecutionContext::sequential(),
    )
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
// At each output time, apply DCT-I to the y = 0 column of u and extract the coefficient c_n(t) of mode n.
let mut points = Vec::new();

        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    points.push([t, values[n].abs()]);
}
// Fit a line to ln|c_n(t)| by least squares; its slope is the measured growth rate.
let logs: Vec<[f64; 2]> = points
    .iter()
    .filter(|p| p[1] > 0.0)
    .map(|p| [p[0], RealScalar::ln(p[1])])
    .collect();
let count = logs.len() as f64;
let mean_t = logs.iter().map(|p| p[0]).sum::<f64>() / count;
let mean_l = logs.iter().map(|p| p[1]).sum::<f64>() / count;
let sxx: f64 = logs.iter().map(|p| (p[0] - mean_t) * (p[0] - mean_t)).sum();
let sxy: f64 = logs.iter().map(|p| (p[0] - mean_t) * (p[1] - mean_l)).sum();
let slope = (logs.len() >= 2).then(|| sxy / sxx);
let deviation = slope.map(|s| {
    logs.iter()
        .map(|p| (p[1] - mean_l - s * (p[0] - mean_t)).abs())
        .fold(0.0, f64::max)
});
let counts = report.counts();
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![serde_json::json!({ "name": "DCT amplitude |c_n|", "points": points })],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Time t [-]",
        "yLabel": "Amplitude |c_n| [-]",
        "yType": "log"
    })
);
let sig6 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 6 });
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let na = "Undefined";
emit_table_with_columns!(
    serde_json::json!([
        { "key": "0", "label": "Continuous-system growth rate σ(k) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
        { "key": "1", "label": "Spatially discrete growth rate λ_h [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
        { "key": "2", "label": "Measured σ̂ [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6, "nullText": na },
        { "key": "3", "label": "Difference from λ_h [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": na },
        { "key": "4", "label": "Maximum residual [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": na },
        { "key": "5", "label": "Accepted steps [count]", "type": "number" },
        { "key": "6", "label": "Right-hand-side evaluations [count]", "type": "number" },
        { "key": "7", "label": "Jacobian evaluations [count]", "type": "number" }
    ]),
    vec![(
        mode_eigenvalues(k * k, ratio)[0][0],
        lambda,
        slope,
        slope.map(|v| (v - lambda).abs()),

        report.accepted_steps(),

        counts.provider(EvaluationRole::Jacobian)
    )],

    .termination()
    .map(|t| t.to_string())

} else {

);
```

With the defaults ($d = 20$, $n = 3$), the amplitude forms a straight line on the logarithmic axis, and the fitted growth rate agrees with $\lambda_h = 0.261817$ to a difference of order $10^{-8}$.
The maximum fit residual is also of order $10^{-9}$, showing that the amplitude follows a single exponential.
This matches the estimated time-integration error of order $10^{-8}$ from the tolerances.
The difference $5 \times 10^{-4}$ from the continuous-system $\sigma(k) = 0.262348$ comes from spatial discretization and is more than four orders of magnitude larger than the time-integration error.
About 250 steps are accepted, with roughly 10,000 right-hand-side evaluations, about 90% of them used for finite-difference Jacobians.

For `below-critical` ($d = 8$), $\lambda_h \approx -0.0984$; the amplitude decreases along a straight line, and the fitted growth rate is negative.
For `short-wave` ($n = 7$), $\lambda_h \approx -0.372$ versus $\sigma(k) \approx -0.482$, increasing their difference to 0.11.
This mode has only about 5.7 points per wavelength; the growth-rate difference reflects insufficient grid resolution.
For both cases and the defaults, the fitted value is close to $\lambda_h$ rather than $\sigma(k)$.
This indicates that time integration correctly solves the semidiscrete system, with the continuous-system discrepancy arising solely from spatial discretization.

Even with $d > d_c$, the discrete wavenumbers allowed by the finite interval need not fall in the unstable band.
At $d = 10$, the unstable band is narrow, $k^2 \in (0.2,\ 0.5)$, containing only $n = 3$ ($k_h^2 = 0.218$) and $n = 4$ ($k_h^2 = 0.382$).
For $n = 3$, $\lambda_h$ is only about 0.019, so the amplitude grows by only about 1.2 times by $t = 10$.

## Verification with semidiscrete eigenvalues

### Assembling the Jacobian

The preceding cell excited only one mode.
All eigenvalues of the semidiscrete Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_h = \mathbf{J}_0 \otimes I - M^{-1}A$ reveal whether this mode grows fastest overall and whether the short-side modes are indeed stable.
Since the system is linear, the right-hand side $M^{-1}(M\mathbf{J}_0\mathbf{e}_j - A\mathbf{e}_j)$ applied to unit vector $\mathbf{e}_j$ is exactly column $j$ of $\mathbf{J}_h$.
We assemble the dense $84 \times 84$ matrix with 84 operator applications.
The constructor `Matrix::from_vec(rows, cols, data)` takes ownership of a row-major array, with element $(i, j)$ stored at `data[i * cols + j]`.

Predictions are the two eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}_0 - \kappa\operatorname{diag}(1, d)$ for $\kappa = 4\sin^2(n\pi/40) + 4\sin^2(m\pi/2)$ at each of the 42 pairs of long-side mode $n = 0, \ldots, 20$ and short-side mode $m = 0, 1$, giving 84 values in total.
Cosine pairs form a complete eigenvector basis of $M^{-1}A$, so in this basis $\mathbf{J}_h$ is similar to a direct sum of $2 \times 2$ blocks; the 84 predictions therefore exhaust the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}_h$.

### Hessenberg reduction and Francis QR

The function `GeneralEigenvaluesResult::compute(&matrix)` computes all eigenvalues of a general square matrix.
It scales by the largest entry magnitude, balances the matrix (diagonal similarity transformations and permutations matching row and column magnitudes), then uses Householder reflections to reduce it to upper Hessenberg form.
For real matrices, it then applies Francis double-shift QR iterations in real arithmetic, converging to quasi-upper-triangular real Schur form.
Computing only eigenvalues costs approximately $10n^3$ floating-point operations, or about $6 \times 10^6$ at $n = 84$.
The default QR iteration limit is $30n$; exceeding it returns `ConvergenceFailure`.
NaN or infinite input entries return `NonFinite`; a nonsquare input returns `DimensionMismatch`.
Eigenvalues are a sequence of complex numbers `Complex`, sorted by ascending real part by default; complex eigenvalues of real matrices are returned as exact conjugate pairs.
The field `report().iterations()` gives the QR iteration count.
[General matrices: Hessenberg reduction and Francis QR iteration](oxref:linalg-general-eigen-qr) gives algorithmic details.

QR is backward stable: computed eigenvalues are exact eigenvalues of $\mathbf{J}_h + E$ with a perturbation of size $\lVert E \rVert \approx \varepsilon\lVert \mathbf{J}_h \rVert$.
Eigenvalue error is approximately $\lVert E \rVert / s(\lambda)$, where $s(\lambda)$ is the cosine of the angle between left and right eigenvectors (the reciprocal eigenvalue condition number).
The matrix $\mathbf{J}_0$ is nonnormal, so $s(\lambda) < 1$; however, for this page's $2 \times 2$ blocks, $s(\lambda)$ is near 1, about 0.71 at $n = 0$ and 0.88 at $n = 3$.
The operator $M^{-1}A$ is symmetric in the $M$ inner product, and the similarity transformation from the mass ratio (interior 0.5, corners 0.25) has condition number only $\sqrt{2}$.
Eigenvalue errors are therefore estimated as a few times the spectral radius times $\varepsilon$, of order $10^{-13}$.
[Eigenvalue sensitivity of nonnormal matrices](oxref:linalg-nonnormal-eigen-sensitivity) gives an example of sensitivity under strong nonnormality.

### Cell inputs and outputs

The following cell takes diffusion ratio $d$, assembles $\mathbf{J}_h$, and computes all eigenvalues.
It measures the complex-plane distance from each predicted eigenvalue to the nearest computed value and takes the maximum as the matching error.
The table columns are the computed and predicted maximum real parts, their difference, counts of eigenvalues with positive real parts (computed and predicted), matching error, spectral radius, and QR iteration count.
The outcome is `satisfied` if the matching error is at most $10^{-12}$ times the spectral radius, or `failed` for failed verification otherwise.
This threshold leaves margin above the preceding estimate of a few times the spectral radius times $\varepsilon$.

```rust
//| id: app-turing-semidiscrete-spectrum
//| title: "Compare all semidiscrete Jacobian eigenvalues with the discrete dispersion relation"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [app-turing-strip-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-pde, scioxide-spatial, scioxide-linalg]
//| inputs:
//|   ratio: {"type":"range","label":"Diffusion ratio d [-]","description":"Below d_c ≈ 8.57, no eigenvalues have positive real parts.","min":8,"max":24,"step":2,"value":20}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"below-critical","label":"d = 8 < d_c","description":"Every eigenvalue has a negative real part.","inputs":{"ratio":8}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"spectrum","path":"/rows/0/0","approx":{"value":0.2618170051541087,"rtol":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"spectrum","path":"/rows/0/3","equals":4}
//|       - {"artifact":"spectrum","path":"/rows/0/4","equals":4}
//|       - {"artifact":"spectrum","path":"/rows/0/5","range":{"max":1e-10}}
//|   - case: below-critical
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"spectrum","path":"/rows/0/0","range":{"max":0}}
//|       - {"artifact":"spectrum","path":"/rows/0/3","equals":0}
use scioxide_core::{LinearOperator, RealScalar};
use scioxide_linalg::{GeneralEigenvaluesResult, Matrix};
use scioxide_pde::SpatialDiscretization;

let plan = strip_plan(ratio).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let size = plan.unknowns();
let mass = plan.lumped_mass();
let mut scratch = vec![0.0; plan.apply_workspace_len().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?];
// Since the system is linear, M⁻¹(M J₀ e_j − A e_j) applied to unit vector e_j gives column j of the Jacobian.
let mut dense = vec![0.0; size * size];
let mut unit = vec![0.0; size];
let mut column = vec![0.0; size];
for j in 0..size {
    unit[j] = 1.0;
    linearized_rhs(&plan, mass, &unit, &mut column, &mut scratch).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    unit[j] = 0.0;
    for i in 0..size {
        dense[i * size + j] = column[i] / mass[i];
    }
}
let matrix = Matrix::from_vec(size, size, dense).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let spectrum = GeneralEigenvaluesResult::compute(&matrix).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let computed: Vec<[f64; 2]> = spectrum
    .eigenvalues()
    .iter()
    .map(|z| [z.re, z.im])
    .collect();
// Independent check: 2×2 eigenvalues for every pair of long-side mode n = 0..=20 and short-side mode m = 0, 1.
let mut predicted = Vec::new();
for m in 0..2 {
    for n in 0..=CELLS {
        let kh2 = discrete_wavenumber2(n, CELLS as f64) + discrete_wavenumber2(m, 1.0);
        predicted.extend(mode_eigenvalues(kh2, ratio));
    }
}
// Measure the distance from each predicted eigenvalue to its nearest computed value; take the maximum as the matching error.
let nearest = |p: [f64; 2]| {
    computed
        .iter()
        .map(|c| RealScalar::hypot(p[0] - c[0], p[1] - c[1]))
        .fold(f64::INFINITY, f64::min)
};
let mismatch = predicted.iter().map(|&p| nearest(p)).fold(0.0, f64::max);
let radius = computed
    .iter()
    .map(|c| RealScalar::hypot(c[0], c[1]))
    .fold(0.0, f64::max);
let largest = |values: &[[f64; 2]]| {
    values
        .iter()
        .map(|v| v[0])
        .fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max)
};
let positive = |values: &[[f64; 2]]| values.iter().filter(|v| v[0] > 0.0).count();
let sig6 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 6 });
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
emit_table_with_columns!(
    serde_json::json!([
        { "key": "0", "label": "Maximum real part (QR) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
        { "key": "1", "label": "Maximum real part (dispersion relation) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
        { "key": "2", "label": "Maximum real-part difference [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
        { "key": "3", "label": "Positive real parts (QR) [count]", "type": "number" },
        { "key": "4", "label": "Positive real parts (dispersion relation) [count]", "type": "number" },
        { "key": "5", "label": "Matching error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
        { "key": "6", "label": "Spectral radius [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
        { "key": "7", "label": "QR iterations [count]", "type": "number" }
    ]),
    vec![(
        largest(&computed),
        largest(&predicted),
        (largest(&computed) - largest(&predicted)).abs(),
        positive(&computed),
        positive(&predicted),
        mismatch,
        radius,
        spectrum.report().iterations()
    )],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "spectrum" })
);
let outcome = if mismatch <= 1e-12 * radius {
    Outcome::satisfied("All eigenvalues agree with the discrete dispersion relation")
} else {
    Outcome::failed("Eigenvalues disagree with the discrete dispersion relation")
};
emit_outcome!(
    outcome
        .diagnostic("Eigenvalue matching error", mismatch, None)
        .diagnostic("Spectral radius", radius, None)
);
```

With the default $d = 20$, the computed maximum real part is $0.261817$, agreeing with $\lambda_h$ for mode $n = 3$ excited in the preceding cell to a difference of order $10^{-15}$.
Both the computation and prediction give four eigenvalues with positive real parts ($n = 2, 3, 4, 5$); all short-side modes $m = 1$ are stable.
The matching error is of order $3 \times 10^{-13}$ and the spectral radius about 161, giving a ratio of order $2 \times 10^{-15}$ consistent with the backward-error estimate.
There are just over 100 QR iterations, or one to two per eigenvalue.

For `below-critical` ($d = 8$), the maximum real part is approximately $-0.029$, and no eigenvalue has a positive real part.
This is the real eigenvalue of mode $n = 4$, $m = 0$, indicating that the uniform state is weakly stable just below criticality.
Increasing $d$ to 24 increases the maximum real part to about 0.307 and the spectral radius to about 193, roughly proportional to $d$.

## Error breakdown and computational cost

The comparisons on this page separately measure four stages of error.

- Critical-diffusion-ratio root error is determined by Brent's residual test and root conditioning, estimated at relative order $3 \times 10^{-14}$ or less; the default relative difference from the closed form is of order $2 \times 10^{-16}$.
- Spatial discretization error is the difference between $\sigma(k)$ and $\lambda_h$, proportional to $h^2$ according to $k_h^2 - k^2 \approx -h^2k^4/12$ and equal to $5 \times 10^{-4}$ for the default mode.
- Time-integration error is the difference between $\hat\sigma$ and $\lambda_h$, of order $10^{-8}$ corresponding to tolerance $10^{-7}$.
- Eigenvalue-computation error comes only from rounding and is of order $10^{-15}$ relative to the spectral radius.

Spatial discretization determines the final growth-rate accuracy; using the semidiscrete growth rate $\lambda_h$ as the reference prevents mistaking this error for time-integration error.
To obtain accurate continuous-system growth rates, reducing spacing $h$ is more effective than tightening time-integration tolerances.
Halving the long-side spacing $h$ reduces spatial error to about one-quarter and approximately doubles the unknown count.
Meanwhile, $k_h^2 = 4/h^2$ for the shortest-wavelength mode also quadruples, increasing stiffness.

Time integration accounts for most of the cost.
Each step performs one dense LU factorization of an $84 \times 84$ matrix, and each recomputed finite-difference Jacobian needs 84 right-hand-side evaluations.
For grids exceeding several thousand unknowns, this dense processing dominates; switch to sparse LU or GMRES through `RosenbrockW::with_backend`, or supply an analytical Jacobian.
Eigenvalue verification costs $O(n^3)$ and is inexpensive at $n = 84$; for larger grids, use [partial-spectrum](../../scioxide-linalg/algorithms/partial-spectra/) methods to compute only a few eigenvalues with large real parts.

## Summary and further reading

The uniform steady state of the Schnakenberg system ($a = 0.1$, $b = 0.9$) becomes unstable above diffusion ratio $d_c \approx 8.5676$; this value follows from a quadratic in $\sqrt{d}$ and agrees with Brent's root to within rounding error.
If changing supply rates gives $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{J}_0) \ge 0$, instability already exists without diffusion, so Turing-instability analysis does not apply.
A finite interval permits only modes $k = n\pi/L$; on a grid, the corresponding squared wavenumbers are $k_h^2 = 4\sin^2(kh/2)/h^2$, so use $\lambda_h = \sigma(k_h)$ as the growth-rate reference.
Comparing measured growth with both $\lambda_h$ and $\sigma(k)$ separates the errors: differences of the order of the tolerance come from time integration, while those explained by the shift between $k_h$ and $k$ come from spatial discretization.
Computing all semidiscrete eigenvalues by QR and comparing with dispersion predictions checks which mode grows fastest and whether short-side modes are stable.
