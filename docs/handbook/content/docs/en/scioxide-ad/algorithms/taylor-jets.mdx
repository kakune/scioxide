Taylor methods for ordinary differential equations, higher rates of change along curves, and multivariate mixed partial derivatives require derivatives through order $K$ along a direction or mixed partials such as $\partial^{a+b} f/\partial x^a \partial y^b$.
Nesting first-order forward mode to obtain order $K$ doubles the components at each level and stores $2^K$ components.
Taylor arithmetic, which stores a truncated power series as its value, obtains derivatives through order $K$ with one evaluation and $O(K^2)$ coefficient operations per operation.
This page covers the recurrences, types and functions, mixed coefficients, special conventions, and accuracy of Taylor jets `Jet` in `scioxide-ad`.
[Forward mode and dual numbers](../forward-mode/) explains first-order forward mode and dual numbers.

The derivation assumes that the function is continuously differentiable through the required order near the evaluation point.

## Normalized Taylor coefficients

For a function $x(t)$ of one variable $t$, the normalized Taylor coefficients are obtained by expanding around $t = 0$ as

$$
x(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} c_k\, t^k + O(t^N), \qquad c_k = \frac{1}{k!} \left.\frac{d^k x}{dt^k}\right|_{t=0}
$$

where $c_k$ denotes a normalized Taylor coefficient.
The derivative of order $k$ is $k!\,c_k$.
For input on a straight line $x(t) = p + t\,d$, the coefficients of $f(x(t))$ are $c_k = \frac{1}{k!} D^k f(p)[d, \dots, d]$, or $c_k = f^{(k)}(p)\,d^k/k!$ in one variable.

There are two reasons to store $f^{(k)}/k!$ rather than the derivatives themselves.
First, the binomial coefficients in the Leibniz rule $(uv)^{(k)} = \sum_j \binom{k}{j} u^{(j)} v^{(k-j)}$ for products disappear, giving the Cauchy product in the next section.
Second, in terms of magnitude, if the radius of convergence in $t$ is $R$, $|c_k|$ stays on the order of $R^{-k}$ whereas $f^{(k)}$ grows on the order of $k!/R^k$.
In double precision, $171! > 1.8 \times 10^{308}$, so at $k = 171$ even $k!$ alone overflows.
`Jet` does not form factorials, so, for example, the coefficients $(-1)^k$ of $1/(1 + t)$ are computed exactly even for $k \ge 171$.

## Coefficient recurrences

Each operation maps input series to an output series.
No rule for composite functions needs to be written: applying the operations in sequence gives the series of $f(g(x(t)))$.
$c_0$ is exactly the value of the same operation in the coefficient type `V`, and $c_1$ is the first-order rule's partial derivatives multiplied by the inputs' $c_1$.
Coefficients for $k \ge 2$ are obtained by equating coefficients on both sides of a differential equation satisfied by the function.

Consider $w = e^u$ as an example.
Writing differentiation with respect to $t$ as $'$ gives $w' = w\,u'$, and multiplying both sides by $t$ gives $t w' = w \cdot t u'$.
The coefficient of order $k$ in $t\,w'$ is $k\,w_k$, so equating coefficients of $t^k$ gives

$$
w_k = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^{k} j\, u_j\, w_{k-j}
$$


Each term is multiplied by the integer $j$ and the sum is divided once by $k$, so no factorials appear.
The same comparison gives the following recurrences for other operations ($k \ge 2$).

| Operation | Relation | Coefficients |
|---|---|---|
| Sum, difference, negation, constant multiple | Linear | Apply the same operation to each coefficient. Adding or subtracting a constant changes only $c_0$ |
| Product $w = uv$ | Cauchy product | $w_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} u_j v_{k-j}$ |
| Quotient $w = u/v$ | $vw = u$ | $w_k = \bigl(u_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k} v_j w_{k-j}\bigr)/v_0$ |
| Reciprocal $w = 1/u$ | $uw = 1$ | $w_k = -\sum_{j=1}^{k} u_j w_{k-j}/u_0$ |
| Square root $w = \sqrt u$ | $w^2 = u$ | $w_k = \bigl(u_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} w_j w_{k-j}\bigr)/(2w_0)$ |
| Exponential $w = e^u$ | $w' = w u'$ | $w_k = \frac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k} j\,u_j w_{k-j}$ |
| Logarithm $w = \ln u$ | $u w' = u'$ | $w_k = \bigl(u_k - \frac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} j\,w_j u_{k-j}\bigr)/u_0$ |
| Power with constant exponent $w = u^p$ (Miller's recurrence) | $u w' = p\,w u'$ | $w_k = \frac{1}{k\,u_0} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \bigl(p j - (k - j)\bigr)\, u_j w_{k-j}$ |
| Sine and cosine $s = \sin u$, $c = \cos u$ | $s' = c u'$, $c' = -s u'$ | $s_k = \frac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k} j\,u_j c_{k-j}$, $c_k = -\frac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k} j\,u_j s_{k-j}$ |
| Tangent $y = \tan u$ | $y' = w u'$, $w = 1 + y^2$ | $y_k = \frac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k} j\,u_j w_{k-j}$, $w_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} y_j y_{k-j}$ |
| Inverse functions such as $y = \operatorname{asin} u$ | $q\,y' = \pm u'$ | $y_k = \bigl(\pm u_k - \frac1k \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} j\,y_j q_{k-j}\bigr)/q_0$ |

Sine and cosine use coupled recurrences advancing both series together; `sinh` and `cosh` have the same form with a sign change.
`tan` also maintains the auxiliary series $w = 1 + y^2$, and `tanh` maintains $w = 1 - y^2$.
`sigmoid` $\sigma(v)$ carries $\sigma' = D v'$ and $D' = (1 - 2\sigma)\,\sigma'$, initializing $1 - 2\sigma$ from $-\tanh(v_0/2)$ so that no term subtracts the rounded saturated value of $\sigma$ from 1.
For the inverse functions, $q$ is $\sqrt{1 - u^2}$ for `asin` and `acos`, $1 + u^2$ for `atan`, $\sqrt{1 + u^2}$ for `asinh`, $\sqrt{u^2 - 1}$ for `acosh`, and $1 - u^2$ for `atanh`.
The series for $q$ is constructed after division by $m = \max(|u_0|, 1)$ ($m^2$ for `atan` and `atanh`), preventing overflow when squaring.
`powi` uses Miller's recurrence for a nonzero base and binary exponentiation of the series (repeated Cauchy products) at a zero base, avoiding division by zero.
`cbrt` uses Miller's recurrence with $p = 1/3$; `hypot` uses a polar-form recurrence maintaining series for the direction cosines $(x/h, y/h)$.
The `hypot` recurrence avoids cancellation that equating coefficients of $h^2 = x^2 + y^2$ causes for nearly radial directions $(x', y')$.

Each nonlinear operation costs $O(N^2)$ operations in `V`; a linear operation costs $O(N)$.
Products in the recurrences are IEEE products, multiplication and addition are separate (without `mul_add`), and sums accumulate in index order, so results do not depend on FMA availability.

## The Jet type

`Jet<V, N>` is a `Copy` type holding `N` normalized coefficients $c_0, \dots, c_{N-1}$ as values of type `V`.
`V` can be any numeric type implementing `Real`; `Jet<Lanes<f64, 8>, N>` expands 8 lanes simultaneously.
`Jet` itself implements `Real`, so a function written once for `R: Real` can be evaluated directly in Taylor arithmetic.
Ordering comparisons use $c_0$.

- `Jet::new(coefficients)` constructs a jet from an array of coefficients.
  `N ≥ 1` is checked at compile time; code constructing `Jet<f64, 0>` does not compile.
- `Jet::variable(x0)` constructs the independent variable $x_0 + t$ ($c_0 = x_0$, $c_1 = 1$, and zero above that).
- `coefficients()` returns the array `[V; N]`; `coefficient(k)` returns $c_k$ as an `Option`, with `None` for $k \ge N$.
- `Jet<V, 3>` holds the value, first derivative, and second derivative ($2c_2$).

$c_0$ agrees bit for bit with the same operation's value in `V`.
$c_1$ uses the same partial derivatives, so it agrees with the derivative from `Dual`, with two differences.
One is the sign of zero.
The other occurs when a nonfinite partial derivative meets an exactly zero coefficient: `Jet` uses IEEE multiplication and gives NaN, while `Dual` uses the absolute-zero product `azmul` in many rules and returns $+0$.
For example, for `sqrt(max(v, 0))` at $v < 0$, the derivative from `Dual` is $+0$ whereas $c_1$ of `Jet` is NaN.

## Coefficients along a straight line with jet

The one-shot `jet(function, point, direction)` evaluates the function with each input represented as $[p_i, d_i, 0, \dots]$ in a `Jet`, returning normalized coefficients of $f(p + t\,d)$ in `[S; N]`.
$c_0$ is the value, $c_1$ is the directional derivative $\nabla f \cdot d$, and $2c_2$ is the curvature $d^{\mathsf T} \nabla^2 f\, d$; in general, $k!\,c_k = D^k f(p)[d, \dots, d]$.
The argument to `function` is `&[Jet<S, N>]`; specify the return type `[f64; N]` to determine `N`.
It returns `DimensionMismatch` if the length of `direction` differs from `point` and `AllocationFailure` if the input array cannot be allocated; errors from the function are returned unchanged.
For functions with multiple outputs or computations with multiple directions, evaluate a generic function directly with `Jet`.

## Rounding error and accuracy limits

The recurrences give the exact coefficients of the truncated series, so there is no truncation error; only rounding error remains.
Each stage uses rounded lower-order coefficients, so error accumulates with order.

The accuracy guarantee for `Jet` that `scioxide-ad` verifies in tests against high-precision references is the following bound.
At points where arguments and coefficients are zero or have magnitudes in $[\sqrt{\mathrm{MIN\_POSITIVE}}, \sqrt{\mathrm{MAX}}]$, the absolute error of coefficient $c_k$ is at most

$$
64\,\varepsilon\,(k + 1)\, g + (\text{smallest subnormal})
$$


$\varepsilon$ is machine epsilon and $g$ is the sum of the absolute values of the terms in the exact composition.
This bound is stated for `f32` and `f64` through order 8, or total order 8 for mixed coefficients; no bound is specified for higher orders.
Outside that range, IEEE arithmetic can overflow or underflow directly.
[Floating-point numbers and conditioning](../../../concepts/floating-point-and-conditioning/) covers floating-point error in general.

$g$ is at least $|c_k|$ and equals $|c_k|$ when all terms have the same sign.
For compositions with cancellation, $g \gg |c_k|$, and the relative error of small coefficients can be large.
The recurrences for quotients, reciprocals, logarithms, square roots, and inverse functions divide by $u_0$ or $w_0$, so near a zero denominator, coefficients and $g$ grow like $(1/u_0)^k$.
Converting back to the derivative $k!\,c_k$ changes relative error only by the rounding of one multiplication, but multiplies the value by $k!$.

The next cell computes coefficients of three series with known closed forms around $t = 0$ through order 30 and shows the error divided by $\varepsilon\,(k + 1)\,g_k$ (called the error ratio below).
The upper bound on the error ratio for $k \le 8$ is 64.

- $\tan t$: $c_k = E_k/k!$ for odd $k$, and zero at even orders.
  $E_k$ are tangent numbers, computed in the cell with Seidel's triangle (integer additions only).
  Every recurrence term is nonnegative, so $g_k = |c_k|$.
- $e^t$：$c_k = 1/k!$, $g_k = c_k$.
- $e^t \cdot e^{-t}$: the correct coefficient for $k \ge 1$ is zero, and the sum of absolute values of Cauchy-product terms is $g_k = \sum_j \frac{1}{j!\,(k-j)!} = 2^k/k!$.

The independent reference values are computed by 64-bit long division of exactly constructed integer fractions, then rounded to `f64`.
The chart plots coefficients with nonzero error on logarithmic axes (zero-error coefficients are counted in the table), with an annotation line for the error-ratio bound 64 and a shaded range for $k \le 8$ where the bound is stated.

```rust
//| id: ad-taylor-exp-series
//| ref: ad-taylor-exp-series
//| title: "Normalized Taylor coefficients of known series and error bounds"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-ad]
//| inputs:
//|   function: {"type":"select","label":"Function","options":["tan t","exp t","e^t · e^(−t)"],"value":"tan t"}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"exp","label":"exp t","description":"The coefficient 1/k! decreases to 10⁻³³ at order 30.","inputs":{"function":"exp t"}}
//|   - {"id":"cancellation","label":"e^t · e^(−t)","description":"The correct coefficient is zero; the error scale is determined by g_k = 2ᵏ/k!.","inputs":{"function":"e^t · e^(−t)"}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"coefficients","path":"/rows/2/1","approx":{"value":0.3333333333333333,"rtol":1e-15}}
//|       - {"artifact":"coefficients","path":"/rows/1/1","equals":0}
//|       - {"artifact":"summary","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"max":64}}
//|       - {"artifact":"summary","path":"/rows/0/2","range":{"max":64}}
//|   - case: exp
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"coefficients","path":"/rows/6/1","approx":{"value":3.769987628815906e-33,"rtol":1e-14}}
//|       - {"artifact":"summary","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"max":64}}
//|   - case: cancellation
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"coefficients","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":0}
//|       - {"artifact":"summary","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"max":64}}
//|       - {"artifact":"summary","path":"/rows/0/2","range":{"max":64}}
use scioxide_ad::{Jet, Real};

const N: usize = 31;
let t = Jet::<f64, N>::variable(0.0);
let series = match function.as_str() {
    "tan t" => t.tan(),
    "exp t" => t.exp(),
    _ => t.exp() * (-t).exp(),
};
let c = series.coefficients();

// Reference value (independent check): compute integer fraction p/q by 64-bit long division, then round once to f64.
let ratio = |p: u128, q: u128| -> f64 {
    if p == 0 {
        return 0.0;
    }
    let (mut p, mut q, mut scale) = (p, q, 0_i32);
    while p / 2 >= q {
        q <<= 1;
        scale += 1;
    }
    while p < q {
        p <<= 1;
        scale -= 1;
    }
    let mut bits = 0_u64;
    for _ in 0..64 {
        bits <<= 1;
        if p >= q {
            p -= q;
            bits |= 1;
        }
        p <<= 1;
    }
    // Exact because it only multiplies by a power of 2 (reference-value computation).
    bits as f64 * 2.0_f64.powi(scale - 63)
};
// Compute tangent numbers E_k with Seidel's triangle.
let mut zigzag = vec![1_u128; N];
let mut row = vec![1_u128];
for k in 1..N {
    let mut next = vec![0_u128; k + 1];
    for j in 1..=k {
        next[j] = next[j - 1] + row[k - j];
    }
    zigzag[k] = next[k];
    row = next;
}
let eps = f64::EPSILON;
let (mut factorial, mut zeros) = (1_u128, 0_usize);
let (mut points, mut rows) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new());
let (mut worst_low, mut worst_high) = (0.0_f64, 0.0_f64);
for k in 0..N {
    if k > 0 {
        factorial *= k as u128;
    }
    // (Reference value c*_k, sum of absolute term values g_k)
    let (exact, growth) = match function.as_str() {
        "tan t" if k % 2 == 1 => (ratio(zigzag[k], factorial), ratio(zigzag[k], factorial)),
        "tan t" => (0.0, 0.0),
        "exp t" => (ratio(1, factorial), ratio(1, factorial)),
        _ => (if k == 0 { 1.0 } else { 0.0 }, ratio(1 << k, factorial)),
    };
    let error = (c[k] - exact).abs();
    let scaled = (growth > 0.0).then(|| error / (eps * (k as f64 + 1.0) * growth));
    if error == 0.0 {
        zeros += 1;
    } else if let Some(value) = scaled {
        points.push([k as f64, value]);
    }
    let worst = if k <= 8 {
        &mut worst_low
    } else {
        &mut worst_high
    };
    *worst = worst.max(scaled.unwrap_or(0.0));
    if [1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 15, 30].contains(&k) {
        rows.push((k, c[k], exact, growth, error, scaled));
    }
}
emit_scatter_chart!(
    &vec![serde_json::json!({ "name": "Error ratio", "points": points })],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Order k [-]",
        "yLabel": "Error ratio |c_k − c*_k| / (ε(k + 1)g_k) [-]",
        "yType": "log",
        "xMin": 0.0,
        "xMax": 30.0,
        "annotations": [
            { "kind": "line", "axis": "y", "value": 64.0, "label": "Upper bound 64" },
            { "kind": "range", "axis": "x", "from": 0.0, "to": 8.0, "label": "Range where the bound is stated" }
        ]
    })
);
let sci4 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 4 });
let sci2 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Order k", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Jet c_k [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Reference c*_k [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "g_k [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "|c_k − c*_k| [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Error ratio [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2, "nullText": "g_k = 0" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "coefficients" })
);
let summary_columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Zero-error coefficients (out of 31)", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Maximum error ratio, k ≤ 8 [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Maximum error ratio, 9 ≤ k ≤ 30 [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    summary_columns,
    vec![(zeros, worst_low, worst_high)],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "summary" })
);
```

For the default $\tan t$, 23 of 31 coefficients agree bit for bit with the reference and all even-order zeros are exactly zero.
Even coefficients with nonzero error have error ratios at most 0.074, about three orders of magnitude below the bound of 64.
Ratios are at the same level for $k \le 8$ and $9 \le k \le 30$, with no tendency to increase with order.

For $e^t$, coefficients decrease by more than 32 orders of magnitude to $3.8 \times 10^{-33}$ at order 30, but 18 of 31 agree exactly and the remaining error ratios are at most 0.079.
For compositions whose terms all share a sign, $g_k = |c_k|$, so relative error stays on the order of $\varepsilon$ even when coefficient magnitudes span many orders of magnitude.

For $e^t \cdot e^{-t}$, coefficients with $k \ge 3$ do not become zero; even at order 30, $1.2 \times 10^{-41}$ remains.
Relative error is undefined because the correct coefficient is zero, but the error ratio of the remaining value is at most 0.024.
The scale of error is determined by the sum of absolute term values $g_k$ rather than $|c_k|$, which explains why the bound is written using $g$.
In all three examples the error ratio is at most 0.08, about three orders of magnitude below the bound of 64.

## Mixed coefficients with nested Jet types

`Jet<Jet<V, M>, N>` holds a bivariate series.
With outer variable $s$ and inner variable $t$, inner coefficient $j$ of outer coefficient $i$ is given by

$$
c_{ij} = \frac{1}{i!\,j!} \left.\frac{\partial^{i+j} x}{\partial s^i\, \partial t^j}\right|_{s = t = 0}
$$

and is read with `f.coefficients()[i].coefficients()[j]`.
Construct $x = x_0 + s$ with `Jet::variable(Jet::constant(x0))` and $y = y_0 + t$ with `Jet::lift(Jet::variable(y0))`.
Outer operations use the same recurrences with inner `Jet` coefficients; outer products and multiplication by weights $j/k$ simply become inner Taylor arithmetic, so factorials do not appear here either.

Storage consists of $N \cdot M$ values of `V`, holding every coefficient in the rectangle $i < N$, $j < M$.
A nonlinear operation performs $O(N^2)$ inner operations at the outer level, each costing $O(M^2)$, giving $O(N^2 M^2)$ operations in `V`.
The entire rectangle is computed even if only a subset of low-total-order coefficients is needed.

The next cell evaluates $f(x, y) = e^{xy}$ with `Jet<Jet<f64, 6>, 6>` and compares $c_{pq}$ with a closed form.
Substituting $(x_0 + s)(y_0 + t) = x_0 y_0 + y_0 s + x_0 t + st$ into the exponential series and expanding gives

$$
c_{pq} = e^{x_0 y_0} \sum_{m=0}^{\min(p, q)} \frac{y_0^{\,p-m}\, x_0^{\,q-m}}{(p-m)!\,(q-m)!\,m!}
$$

which is computed independently in `f64`.
The input is evaluation point $(x_0, y_0)$.

## Kinks and operations that do not compute coefficients

`Jet` applies the first-order kink convention shared by all numeric types (the midpoint of one-sided derivatives) coefficient by coefficient ([Kinks and nonfinite-value policies](../nonsmooth-and-nonfinite-policies/)).
The rules for `abs`, `min`, `max`, `clamp`, and `%` multiply partial derivatives by coefficients with the absolute-zero product `azmul`, so even NaN or infinite coefficients on a side with zero partial derivative (the unselected side, or `abs` at $x_0 = 0$) do not enter the result.

- `abs` multiplies all coefficients from $c_1$ upward by $\operatorname{sign} x_0$, or by zero at $x_0 = 0$.
- `min`, `max`, and `clamp` take coefficients from the selected side, averaging the two sides' coefficients when $c_0$ is equal.
- `floor`, `ceil`, `round`, `trunc`, and `signum` have zero coefficients from $c_1$ upward.
- `fract` and `%` follow the definitions $x - \operatorname{trunc}(x)$ and $a - \operatorname{trunc}(a_0/b_0)\,b$.
- At the origin, `hypot` uses partial derivatives $(0, 0)$ for $c_1$ and gives NaN from $c_2$ upward.

The rule for `powf` depends on the exponent's series.

- If the exponent has zero coefficients from $c_1$ upward (constant exponent $y_0$), Miller's recurrence is used, so coefficients can be obtained for a negative base when the value is finite, as in $(-2 + t)^3$.
- For a constant exponent and zero base, a nonnegative integer $y_0$ gives the same coefficients as `powi`.
  Otherwise coefficients for $k \ge 2$ are zero when $k < y_0$ and NaN when $k \ge y_0$.
  For example, $t^{2.5}$ has $c_0 = c_1 = c_2 = 0$ and NaN from $c_3$ upward, consistent with the divergence of the third derivative of $t^{2.5}$ at $t = 0$.
- For a varying exponent, the recurrence for $e^{y \ln x}$ is used; a zero base gives NaN from $c_2$ upward, and a negative base from $c_1$ upward.

Some operations do not compute higher coefficients and set them to NaN.

- `atan2` and `Real::combine` are exact through first order and NaN from $c_2$ upward.
- `exprel`, `sinc`, `sincpi`, and `Real::map_scalar`, for which the caller provides the value and first and second derivatives (likewise `try_map_scalar`), are exact through second order and NaN from $c_3$ upward.

Using NaN rather than zero propagates uncomputed coefficients to every dependent result, preventing them from remaining as incorrect finite values.
When higher coefficients of these operations are needed, rewrite the same function using operations with coefficient recurrences.
For example, writing `sinc` as `sin(x) / x` gives all coefficients (at points with $x_0 \ne 0$).

The next cell computes `Jet<f64, 4>` coefficients at points where these conventions apply and places them beside derivatives from `Dual`.

## Summary

Derivatives through order $N - 1$ along direction $d$ are obtained with `jet` as normalized coefficients $c_k = D^k f(p)[d, \dots, d]/k!$, at a cost of $O(N^2)$ per nonlinear operation.
Obtain bivariate mixed partial derivatives by multiplying $c_{ij}$ of `Jet<Jet<f64, M>, N>` by $i!\,j!$, keeping in mind that $s$ is the outer variable and $t$ the inner variable.
The error bound through order 8 is $64\,\varepsilon\,(k + 1)\,g$; because $g$ is the sum of absolute term values in the composition, check relative error against $|c_k|$ separately for functions with cancellation or nearly zero denominators.
Higher coefficients of `atan2`, `combine`, `exprel`, `sinc`, `sincpi`, and `map_scalar` become NaN; if needed, rewrite the same function using operations with coefficient recurrences.
Converting to the derivative $k!\,c_k$ multiplies the value by $k!$, so retain the coefficients at high orders.
