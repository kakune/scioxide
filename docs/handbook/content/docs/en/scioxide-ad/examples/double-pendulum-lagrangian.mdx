In Lagrangian mechanics, writing the Lagrangian $L(\theta, \dot\theta) = T - V$ as a function of generalized coordinates $\theta$ and velocities $\dot\theta$ determines the equations of motion through the Euler–Lagrange equations.
The equations involve first and second partial derivatives of $L$; as degrees of freedom increase, manually differentiating and simplifying products of trigonometric functions becomes lengthy and error-prone.
With automatic differentiation (AD), writing one function computing $L$ yields those partial derivatives without finite-difference truncation error (rounding error remains).

This example obtains the double-pendulum equation-of-motion terms with `grad`, `hessian`, and `hvp` and compares them with hand-derived closed forms.
It then differentiates the same Lagrangian on a reused tape to assemble the equations, integrates them with explicit Runge–Kutta methods, and checks total-energy conservation.

## Double-pendulum model

Suspend a point mass $m_1$ from a fixed point by a massless rod of length $l_1$, then suspend a mass $m_2$ from it by a rod of length $l_2$.
Let $\theta_1, \theta_2$ be the angles each rod makes with the downward vertical, positive counterclockwise; the mass positions are $x_1 = l_1\sin\theta_1$, $y_1 = -l_1\cos\theta_1$, $x_2 = x_1 + l_2\sin\theta_2$, and $y_2 = y_1 - l_2\cos\theta_2$.
Differentiating these in time and summing squared velocities gives the following kinetic energy $T$ and potential energy $V$ relative to the fixed-point height.

$$
T = \tfrac12\,\dot\theta^{\mathsf T} M(\theta)\,\dot\theta,\quad
M(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} (m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2 & m_2 l_1 l_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) \\ m_2 l_1 l_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) & m_2 l_2^2 \end{bmatrix},\quad
V = -(m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2\,g\,l_2\cos\theta_2
$$

$M(\theta)$ is the mass matrix; since $\det M = m_2 l_1^2 l_2^2\,\bigl(m_1 + m_2\sin^2(\theta_1-\theta_2)\bigr) > 0$, it is positive definite for every $\theta$.

The numerical example fixes $m_1 = 1$ kg, $l_1 = 1$ m, and $g = 9.80665$ m/s², with controls for mass ratio $\mu = m_2/m_1$ and length ratio $\lambda = l_2/l_1$.
Energy has units J, generalized forces such as $\partial L/\partial\theta$ have units N·m, and the mass matrix has units kg·m².

## Equations of motion as derivatives of the Lagrangian

Since $\partial L/\partial\dot\theta$ depends on $\theta$ and $\dot\theta$, the chain rule expands the first term of the Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} = 0$ into $\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial\dot\theta^2}\ddot\theta + \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial\dot\theta\,\partial\theta}\dot\theta$.
Here $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot\theta\,\partial\theta$ is the matrix whose $(i, k)$ entry is $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot\theta_i\partial\theta_k$.
Since $T$ is a quadratic form in $\dot\theta$ and $V$ is independent of $\dot\theta$, $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot\theta^2 = M(\theta)$ holds.
Furthermore, $\partial L/\partial\dot\theta = M(\theta)\dot\theta$ gives $\bigl(\partial^2 L/\partial\dot\theta\,\partial\theta\bigr)\dot\theta = \sum_k (\partial M/\partial\theta_k)\,\dot\theta_k\,\dot\theta = \dot M\dot\theta$, so the equations of motion are

$$
M(\theta)\,\ddot\theta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} - \dot M\dot\theta
$$


Both $M$ on the left and the terms on the right are first or second partial derivatives of $L$; since $M$ is positive definite, this linear system has a unique solution for $\ddot\theta$.

Derive each term by hand for an independent check.
Setting $\Delta = \theta_1 - \theta_2$ and $k = m_2 l_1 l_2$ gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} -k\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\sin\Delta - (m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1\sin\theta_1 \\ k\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\sin\Delta - m_2\,g\,l_2\sin\theta_2 \end{bmatrix},\qquad
\dot M\dot\theta = -k\sin\Delta\,(\dot\theta_1-\dot\theta_2)\begin{bmatrix} \dot\theta_2 \\ \dot\theta_1 \end{bmatrix}
$$


Substitute these and multiply by the inverse of $M$ for angular accelerations, then compare with closed forms sharing denominator $l_i(2m_1 + m_2 - m_2\cos 2\Delta)$.

## Roles and computational costs of the differentiation APIs

Use four inputs $z = [\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot\theta_1, \dot\theta_2]$ and write a scalar $L(z)$ function once for `R: Real`.
`Real` is a trait shared by numeric types such as `f64`, forward-mode `Dual`, and reverse-mode `Var`; the same function is evaluated and differentiated with the caller's chosen numeric type ([Numeric types, lanes, and generic code](../../algorithms/number-types-and-lanes/)).
Apply the following one-shot functions to extract each equation-of-motion term.

| Equation-of-motion term | API | Used part of the result |
|---|---|---|
| $\partial L/\partial\theta$ | `grad` | First two entries of $\nabla_z L$ |
| $M(\theta) = \partial^2 L/\partial\dot\theta^2$ | `hessian` | Bottom-right $2\times 2$ block of the row-major $4\times 4$ matrix $H = \nabla_z^2 L$ |
| $\dot M\dot\theta = (\partial^2 L/\partial\dot\theta\,\partial\theta)\,\dot\theta$ | `hvp` (direction $u = [\dot\theta; 0]$) | Last two entries of $Hu$ |

`grad` records evaluation of $L$ on a new tape (a sequence recording operations) and returns the gradient with one backward sweep from the output adjoint $\bar L = 1$.
The gradient of a scalar output costs a constant multiple of evaluating $L$, independently of input dimension ([Reverse mode and tapes](../../algorithms/reverse-mode-and-replay/)).

`hvp` returns a Hessian–vector product (HVP) $Hu$.
Record inputs $\mathrm{Dual}(z_i, u_i)$ on `Var<Dual<f64>>` and perform one reverse sweep: the adjoints' value parts give the gradient and derivative parts give $Hu$ (forward-over-reverse).
`hessian` repeats this recording and sweep for each unit direction on one tape, so a dense Hessian with $n$ inputs needs $n$ recordings ([Second derivatives and Hessian operators](../../algorithms/second-order-derivatives/)).
The first recording on a new tape executes the function once more to count statements, giving $n + 1$ function executions.
$M$ needs all $2\times 2$ entries in the $\dot\theta$ directions, so assemble Hessian columns; $\dot M\dot\theta$ is a single vector, requiring only one HVP.

All three functions create a new tape per call and allocate and return results as a `Vector` or `Matrix` from `scioxide_linalg`.
When differentiating the same $L$ at many points, as in time integration, create the tape once and reuse it ([Obtaining a gradient and three HVPs in one sweep](#obtaining-a-gradient-and-three-hvps-in-one-sweep)).

The following block defines the model shared by the two cells.
`Pendulum` holds masses and lengths, and `lagrangian` corresponds to $L(z) = T - V$.
Place `f64` coefficients to the right of operators so the same expression works with `f64`, `Var<f64>`, `Var<Dual<f64>>`, and the second cell's `Var<MultiDual<f64, 3>>`.

```rust
//| id: ad-pendulum-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Double-pendulum Lagrangian"
//| crates: [scioxide-ad]
use scioxide_ad::Real;

/// Standard gravitational acceleration g [m/s²].
pub(crate) const G: f64 = 9.80665;

/// Masses m₁, m₂ [kg] and rod lengths l₁, l₂ [m].
#[derive(Clone, Copy)]
pub(crate) struct Pendulum {
    pub(crate) m1: f64,
    pub(crate) m2: f64,
    pub(crate) l1: f64,
    pub(crate) l2: f64,
}

/// L(z) = T − V. z = [θ₁, θ₂, θ̇₁, θ̇₂] (rad, rad/s), with value in J.
pub(crate) fn lagrangian<R: Real>(z: &[R], p: &Pendulum) -> R {
    let (t1, t2, w1, w2) = (z[0], z[1], z[2], z[3]);
    let coupling = p.m2 * p.l1 * p.l2;
    // T = ½(m₁ + m₂)l₁²θ̇₁² + ½m₂l₂²θ̇₂² + m₂l₁l₂ cos(θ₁ − θ₂) θ̇₁θ̇₂
    let kinetic = w1 * w1 * (0.5 * (p.m1 + p.m2) * p.l1 * p.l1)
        + w2 * w2 * (0.5 * p.m2 * p.l2 * p.l2)
        + w1 * w2 * (t1 - t2).cos() * coupling;
    // −V = (m₁ + m₂) g l₁ cos θ₁ + m₂ g l₂ cos θ₂
    let minus_potential = t1.cos() * ((p.m1 + p.m2) * G * p.l1) + t2.cos() * (p.m2 * G * p.l2);
    kinetic + minus_potential
}
```

## Comparing equation-of-motion terms

The following cell takes mass ratio $\mu$, length ratio $\lambda$, and angles $\theta_1, \theta_2$ (from $-180°$ to $180°$), obtains $M$, $\partial L/\partial\theta$, and $\dot M\dot\theta$ with the three APIs above, and solves $M\ddot\theta = \partial L/\partial\theta - \dot M\dot\theta$ with Cramer's rule for $\ddot\theta$.
At $\dot\theta = 0$, $\partial T/\partial\theta$ and $\dot M\dot\theta$ vanish and the velocity-dependent terms cannot be checked, so fix angular velocities at $\dot\theta = (1, -1)$ rad/s.

The table shows each component's AD value, the closed form derived above, and their difference.
The closed forms are independent checks computed separately within the cell with `f64` trigonometric functions, bypassing AD recording.

```rust

//|   - {"id":"aligned","label":"θ₁ = θ₂ = 30°","description":"With sin Δ = 0, Ṁθ̇ vanishes and M₁₂ is maximal.","inputs":{"theta1_deg":30,"theta2_deg":30}}

//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/8/2","approx":{"value":-3.46914,"atol":1e-4}}
//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/7/4","range":{"min":-1e-13,"max":1e-13}}

//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/1/2","approx":{"value":-1.0037587636,"atol":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/7/4","range":{"min":-1e-13,"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/8/4","range":{"min":-1e-13,"max":1e-13}}
//|   - case: aligned

//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/1/2","approx":{"value":1.0,"atol":1e-14}}
//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/5/2","approx":{"value":0.0,"atol":1e-14}}
//|       - {"artifact":"terms","path":"/rows/6/2","approx":{"value":0.0,"atol":1e-14}}
use scioxide_ad::{grad, hessian, hvp};
use scioxide_core::RealScalar;

let p = Pendulum {
    m1: 1.0,
    m2: mass_ratio,
    l1: 1.0,
    l2: length_ratio,
};
// Angular velocities θ̇ = (1, −1) rad/s keep velocity-dependent terms nonzero.
let (w1, w2) = (1.0, -1.0);
let (t1, t2) = (
    RealScalar::to_radians(theta1_deg),
    RealScalar::to_radians(theta2_deg),
);
let z = [t1, t2, w1, w2];
let g = grad(|x| Ok(lagrangian(x, &p)), &z).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let h = hessian(|x| Ok(lagrangian(x, &p)), &z).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let u = [w1, w2, 0.0, 0.0];
let hu = hvp(|x| Ok(lagrangian(x, &p)), &z, &u).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let (g, h, hu) = (g.as_slice(), h.as_slice(), hu.as_slice());
// M is the bottom-right block of row-major 4×4 H; Ṁθ̇ is the last two entries of H·[θ̇; 0].
let mass = [h[10], h[11], h[15]];
let mdot = [hu[2], hu[3]];
let force = [g[0] - mdot[0], g[1] - mdot[1]];
// Solve M θ̈ = ∂L/∂θ − Ṁθ̇ with Cramer's rule (M is positive definite).
let det = mass[0] * mass[2] - mass[1] * mass[1];
let acc = [
    (mass[2] * force[0] - mass[1] * force[1]) / det,
    (mass[0] * force[1] - mass[1] * force[0]) / det,
];

// Independent check: hand-derived closed forms (f64 sin and cos only compute reference values).
let (s, c) = ((t1 - t2).sin(), (t1 - t2).cos());

let k = m2 * l1 * l2;
let den = 2.0 * m1 + m2 - m2 * (2.0 * (t1 - t2)).cos();
let closed = [
    (m1 + m2) * l1 * l1,
    k * c,
    m2 * l2 * l2,
    -k * w1 * w2 * s - (m1 + m2) * G * l1 * t1.sin(),
    k * w1 * w2 * s - m2 * G * l2 * t2.sin(),
    -k * s * (w1 - w2) * w2,
    -k * s * (w1 - w2) * w1,
    (-G * (2.0 * m1 + m2) * t1.sin()
        - m2 * G * (t1 - 2.0 * t2).sin()
        - 2.0 * s * m2 * (w2 * w2 * l2 + w1 * w1 * l1 * c))
        / (l1 * den),
    2.0 * s * (w1 * w1 * l1 * (m1 + m2) + G * (m1 + m2) * t1.cos() + w2 * w2 * l2 * m2 * c)
        / (l2 * den),

let ad = [
    mass[0], mass[1], mass[2], g[0], g[1], mdot[0], mdot[1], acc[0], acc[1],
];
let names = [
    ("M₁₁", "kg·m²"),
    ("M₁₂", "kg·m²"),
    ("M₂₂", "kg·m²"),
    ("∂L/∂θ₁", "N·m"),
    ("∂L/∂θ₂", "N·m"),
    ("(Ṁθ̇)₁", "N·m"),
    ("(Ṁθ̇)₂", "N·m"),
    ("θ̈₁", "rad/s²"),
    ("θ̈₂", "rad/s²"),
];
let rows: Vec<(&str, &str, f64, f64, f64)> = names
    .iter()
    .zip(ad.iter().zip(&closed))
    .map(|(&(name, unit), (&a, &b))| (name, unit, a, b, a - b))
    .collect();
let digits = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 12 });
let sci2 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Component" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Unit" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "AD", "type": "number", "format": digits },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Closed form", "type": "number", "format": digits },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Difference (AD − closed form)", "type": "number", "format": sci2 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "terms" })
);
```

At the default inputs ($\mu = \lambda = 1$, $\theta = (120°, -30°)$), all seven component differences for $M$, $\partial L/\partial\theta$, and $\dot M\dot\theta$ are 0, so AD and the closed forms agree bit for bit.
The differences in $\ddot\theta = (-10.2450, -3.4691)$ rad/s² are $1.8\times 10^{-15}$ and $2.2\times 10^{-15}$ rad/s².
These are rounding differences from the different operation orders of the AD-side $2\times 2$ linear solve and the closed forms, remaining within a few times the value magnitude multiplied by double-precision unit roundoff (about $1.1\times 10^{-16}$).

With $\mu = 3.5$, $\lambda = 0.5$, and $\theta = (45°, 170°)$, $M_{12} = -1.00376$ kg·m² is negative and differences in $\ddot\theta = (-10.4028, -16.9777)$ rad/s² are at most $7.1\times 10^{-15}$ rad/s².
At $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 30°$, $\sin\Delta = 0$, so $\dot M\dot\theta$ becomes 0 and $M_{12}$ takes its maximum $k = m_2 l_1 l_2 = 1$ kg·m².
For the tested inputs, differences remain at about $10^{-15}$ times the magnitude of $\ddot\theta$ or below, without the step-dependent truncation error of finite differences.

## Checking total-energy conservation

Since $L$ contains no explicit time dependence, solutions of the correct equations conserve total energy $E = T + V$.
Integrate the AD-assembled right-hand side $f(y) = [\dot\theta, \ddot\theta]$ ($y = [\theta, \dot\theta]$) with explicit Runge–Kutta methods, and measure $E(t) - E(0)$ with manually implemented closed forms of $T$ and $V$.

The relationship between method order $p$ and energy deviation is as follows.
An order-$p$ method has one-step local error $O(h^{p+1})$ and global error $O(h^p)$ over a fixed interval $[0, t_{\mathrm{end}}]$.
The coefficient increases with $t_{\mathrm{end}}$ and can grow exponentially for chaotic motion.
Since $E$ is a smooth function of the state, $|E(y_n) - E(0)| = |E(y_n) - E(y(t_n))|$ is also bounded by $O(h^p)$.
Halving $h$ reduces the deviation by about a factor of $2^{-p}$, so define the observed order as $\log_2\bigl(\max|E - E_0|_{h} / \max|E - E_0|_{h/2}\bigr)$.

For small-amplitude motion close to harmonic oscillation, phase error may dominate and energy deviation can decrease one order faster, as $O(h^{p+1})$.
For $y' = i\omega y$, the amplification factor of the $p = 2, 4$ method is $|R(i\omega h)|^2 = 1 + O((\omega h)^{p+2})$, giving a one-step energy change $O(h^{p+2})$ and $O(h^{p+1})$ over $t_{\mathrm{end}}/h$ steps.

If an equation-of-motion term is wrong, the exact solution of the incorrect differential equation generally does not conserve energy, so the deviation does not approach 0 even as $h \to 0$.
To expose this difference, the cell includes a switch that omits the $\dot M\dot\theta$ term during integration.

### Obtaining a gradient and three HVPs in one sweep

One right-hand-side evaluation needs four quantities: $\partial L/\partial\theta$, two columns of $M$, and $\dot M\dot\theta$.
Calling `grad`, `hessian`, and `hvp` as in the previous section creates three new tapes per evaluation and performs six recordings and reverse sweeps (one each for `grad` and `hvp`, and four directions for `hessian`).
All three tapes are new, so their first recording executes the function twice; total executions are nine (two each for `grad` and `hvp`, five for `hessian`).

This cell creates `Tape<MultiDual<f64, 3>>` once with tape value type `MultiDual<f64, 3>` (a value and three tangents), then calls `Tape::gradient_into` at each point.
Record inputs $z_i$ seeded in three directions $(e_3)_i$, $(e_4)_i$, and $u_i$ ($u = [\dot\theta; 0]$); one reverse sweep then gives $\partial L/\partial z_i$ in each input adjoint's value and $(He_3)_i$, $(He_4)_i$, and $(Hu)_i$ in its three tangent components.
This is the same forward-over-reverse method by which `hvp` carries one direction in `Var<Dual<f64>>`, applied to three directions together.
Each statement value holds four `f64` values (two for `Var<Dual<f64>>`), requiring only one recording and sweep.

If the recording does not fit, `gradient_into` counts statements, expands the tape to that size, and executes the function again.
Only the first call therefore executes twice; subsequent calls on a tape that has recorded the same function allocate nothing ([Tape lifecycle and reuse](../../algorithms/tape-lifecycle/)).
One-shot functions create new tapes every time, so both executions and allocation recur on each call.
The function must be deterministic.

The following cell releases the double pendulum from rest at $\theta_1(0) = \theta_2(0) = \theta_0$ and integrates to $t_{\mathrm{end}} = 5$ s with Heun's method ($p = 2$) and classical Runge–Kutta ($p = 4$), using both steps $h$ and $h/2$.
Inputs are the mass ratio, length ratio, initial angle $\theta_0$ (5°–175°), step $h$ [s] (rounded to divide the 5 s interval evenly, with the actual value tabulated), and whether to omit $\dot M\dot\theta$.

Outputs are a table of each method's $\max|E - E_0|$, their ratio, and observed order, and a semilog graph of $|E(t) - E(0)|$ over time (solid for $h$, dashed for $h/2$).
Times with exactly zero deviation cannot be plotted on a logarithmic axis and are omitted; their count appears in the table.

```rust
let mut tape = Tape::<MultiDual<f64, 3>>::new(TapeOptions::new());
let cx = ExecutionContext::sequential();
// y = [θ, θ̇] → [θ̇, θ̈]. Three seed directions: e₃, e₄ (two columns of M), and u = [θ̇; 0] (Ṁθ̇).
let mut rhs = |y: &[f64; 4]| -> Result<[f64; 4], SciOxideError> {
    let seeds = |k: usize| {
        [
            f64::from(k == 2),
            f64::from(k == 3),
            if k < 2 { y[k + 2] } else { 0.0 },
        ]
    };
    let point: [MultiDual<f64, 3>; 4] =
        core::array::from_fn(|k| MultiDual::new(y[k], seeds(k)));
    let mut adjoint = [MultiDual::new(0.0, [0.0; 3]); 4];
    tape.gradient_into(
        |z: &[Var<'_, MultiDual<f64, 3>>]| Ok(lagrangian(z, &p)),
        &point,
        &mut adjoint,
        cx,
    )?;
    // adjoint[i] = (∂L/∂zᵢ, [(H e₃)ᵢ, (H e₄)ᵢ, (H u)ᵢ])
    let (row1, row2) = (adjoint[2].gradient(), adjoint[3].gradient());
    let mass = [row1[0], row1[1], row2[1]];
    let mdot = if drop_mdot {
        [0.0; 2]
    } else {
        [row1[2], row2[2]]
    };
    let force = [adjoint[0].value() - mdot[0], adjoint[1].value() - mdot[1]];
    let det = mass[0] * mass[2] - mass[1] * mass[1];
    Ok([
        y[2],
        y[3],
        (mass[2] * force[0] - mass[1] * force[1]) / det,
        (mass[0] * force[1] - mass[1] * force[0]) / det,
    ])
};
// Independent check: closed-form total energy E = T + V (bypasses AD; f64 cos supplies reference values).
let energy = |y: &[f64; 4]| {
    0.5 * (p.m1 + p.m2) * p.l1 * p.l1 * y[2] * y[2]
        + 0.5 * p.m2 * p.l2 * p.l2 * y[3] * y[3]
        + p.m2 * p.l1 * p.l2 * y[2] * y[3] * (y[0] - y[1]).cos()
        - (p.m1 + p.m2) * G * p.l1 * y[0].cos()
        - p.m2 * G * p.l2 * y[1].cos()
};
// Butcher tableaux for explicit Runge–Kutta methods (a holds the lower triangle, b the weights).
let heun: (&[&[f64]], &[f64]) = (&[&[], &[1.0]], &[0.5, 0.5]);
let rk4: (&[&[f64]], &[f64]) = (
    &[&[], &[0.5], &[0.0, 0.5], &[0.0, 0.0, 1.0]],
    &[1.0 / 6.0, 1.0 / 3.0, 1.0 / 3.0, 1.0 / 6.0],
);
let t_end = 5.0;
let n0 = (t_end / step).round() as usize;
let theta0 = RealScalar::to_radians(theta0_deg);
let y0 = [theta0, theta0, 0.0, 0.0];
let e0 = energy(&y0);
let (mut rows, mut series) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new());
for (name, order, (a, b)) in [("Heun's method", 2, heun), ("Classical Runge–Kutta", 4, rk4)] {
    let mut worst = [0.0_f64; 2];
    let mut zeros = 0;
    for (level, n) in [n0, 2 * n0].into_iter().enumerate() {
        let h = t_end / n as f64;
        let (mut y, mut points) = (y0, Vec::new());
        for i in 1..=n {
            let mut k = [[0.0; 4]; 4];
            for (s, row) in a.iter().enumerate() {
                let mut stage = y;
                for (&aij, kj) in row.iter().zip(&k) {
                    for c in 0..4 {
                        stage[c] += h * aij * kj[c];
                    }
                }
                k[s] = rhs(&stage).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
            }
            for (&bi, ki) in b.iter().zip(&k) {
                for c in 0..4 {
                    y[c] += h * bi * ki[c];
                }
            }
            let drift = (energy(&y) - e0).abs();
            worst[level] = worst[level].max(drift);
            if drift == 0.0 {
                zeros += 1;
            } else if i % (n / 250).max(1) == 0 {
                points.push([i as f64 * h, drift]);
            }
        }
        let style = if level == 0 { "solid" } else { "dashed" };
        series.push(serde_json::json!({
            "name": format!("{name}（h = {h:.5} s）"),
            "points": points,
            "style": { "lineType": style, "showSymbol": false }
        }));
    }
    let ratio = worst[0] / worst[1];
    rows.push((
        name,
        order,
        t_end / n0 as f64,
        worst[0],
        worst[1],
        ratio,
        RealScalar::log2(ratio),
        zeros,
    ));
}
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let fixed2 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Method" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Order p", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "h [s]", "type": "number", "format": { "significantDigits": 4 } },
    { "key": "3", "label": "max|E − E₀|（h）[J]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "max|E − E₀|（h/2）[J]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Ratio [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2 },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Observed order [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2 },
    { "key": "7", "label": "Times with zero deviation", "type": "integer" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "orders" })
);
emit_chart!(
    serde_json::json!({
        "kind": "line",
        "xLabel": "Time t [s]",
        "yLabel": "Energy deviation |E(t) − E(0)| [J]",
        "yType": "log",
        "dataZoom": true,
        "tooltip": true,
        "series": series
    }),
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "drift" })
);
```

At the default inputs ($\theta_0 = 120°$, $h = 0.005$ s), Heun's $\max|E - E_0|$ decreases from $9.80\times 10^{-4}$ J to $2.55\times 10^{-4}$ J, with observed order 1.94.
Classical Runge–Kutta decreases from $4.44\times 10^{-7}$ J to $2.96\times 10^{-8}$ J, with order 3.91.
Both are close to the theoretical orders 2 and 4; energy deviation of the AD-assembled equations' solution approaches 0 as the step decreases.
Deviation in the graph does not grow monotonically: it fluctuates strongly, reaches near its maximum within about the first 1 s, and remains at the same order of magnitude afterward.
Local error is proportional to higher time derivatives of the solution, so deviations change sharply where the motion changes rapidly.

Omitting $\dot M\dot\theta$ gives $\max|E - E_0|$ of about $2.2\times 10^2$ J for both methods and both steps, with ratio 1.00 and observed order nearly 0 ($-0.001$).
A large amount of energy is injected compared with the initial $E_0 = 14.71$ J; this deviation comes from incorrect equations, rather than integration error.
At $t = 0.02$ s, the deviation is $2.2\times 10^{-7}$ J (Heun), almost the same as for correct equations.
At the start, $\dot M\dot\theta$, which is quadratic in $\dot\theta$, is small, so the omission does not appear immediately.

## Behavior when parameters change

At $h = 0.02$ s, observed orders are 3.16 for Heun and 5.02 for classical Runge–Kutta, departing from theoretical orders.
At this step, error terms beyond the leading term are still large; observed order reflects theoretical order only when the leading term dominates at both $h$ and $h/2$ (1.97 and 3.92 at $h = 0.0025$ s).

With initial angle $\theta_0 = 10°$ and step $h = 0.01$ s, the orders are 3.00 and 5.01, agreeing with $p + 1$.
At small amplitudes the motion is approximately a superposition of two normal modes, corresponding to the phase-error-dominated case above.
Returning to the default step $h = 0.005$ s and setting $\theta_0 = 5°$ reduces classical Runge–Kutta's $\max|E - E_0|$ at $h/2$ to $9.4\times 10^{-13}$ J, with four times having exactly zero deviation.
For large $\theta_0$ such as $120°$ or $170°$, the motion becomes chaotic and orders approach $p$.

With mass ratio $\mu = 5$, even the same $h = 0.005$ s step increases Heun's deviation to $0.749$ J and classical Runge–Kutta's to $9.77\times 10^{-3}$ J; the latter's order 4.83 is before the asymptotic regime.
A length ratio of $\lambda = 0.5$ also increases the lower pendulum's natural angular frequency $\sqrt{g/l_2}$, raising deviations to 0.125 J for Heun and $2.29\times 10^{-4}$ J for classical Runge–Kutta.
In both cases, the first cell's AD–closed-form differences remain about $10^{-15}$ times the magnitude of $\ddot\theta$; the accuracy of equation terms and integration difficulty are separate issues.

## Summary

Write $L$ once as a generic `R: Real` function to obtain equation-of-motion terms $M(\theta)$, $\partial L/\partial\theta$, and $\dot M\dot\theta$ with `hessian`, `grad`, and `hvp`; their closed-form differences remain at the rounding-error level.
Assemble Hessian columns for quantities needing all entries, such as $M$, and use one HVP for quantities requiring a single vector, such as $\dot M\dot\theta$.
For differentiating the same $L$ at many points, create a tape once, such as `Tape<MultiDual<f64, 3>>`, and obtain the gradient and several HVPs together with one `gradient_into` recording and sweep.
Check equations by computing observed order from energy-deviation ratios when halving the step; a missing term leaves a deviation that does not approach 0 regardless of $h$.
