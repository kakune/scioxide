If an ordinary differential equation (ODE) solution has a power-series expansion around $t = 0$, its coefficients reveal both solution values and the range where the series is usable.
The largest radius within which a power series converges is its radius of convergence.
This equals the distance from the expansion center to the solution's nearest singularity in the complex plane.
Taylor-series ODE solvers compute solution coefficients at each step and advance with a step sufficiently smaller than the convergence radius.
This example obtains coefficients one order at a time using `scioxide-ad`'s Taylor-jet `Jet` arithmetic, checking them against exact coefficients and examining convergence-radius estimates from them.

## Three initial-value problems and exact coefficients

Write the solution as $y(t) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n t^n$, with normalized Taylor coefficients $a_n = y^{(n)}(0)/n!$.
Consider three autonomous systems $y' = f(y)$.

- The solution of $y' = y^2$ with $y(0) = 1$ is $y = 1/(1 - t)$, with every coefficient $a_n = 1$.
  The pole at $t = 1$ gives convergence radius $R = 1$.
- The solution of $y' = 1 + y^2$ with $y(0) = 0$ is $y = \tan t$, with poles $t = \pm\pi/2$ giving $R = \pi/2$.
- The solution of $y' = 1 - y^2$ with $y(0) = 0$ is $y = \tanh t$.
  Although $\tanh t$ is bounded and smooth on the real axis, its imaginary-axis poles $t = \pm i\pi/2$ again give convergence radius $R = \pi/2$.

Since $\tan t$ and $\tanh t$ are odd functions, their even coefficients are 0 and their odd coefficients are

$$
a_{2m-1}^{\tan} = \frac{T_{2m-1}}{(2m-1)!}, \qquad
a_{2m-1}^{\tanh} = (-1)^{m-1}\,\frac{T_{2m-1}}{(2m-1)!}, \qquad
a_1^{\tan}, a_3^{\tan}, a_5^{\tan}, a_7^{\tan}, \ldots = 1, \tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{2}{15}, \tfrac{17}{315}, \ldots
$$


$T_{2m-1} = 1, 2, 16, 272, 7936, \ldots$ are tangent numbers, expressible with Bernoulli numbers $B_{2m}$ as $T_{2m-1} = (-1)^{m-1} 2^{2m}(2^{2m} - 1) B_{2m}/(2m)$.
Knuth and Buckholtz's integer recurrence computes tangent numbers, providing a check independent of the ODE.
The recurrence uses only sums and products with nonnegative coefficients, so even in `f64` its relative error remains at the rounding scale proportional to operation count.

Expanding the partial-fraction representation $\tan t = \sum_{k \ge 1} 8t / \bigl((2k - 1)^2\pi^2 - 4t^2\bigr)$ of $\tan t$ in $t$ gives

$$
|a_{2m-1}| = 2 \left( \frac{2}{\pi} \right)^{2m} \lambda(2m), \qquad
\lambda(s) = \sum_{k = 1, 3, 5, \ldots} k^{-s} = 1 + 3^{-s} + 5^{-s} + \cdots
$$


Since $\lambda(s) \to 1$, odd coefficients decrease approximately geometrically as $|a_n| \approx (4/\pi) R^{-n}$.

## A recurrence determining one order at a time

Comparing coefficients of $t^k$ on both sides of $y' = f(y)$ gives

$$
(k + 1)\,a_{k+1} = [f(y)]_k
$$


Here $[u]_k$ denotes the coefficient of order $k$ in series $u(t)$.
Products of Taylor coefficients use the Cauchy product $[uv]_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} u_j v_{k-j}$, while sums and constant multiplication also act order by order, so $[f(y)]_k$ depends only on $a_0, \ldots, a_k$.
Passing a series with known $a_0, \ldots, a_k$ and higher coefficients set to 0 through $f$ therefore gives the correct order-$k$ coefficient, determining $a_{k+1}$.
Repeating for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, K - 1$ obtains $a_1, \ldots, a_K$.

`Jet<V, N>` is a numeric type holding normalized Taylor coefficients $c_0, \ldots, c_{N-1}$ of a univariate function; `Real` operations return composed-series coefficients through order $N - 1$ ([Taylor jets and higher-order coefficients](../../algorithms/taylor-jets/)).
`Jet::new(coefficients)` constructs a series from its coefficient array, and `coefficients()` returns that array.
Write the right-hand side $f$ once for `R: Real` and pass `Jet::<f64, N>::new(a)` to obtain $[f(y)]_0, \ldots, [f(y)]_{N-1}$.
The $N$ of `Jet` is a type constant (a const generic), so a runtime input order $K$ cannot change the type.
Here fix $N = 41$ to match the input upper bound $K = 40$; $K$ only changes the loop count.
Coefficients above $k$ are placeholders set to 0 and do not affect $[f(y)]_k$.

One nonlinear operation requires $O(N^2)$ arithmetic operations, so $K$ recurrence steps cost $O(KN^2)$.
For a specific $f$ such as $y^2$, manually implementing recurrence $(k + 1)a_{k+1} = \sum_j a_j a_{k-j}$ reduces total cost to $O(K^2)$, but jets apply the same procedure to any $f$ expressible with `Real` operations.
`Jet` guarantees accuracy through order 8: where arguments and coefficients are 0 or have magnitudes in `[√MIN_POSITIVE, √MAX]`, coefficient $c_k$ has absolute error at most $64\varepsilon(k + 1)g$ plus the smallest subnormal number ($g$ is the sum of absolute terms in the exact composition).
No bound is supplied for higher orders.
Coefficients of order 9 and above in this example are checked against exact coefficients.
`scioxide-ad` does not itself provide a Taylor-series ODE solver; for integration with step control, use [scioxide-ode](../../../scioxide-ode/) solvers.

The following block defines the model shared by the three cells.
`Equation` represents the three initial-value problems; `rhs` is the right-hand side $f$, `initial` gives $y(0)$, `radius` the convergence radius $R$, `solution` the exact solution, and `exact_coefficients` exact coefficients from tangent numbers.
`solution` computes reference values with `f64` standard functions, while `exact_coefficients` obtains coefficients without automatic differentiation.

```rust
//| id: ad-taylor-ode-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Right-hand sides, exact solutions, and exact coefficients of three initial-value problems"
//| crates: [scioxide-ad]
use scioxide_ad::Real;

/// Initial-value problem y′ = f(y), y(0) = y₀.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub(crate) enum Equation {
    /// y′ = y², y(0) = 1. Solution 1/(1 − t).
    Pole,
    /// y′ = 1 + y², y(0) = 0. Solution tan t.
    Tangent,
    /// y′ = 1 − y², y(0) = 0. Solution tanh t.
    Tanh,
}

impl Equation {
    pub(crate) fn from_label(label: &str) -> Result<Self, String> {
        match label {
            "y′ = y², y(0) = 1" => Ok(Self::Pole),
            "y′ = 1 + y², y(0) = 0" => Ok(Self::Tangent),
            "y′ = 1 − y², y(0) = 0" => Ok(Self::Tanh),
            _ => Err(format!("Unknown initial-value problem: {label}")),
        }
    }

    pub(crate) fn label(self) -> &'static str {
        match self {
            Self::Pole => "y′ = y², y(0) = 1",
            Self::Tangent => "y′ = 1 + y², y(0) = 0",
            Self::Tanh => "y′ = 1 − y², y(0) = 0",
        }
    }

    /// Right-hand side f(y). Written once for numeric type R.
    pub(crate) fn rhs<R: Real>(self, y: R) -> R {
        match self {
            Self::Pole => y * y,
            Self::Tangent => y * y + 1.0,
            Self::Tanh => -(y * y) + 1.0,
        }
    }

    pub(crate) fn initial(self) -> f64 {
        if self == Self::Pole { 1.0 } else { 0.0 }
    }

    /// Convergence radius R (distance to the nearest singularity).
    pub(crate) fn radius(self) -> f64 {
        if self == Self::Pole {
            1.0
        } else {
            std::f64::consts::FRAC_PI_2
        }
    }

    /// Exact solution y(t). Reference value (f64 standard functions).
    pub(crate) fn solution(self, t: f64) -> f64 {
        match self {
            Self::Pole => 1.0 / (1.0 - t),
            Self::Tangent => t.tan(),
            Self::Tanh => t.tanh(),
        }
    }

    /// Exact coefficients a_0, …, a_order. Compute tangent numbers T_{2m−1} with the Knuth–Buckholtz recurrence.
    pub(crate) fn exact_coefficients(self, order: usize) -> Vec<f64> {
        if self == Self::Pole {
            return vec![1.0; order + 1];
        }
        let count = order / 2 + 1;
        let mut tangent = vec![0.0_f64; count + 1];
        tangent[1] = 1.0;
        for k in 2..=count {
            tangent[k] = (k - 1) as f64 * tangent[k - 1];
        }
        for k in 2..=count {
            for j in k..=count {
                tangent[j] = (j - k) as f64 * tangent[j - 1] + (j - k + 2) as f64 * tangent[j];
            }
        }
        let mut coefficients = vec![0.0; order + 1];
        let mut factorial = 1.0;
        for n in 1..=order {
            factorial *= n as f64;
            if n % 2 == 1 {
                let m = (n + 1) / 2;
                let sign = if self == Self::Tanh && m % 2 == 0 {
                    -1.0
                } else {
                    1.0
                };
                coefficients[n] = sign * tangent[m] / factorial;
            }
        }
        coefficients
    }
}
```

At the defaults ($y' = 1 + y^2$, $K = 25$), even coefficients are exactly 0 and odd coefficients agree with exact tangent-number coefficients to relative error at most $2.3 \times 10^{-16}$.
Even at $K = 40$, relative differences remain at most $6.0 \times 10^{-16}$; errors barely grow with order, including orders 9 and above outside `Jet`'s accuracy guarantee.
The Cauchy product $\sum_j a_j a_{k-j}$ on the right-hand side of $\tan t$ sums same-sign terms and suffers no cancellation.
In the graph, $|a_n|$ decreases nearly parallel to dashed $R^{-n}$, at rate $(2/\pi)^n$.
The ratio of $|a_n|$ to the dashed line is nearly constant at $4/\pi \approx 1.27$; the common ratio of the approximately geometric decay represents $1/R$.

Switching to $y' = 1 - y^2$ alternates coefficient signs as $+, -, +, \ldots$, while magnitudes match $\tan t$ and relative errors are the same.
The sign of product $a_j a_{k-j}$ depends only on $k$, so this case also sums same-sign terms.
With $y' = y^2$ and $K = 40$, every coefficient is exactly 1 and errors are 0.
This is because the sum giving $[y^2]_k = k + 1$ is computed exactly as an integer sum; graph points lie on dashed $R^{-n} = 1$.

## Estimating the convergence radius

By the Cauchy–Hadamard formula $1/R = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |a_n|^{1/n}$, the root estimate $\hat{R}_{\mathrm{root}} = |a_n|^{-1/n}$ approaches $R$.
A ratio estimate $\hat{R}_{\mathrm{ratio}} = |a_p/a_n|^{1/(n-p)}$ from successive nonzero coefficients $a_p$ and $a_n$ ($p < n$) is also available.
For $1/(1 - t)$, both estimates return $R = 1$ from the start.

For $\tan t$ and $\tanh t$, the previous section's formula gives

$$
\hat{R}_{\mathrm{root}} = \frac{\pi}{2} \left( \frac{\pi}{4\lambda(n + 1)} \right)^{1/n}, \qquad
\hat{R}_{\mathrm{ratio}} = \frac{\pi}{2} \sqrt{\frac{\lambda(n - 1)}{\lambda(n + 1)}}
$$

where $n$ is odd and $p = n - 2$.
The root estimate retains factor $(\pi/4)^{1/n}$ even as $\lambda \to 1$, so relative error is about $\ln(4/\pi)/n \approx 0.24/n$ and decreases only inversely with $n$.
Since $\lambda(s) - 1 \approx 3^{-s}$, the ratio estimate's relative error is about $\tfrac{4}{9} \cdot 3^{-(n-1)}$, decreasing ninefold whenever order increases by two.

Ratio-estimate error decreases approximately geometrically with order when the nearest singularity is a simple pole, or a pair of simple poles aligning coefficient signs such as $\pm\pi/2$ for $\tan t$, with other singularities farther away.
For $\tan t$, the distance ratio $1/3$ to the next poles $\pm 3\pi/2$ appears in the error factor $3^{-(n-1)}$.
When the nearest singularities are a complex-conjugate pair $t = \rho e^{\pm i\theta}$ ($0 < \theta < \pi/2$) away from the real axis, coefficients oscillate like $\cos(n\theta)$ and adjacent coefficient ratios do not converge.
Even then, the limit superior of $|a_n|^{1/n}$ gives $1/R$, making the slower root estimate more robust.

## Partial-sum errors and step size

The convergence radius appears in the decay of partial-sum $S_n(h) = \sum_{j \le n} a_j h^j$ errors.
If $|a_n| \approx C R^{-n}$, term magnitudes are $C(h/R)^n$; for $h < R$ errors also decrease at rate $(h/R)^n$, while for $h \ge R$ terms do not decrease and partial sums do not converge.
$\tanh t$ is finite even at $h \ge \pi/2$, but its power series diverges because of complex-plane poles $\pm i\pi/2$.
For $1/(1 - t)$, the closed-form error $y(h) - S_K(h) = h^{K+1}/(1 - h)$ provides a check.

The following cell receives the initial-value problem and coefficients, selects the step-to-radius ratio $h/R$ (0.2–1.2), and computes partial sums at $h = (h/R) \cdot R$.

Reference $y(h)$ is the exact solution evaluated with `f64` standard functions; for $h \ge R$, no partial-sum comparison is made.

The horizontal line marks the rounding scale $\varepsilon |y(h)|$ of $y(h)$; once partial-sum error reaches this level, rounding determines further behavior.

## Summary

Obtain series coefficients for ODE $y' = f(y)$ one order at a time by writing its right-hand side for `R: Real`, passing a `Jet::new(a)` of known coefficients through it, and using $(k + 1)a_{k+1} = [f(y)]_k$.
The length $N$ of `Jet` is a type constant; choose the type for the maximum required order and leave uncomputed coefficients at 0, with cost $O(KN^2)$.
`Jet` guarantees accuracy only through order 8; verify higher orders with exact coefficients or another method.
In this example, relative differences through order 40 were at most $6 \times 10^{-16}$.
Estimating convergence radii from successive nonzero coefficient ratios converges rapidly; estimates from $|a_n|^{-1/n}$ approach only as $1/n$, but also work when coefficients oscillate.
Partial-sum errors decrease at rate $(h/R)^n$; even solutions smooth on the real axis, such as $\tanh t$, have series diverging for $h \ge R$ because of complex-plane singularities.
Choose a Taylor-series step so $h/R$ is sufficiently below 1 for the estimated $R$, balancing desired accuracy and order.
