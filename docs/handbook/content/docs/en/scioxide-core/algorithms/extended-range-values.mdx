Positive normal double-precision values range approximately from $2.2 \times 10^{-308}$ to $1.8 \times 10^{308}$; including subnormals lowers the minimum only to about $4.9 \times 10^{-324}$.
Large factorials, partition functions, and probability products commonly have results or intermediates outside this range.
`scioxide-core` provides types storing exponents separately as 64-bit integers, with type-specific rules for projection back to IEEE types.
[Floating-point numbers and condition numbers](../../../concepts/floating-point-and-conditioning/) explains IEEE 754 ranges and subnormals.

## Binary-normalized values `Scaled`

`Scaled<S>` (`S` is `f32` or `f64`) represents nonzero values

$$
x = m \cdot 2^{e}, \qquad 1 \le |m| < 2, \quad e \in \mathbb{Z},\ -2^{63} \le e < 2^{63}
$$


Significand $m$ has `S` precision; exponent $e$ is `i64`.
Signed zeros preserve their sign with exponent zero.

This representation computes multiplication and division separately for significands and exponents.

$$
(m_1 2^{e_1})(m_2 2^{e_2}) = (m_1 m_2)\, 2^{e_1 + e_2}, \qquad 1 \le |m_1 m_2| < 4
$$

A one-bit shift restores significand products to $[1, 2)$, so normalization needs only one shift.
Addition and subtraction first shift the smaller-exponent significand right by $e_1 - e_2$ bits.
When the exponent gap exceeds significand width by at least two bits, rounding loses the smaller term (absorption into the larger term).
All arithmetic rounds at significand precision independently of exponent magnitude.
No IEEE overflow or underflow occurs before projection; range failures occur only when the exponent exceeds `i64`.

There are three construction methods.

- `from_value(x)` exactly decomposes finite IEEE values, including subnormals and signed zeros.
- `from_parts(x, e)` constructs $x \cdot 2^e$.
- `from_log(sign, ℓ)` constructs $\mathrm{sign} \cdot e^{\ell}$ from sign $\pm 1$ and logarithm $\ell$.
  Error is within 4 ULP when the result lies in the normal range of `S`.

`add`, `sub`, `mul`, `div`, `square`, and `sqrt` compute in extended range, rounding the result significand once to `S` precision.
`shifted(k)` returns the value multiplied exactly by $2^k$.
`difference_of_products(b, c, d)` rounds the exact value of $x b - c d$ once.
Cancellation precedes exponent-range checking, so intermediate products alone exceeding `i64` do not cause failure.
Without projection, `ln_abs()` returns $\ln |x|$ as a `S` value with absolute error at most $16\,\varepsilon\,(1 + |\ln |x||)$ (not a relative error guarantee near 1).
Division by zero, `ln_abs` of zero, and `sqrt` of negatives produce `DomainError`.

All operations use fixed stack storage without heap allocation, including failure paths.
Since `Scaled` addition and subtraction round at significand precision, they do not provide exact-sum contracts like `RangeAccumulator`.

### Projection and range errors

There are two ways to return extended-range values to IEEE types.
`round()` and `round_to::<R>()` project with one round-to-nearest, ties-to-even operation, permitting gradual underflow to subnormals or signed zeros.
`try_value()` and `try_value_to::<R>()` instead reject nonzero values rounded to zero.
Merely rounding to a subnormal is not an error in either form.
Range failures have `kind()` equal to `NumericalInstability`, with three cases distinguished by `NumericalIssue` in detailed view `view()`.

| `NumericalIssue` | Situation |
|---|---|
| `Overflow` | Projected value exceeds the largest finite value |
| `Underflow` | A nonzero value rounds to zero through `try_value` |
| `ExponentOutOfRange` | Normalized exponent exceeds `i64` (separate from IEEE projection) |

The following cell computes $n!$ through `Scaled::mul` and compares $\ln n!$ obtained through `ln_abs()` with the Stirling series

$$
\ln n! \approx n \ln n - n + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi n) + \frac{1}{12 n} - \frac{1}{360 n^3}
$$


Series truncation error is of the order of the next term $1/(1260\, n^5)$ and at most $10^{-8}$ when $n \ge 10$.
Input is $n$ (10–500 in steps of 10).
The first table shows the significand and exponent of $n! = m \cdot 2^e$, both $\ln n!$ values, their difference, and the `ln_abs` error bound.
The second projects $n!$ and $1/n!$ through `round()` and `try_value()`, displaying failed `NumericalIssue` values as variant names.
At $n > 170$, $n!$ exceeds the largest double; at $n \ge 178$, $1/n!$ falls below half the smallest subnormal.

For projection, both `round()` and `try_value()` fail on $n!$ with `Overflow`.
For $1/n!$, `round()` returns zero while `try_value()` fails with `Underflow`.

Reducing to $n = 10$ makes `round()` of $n!$ return 3628800; the logarithm's difference $7.9 \times 10^{-9}$ from the Stirling series corresponds to series truncation error $1/(1260 \cdot 10^5)$.
At $n = 170$, both $170! \approx 7.257416 \times 10^{306}$ and $1/170! \approx 1.38 \times 10^{-307}$ fit double precision, and every projection succeeds.

## Compensated summation with `ScaledAccumulator`

Addition and subtraction of `Scaled` values can lose small terms added to large ones through significand rounding.
`ScaledAccumulator<S>` holds two `Scaled` values, a sum and a correction, using Neumaier compensation to retain lost low-order contributions.
If later terms cancel the sum, the correction contributes to the result.
On failure, `add_scaled`, `sub_scaled`, and `add_value` preserve both sum and correction.
`total()` adds sum and correction with one rounding to return `Scaled`; `round()` projects it to `S`.

Product method `add_product` adds rounded products, and the correction has only `S` precision.
The sum is therefore not exact; neither sign nor correct rounding is guaranteed.
Use `RangeAccumulator` when an exact sum is required.
Use `ScaledAccumulator` for low-storage compensated sums of values beyond double-precision range.

`absorbs(exponent)` tests whether adding any number of terms with normalized exponents at most `exponent` leaves the bits of both sum and correction unchanged.
It returns `true` only when both are nonzero and both exponents exceed `exponent` by more than significand width $+ 2$.
This checks absorption with extra margin for both sum and correction, supporting omission of entire series tails.
`false` does not imply absence of absorption.

The following cell computes $2^{e} + 1 - 2^{e}$ three ways.
Input is binary exponent $e$ (50–2000 in steps of 50); the mathematical result is 1 independently of $e$.
The table shows ordinary double computation, `Scaled` arithmetic, `ScaledAccumulator` results, and the final sum and correction held by `ScaledAccumulator`.
At $e > 1023$, $2^{e}$ itself cannot fit double precision; ordinary computation gives $\infty - \infty$, displayed as a missing value.

//|   - {"id":"beyond-f64","label":"e = 1100","description":"2^e is outside double range, so f64 evaluates ∞ − ∞.","inputs":{"large_exponent":1100}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"sums","path":"/rows/0/0","equals":0}
//|       - {"artifact":"sums","path":"/rows/0/1","equals":0}
//|       - {"artifact":"sums","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":1}
//|       - {"artifact":"sums","path":"/rows/0/4","equals":1}

//|       - {"artifact":"sums","path":"/rows/0/1","equals":1}

    .add(Scaled::ONE)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?
    .sub(big)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?

    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

// Compensated sum: the lost one remains in the correction.

compensated.add_scaled(big).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
compensated
    .add_scaled(Scaled::ONE)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

        "key": "0",
        "label": "f64 [-]",
        "type": "number",

    { "key": "2", "label": "round() from ScaledAccumulator [-]", "type": "number", "format": digits },

    { "key": "4", "label": "Final correction [-]", "type": "number", "format": digits }
]);

    columns,
    vec![(
        naive,
        direct,
        compensated.round().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,
        sum.round().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,

At the default $e = 500$, both f64 and `Scaled` addition/subtraction return zero.
`ScaledAccumulator` returns 1, with final sum zero and correction 1.
The one lost to rounding when added to $2^{500}$ remains in the correction and reappears after subtracting $2^{500}$.

At $e = 50$, $2^{50} + 1$ fits the 53-bit significand, so all three methods return 1 with zero correction.
At $e = 1100$, f64 computes $\infty - \infty$ and gives `NaN`, while `Scaled` and `ScaledAccumulator` match their results at $e = 500$.

## Per-component scales in `ScaledValue` and shared exponents in `ProjectiveValue`

Widely separated real and imaginary magnitudes cannot both be represented with one exponent.

- `ScaledValue<V>` (real or complex `V`) holds an independent `Scaled` per component.
  `from_value` preserves every bit of finite IEEE values, recoverable through `try_value`.
  For real values, the imaginary part is fixed at $+0$.
  Complex quotients have pre-projection relative error at most $\gamma_3 = 3u/(1-3u)$ per component (unit roundoff $u = \varepsilon/2$).
- `ProjectiveValue<V>` has coordinates $\mathbf{s} \cdot 2^{k}$ sharing one binary exponent, with largest component magnitude in $[1, 2)$.
  Constructed only through `ScaledValue::try_projective()`, rejecting conversion if aligning the shared exponent loses any nonzero significand bits.
  This representation defines no arithmetic.
- `ScaledValue::scale_exponent()` returns the largest nonzero component exponent; `scaled_magnitude()` returns a norm in units of $2^{k}$ for that exponent $k$.
  Use it for norm-based error estimates.

A `try_projective()` rejection has `kind()` equal to `NumericalInstability`, with two possible `NumericalIssue` reasons.
Lost low-order bits of a nonzero component produce `LossOfSignificance`; a nonzero component becoming zero produces `Underflow`.

The following cell constructs a complex value with real part $2^{d}$ and imaginary part $1/3$ (rounded to double) through `ScaledValue`, then tries `try_projective()`.
Input is real/imaginary exponent gap $d$ (0–1100 in steps of 50).
The shared exponent becomes $d$, reducing the imaginary significand to $2^{-d}/3$.
Since $1/3 \approx 1.33 \times 2^{-2}$, for $d \le 1020$ all 53 imaginary significand bits fit a normal double and round-trip unchanged.
At $d > 1020$, it becomes subnormal and loses low-order bits, so conversion is rejected.
The first table shows componentwise significands and exponents; the second shows conversion results, leaving shared-exponent fields empty on rejection and naming the `NumericalIssue` reason.

//|   - case: lost-bits

//|       - {"artifact":"projective","path":"/rows/0/3","equals":"LossOfSignificance"}

//|       - {"artifact":"projective","path":"/rows/0/0","equals":0}

At $d = 0$, the shared exponent is zero and the imaginary significand is precisely the double rounded from $1/3$.
For every $d$, componentwise-exponent `ScaledValue` retains both parts exactly.

## Logarithmic representation with `LogValue`

`LogValue<V, R>` represents nonzero values as $x = p\,e^{\ell}$ using unit phase $p$ (sign $\pm 1$ for reals, a unit complex number for complex values) and finite natural logarithm $\ell = \ln |x|$; zero is separate.
Phases have per-component exponents, preserving tiny nonzero components.

- `from_log(phase, log_magnitude)` rejects real phases other than $\pm 1$ and complex phase magnitudes differing from 1 by more than $16\varepsilon$ as `InvalidInput`.
- `from_value(x)` always constructs from finite IEEE values, but its logarithm is a rounded approximation.
  It is not an exact conversion like `ScaledValue::from_value`.
- `log_magnitude()` returns `None` only for zero.
  `LogValue::ZERO` represents empty or exactly cancelling sums without storing infinite logarithms.
- `round()` projects to IEEE through one scaling operation per component, without forming $e^{\ell}$ separately.
  Thus projection can succeed even when $\ell$ slightly exceeds the log of the largest double, provided each component fits.
  `try_value()` rejects nonzero components rounding to zero.

`LogValue` is returned by `logsumexp_value` and `weighted_logsumexp`, representing exactly cancelling signed weighted sums as exact zero.
[Logarithmic sums, weighted sums, and normalization](../logarithmic-reductions/) gives details.

## Value/derivative pairs `ValueAndDerivative`

`ValueAndDerivative<V, D>` pairs function value $f(x)$ with derivative $f'(x)$.
Construct with `new(value, derivative)`, borrow references with `value()` and `derivative()`, and extract a `(V, D)` pair with `into_parts()`.
Value and derivative types are independent, for example a `Scaled<f64>` value and `f64` derivative.
Construction performs no value validation; the type itself has no numerical contract.
Accuracy depends on the function returning the pair.

## Summary

Compute out-of-range double intermediates or results with `Scaled`, choosing `ln_abs()` for logarithms or `round()` / `try_value()` for projection according to the use.
Projection failure has `kind()` equal to `NumericalInstability`; distinguish the failing range stage through `Overflow`, `Underflow`, or `ExponentOutOfRange` in `view()`.
Use `RangeAccumulator` for exact sums of differently scaled values, or `ScaledAccumulator` when extended-range compensated sums suffice.
Retain widely separated complex component scales in `ScaledValue`; convert to shared-exponent `ProjectiveValue` only without information loss.
