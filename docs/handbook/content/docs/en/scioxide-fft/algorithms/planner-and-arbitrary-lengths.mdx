Computing the DFT directly from its definition requires $N^2$ complex multiplications.
The fast Fourier transform (FFT) uses twiddle-factor $W_N = e^{-2\pi i/N}$ periodicity $W_N^{k+N} = W_N^k$ and even-length symmetry $W_N^{k+N/2} = -W_N^k$ to decompose a length-$N$ transform into shorter transforms, computing it in $O(N\log N)$ operations.
This page explains the decomposition formulas, two reductions of prime lengths to convolution, the length-based algorithm selection in `scioxide-fft`, cost, and rounding error.
[FFT plans, workspaces, and caches](../plans-workspaces-and-providers/) covers reuse of selected plans, workspaces, and caches; [External FFT implementations and CPU instruction sets](../providers-and-cpu-backends/) covers external-implementation injection.

## Cooley–Tukey decomposition

### Radix-2 decimation in time

For $N = 2M$, split input into even-indexed $x_{2m}$ and odd-indexed $x_{2m+1}$ elements.

$$
X_k = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} x_{2m}\, W_M^{km} + W_N^k \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} x_{2m+1}\, W_M^{km} = E_k + W_N^k O_k
$$

$E_k$ and $O_k$ are length-$M$ DFTs with period $M$.
From $W_N^{k+M} = -W_N^k$, the second-half bins are obtained without extra multiplication as

$$
X_k = E_k + W_N^k O_k, \qquad X_{k+M} = E_k - W_N^k O_k \qquad (0 \le k < M)
$$

shown above.
This addition/subtraction pair is called a butterfly operation.
Counting twiddle multiplications including trivial cases gives $C(N) = 2C(N/2) + N/2$, and $N = 2^p$ requires $C(N) = \frac{N}{2}\log_2 N$ multiplications.

### Mixed radices

For $N = N_1 N_2$, split indices as $n = n_1 N_2 + n_2$, $k = k_2 N_1 + k_1$ ($0 \le n_1, k_1 < N_1$, $0 \le n_2, k_2 < N_2$), giving

$$
W_N^{nk} = W_{N_1}^{n_1 k_1}\, W_N^{n_2 k_1}\, W_{N_2}^{n_2 k_2}
$$

the expression above (the $W_N^{n_1 k_2 N_1 N_2} = 1$ term vanishes).
A length-$N$ transform splits into 3 stages: $N_2$ transforms of length $N_1$, multiplication by twiddle factors $W_N^{n_2 k_1}$, and $N_1$ transforms of length $N_2$.
Repeating this gives one stage for each factor $r_i$ of $N = r_1 r_2 \cdots r_s$.

`scioxide-fft` has small DFT stages of radix 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7.
It uses mixed radices only for $N = 2^a 3^b 5^c 7^d$ (lengths that are products of 2, 3, 5, and 7), with no stages for prime factors 11 or larger.
Powers of 2 use radix-4 stages, adding one radix-2 stage if the number of factors of 2 is odd.
For other lengths, dynamic programming compares candidate stage orders using a computational-cost model (operations per element, twiddle-table reads, and small-block counts), deterministically selecting the least costly order.
Equal costs prefer radix 4.
This model is a relative guide for ranking candidates, not an actual operation count.

## Prime-length transforms

A prime length $p$ cannot be split into stages.
Both methods below rewrite a length-$p$ DFT as a convolution computable by FFTs of another length.

### Rader's algorithm

The nonzero residues $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ modulo prime $p$ form a cyclic group of order $p-1$ and can be written using primitive root $g$ as $n = g^{-q}$, $k = g^{r}$ ($q, r = 0,\dots,p-2$).
Substitution gives $nk = g^{r-q}$, hence

$$
X_{g^{r}} = x_0 + \sum_{q=0}^{p-2} x_{g^{-q}}\, W_p^{g^{r-q}}
$$

the expression above, reducing to a length-$p-1$ circular convolution of $a_q = x_{g^{-q}}$ and $b_m = W_p^{g^m}$.
Sum $X_0 = \sum_n x_n$ separately.
During preparation, `scioxide-fft` stores the index permutation and FFT of $b$; execution computes convolution with 2 length-$p-1$ FFTs (the transform of $a$ and the inverse transform of the product) and an elementwise product.

### Bluestein's algorithm

Using identity $jk = \frac{1}{2}\left[j^2 + k^2 - (k-j)^2\right]$ and defining chirp $c_j = e^{-\pi i j^2/N}$ gives

$$
X_k = c_k \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left(x_j c_j\right) \overline{c_{k-j}}
$$

the expression above.
Thus a DFT of arbitrary length becomes the linear convolution of a chirp-weighted sequence and $\overline{c_m}$ ($-N < m < N$).
Computing linear convolution through circular convolution without wraparound requires length $L \ge 2N-1$.
`scioxide-fft` chooses $L$ as the smallest product of 2, 3, 5, and 7 at least $2N-1$, and stores the FFT of $\overline{c}$ during preparation.
Execution uses 2 FFTs of length $L$; the inverse reuses the same chirp through $\mathrm{IDFT}(x) = \overline{\mathrm{DFT}(\overline{x})}$.

Computing chirp phase $\pi j^2/N$ directly in floating point makes $j^2$ of order $N^2$, growing absolute phase error to order $\varepsilon N$ ($\varepsilon$ is machine epsilon).
`scioxide-fft` computes $j^2 \bmod 2N$ with integers before converting to an angle, giving each chirp rounding-scale error.
The chirp Z-transform extends this identity to contours outside the unit circle; see [Chirp Z-transform and zoom FFT](../chirp-z-and-zoom-fft/).

## Algorithm-selection rules

`FftPlan` selects algorithms by length using the following rules and reports them as `FftAlgorithmKind` through `diagnostics().algorithm()`.

| Length $N$ | Algorithm | `FftAlgorithmKind` |
|---|---|---|
| $N = 1$ | Identity | `Identity` |
| Product of 2, 3, 5, and 7 ($N > 1$) | Mixed-radix Cooley–Tukey | `MixedRadix` |
| Prime at most $2^{20}$, with $p-1$ a product of 2, 3, 5, and 7, and lower modeled cost than Bluestein | Rader | `Rader` |
| Otherwise (including composite lengths with prime factors 11 or larger) | Bluestein | `Bluestein` |
| Length accepted by an injected implementation | Provider implementation | `Provider` |

Rader–Bluestein comparisons use the same model as mixed radices to compare 2 FFTs of length $p-1$ with 2 of length $L$.
Using Rader for a prime whose $p-1$ is not a product of 2, 3, 5, and 7 requires another Rader or Bluestein transform inside the convolution.
`scioxide-fft` avoids this nesting and uses Bluestein in that case.
Composite lengths with prime factors 11 or larger (for example, $22 = 2\cdot 11$) also use Bluestein for the entire transform rather than creating a radix-11 stage.
The $2^{20}$ limit controls preparation work for trial-division primality testing and primitive-root search.

`diagnostics().stage_count()` returns the maximum number of FFT stages on the execution path.
For mixed radices this is the stage count; for Rader and Bluestein it is the inner convolution FFT's stage count plus 2 stages for phase products before and after it.
Plans with real layouts (`FftLayout::Real`, `Hermitian`) report the inner complex FFT algorithm.
For even lengths, the inner transform is a length-$N/2$ complex FFT, with one additional stage for real-spectrum assembly.
Odd lengths divisible by 3, 5, or 7 and greater than that factor (9, 15, 21, and so on) split the real data by that radix and also add one assembly stage.
Other odd lengths directly use a length-$N$ complex FFT and its stage count.

## Cost and rounding error

Every length costs $O(N\log N)$, but constants vary by algorithm.

- A mixed-radix stage combines a small DFT with a fixed per-element operation count for each radix and twiddle products.
  A radix-2 FFT uses approximately $5N\log_2 N$ real operations; radix-4, -5, and -7 stages process several factors in one stage, reducing the stage count.
- Rader uses 2 FFTs of length $p-1$, giving an estimated cost about twice a nearby mixed-radix transform.
- Bluestein uses 2 FFTs of length $L \ge 2N-1$ and $2N$ chirp products.
  Two FFTs of length approximately $2N$ require slightly more than 4 times the operations of a length-$N$ FFT, giving an estimated cost at least 4 times a nearby mixed-radix transform.
  Since $L$ is the smallest product of 2, 3, 5, and 7 at least $2N-1$, $L/(2N-1)$ is close to 1.

Preparation (`FftPlan::new`, `build`) computes the stage order, twiddle tables, Rader permutation and kernel FFT, and Bluestein chirp and kernel FFT once.
Bluestein preparation includes one kernel FFT and is therefore more expensive than one execution.
This is the reason for reusing plans.

For rounding error, radix-2 Cooley–Tukey with twiddle error $\mu$ is known to have normalized relative 2-norm error bounded by $\log_2 N$ times $\mu + O(\varepsilon)$ (Higham 2002, §24.1).
For a direct DFT summing terms one by one, the error bound grows in proportion to $N$.
For SciOxide CPU complex and real forward transforms, tests against reference DFTs computed at 100- and 160-digit precision verify relative L2 error at most

$$
\frac{\lVert \hat X - X\rVert_2}{\lVert X\rVert_2} \le 2\varepsilon\max(1, \log_2 N)
$$

the expression above.
The tested range covers product-of-2,3,5,7 lengths through 6720, odd length 3375, Rader prime lengths through 65537, and Bluestein prime lengths through 10007, for `f32` and `f64` and every CPU implementation.
This is a normwise range verified by tests, not a proven upper bound.
Bins much smaller than the largest bin can have large relative errors.
Injected external implementations' accuracy is outside this range.

## Choosing fast lengths

When length is free to choose (such as convolution zero-padding), `next_fast_len(n)` returns the smallest product of 2, 3, 5, and 7 at least $n$.
`prev_fast_len(n)` returns the largest such product at most $n$, for truncating sequences.
Both return `InvalidInput` for $n = 0$; `next_fast_len` returns `SizeOverflow` if the result exceeds `usize`.
The result is shared by real and complex transforms.
Returned lengths use only mixed-radix stages but need not be the fastest on every processor.
Since the DFT uses the exact input length, zero-padding changes the spectral grid.
[Convolution and correlation](../convolution-correlation-and-analytic-signals/) gives an example of choosing zero-padding length.

## Experiments on length-dependent algorithms and error

The following cell creates `FftPlan` with `f32` for 12 lengths from the input $N$ to $N+11$ and tabulates the algorithm, stage count, workspace element count, and Bluestein convolution length $L$.
For each length, transform a deterministic input and compare with an independent reference computed from the same input by an $O(N^2)$ direct sum in `f64`.
Reference error is over 6 orders of magnitude smaller than the `f32` FFT error, so the relative L2 difference can be taken as the `f32` FFT error.
The final column divides this difference by the tested accuracy range $2\varepsilon_{32}\max(1,\log_2 N)$ ($\varepsilon_{32} = 2^{-23}$).
Input and reference twiddle generation use f64 `cos` and `sin`.
For the same 12 values of $N$, a second table compares `prev_fast_len` and `next_fast_len` with an independent search moving one step down and up from $N$ to find the first product of 2, 3, 5, and 7.
It also reports algorithms of plans at the returned lengths, checking that those lengths use only mixed-radix stages.

```rust
//| id: fft-planner-algorithm-choice
//| ref: fft-planner-algorithm-choice
//| title: "Check length-dependent algorithm selection and f32 error with plan diagnostics"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-fft]
//| inputs:
//|   start_length: {"type":"range","label":"First transform length N","description":"Create plans for 12 lengths from N through N+11.","min":1,"max":1000,"step":1,"value":96}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"short-lengths","label":"N = 1〜12","description":"The length-1 identity and Rader for prime 11 appear.","inputs":{"start_length":1}}
//|   - {"id":"near-thousand","label":"N = 1000〜1011","description":"Large-prime-factor lengths use Bluestein more often, with convolution length L slightly above 2N − 1.","inputs":{"start_length":1000}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":"MixedRadix"}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/1/2","equals":"Rader"}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/7/2","equals":"Bluestein"}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/0/4","approx":{"value":192,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/7/5","approx":{"value":210,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/0/7","range":{"max":1}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":96,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/1/2","approx":{"value":96,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/1/4","approx":{"value":98,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/7/1","approx":{"value":100,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/7/3","equals":"MixedRadix"}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/10/4","approx":{"value":108,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/10/5","approx":{"value":108,"atol":0.5}}
//|   - case: short-lengths
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":"Identity"}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/0/4","approx":{"value":0,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/10/2","equals":"Rader"}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/0/3","equals":"Identity"}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/10/1","approx":{"value":10,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/10/4","approx":{"value":12,"atol":0.5}}
//|   - case: near-thousand
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":"MixedRadix"}
//|       - {"artifact":"algorithms","path":"/rows/1/2","equals":"Bluestein"}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/1/4","approx":{"value":1008,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/11/1","approx":{"value":1008,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/11/4","approx":{"value":1024,"atol":0.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"fast-lengths","path":"/rows/11/6","equals":"MixedRadix"}
use scioxide_core::{Complex, ExecutionContext};
use scioxide_fft::{
    FftAlgorithmKind, FftDirection, FftLayout, FftPlan, FftPlanSpec, Normalization,
    next_fast_len, prev_fast_len,
};

let first = start_length.round() as usize;
let cx = ExecutionContext::sequential();
// Format prime factorization as a string (for display)
let factor = |mut n: usize| {
    let mut parts = Vec::new();
    let mut d = 2;
    while d * d <= n {
        while n % d == 0 {
            parts.push(d.to_string());
            n /= d;
        }
        d += 1;
    }
    if n > 1 || parts.is_empty() {
        parts.push(n.to_string());
    }
    parts.join("·")
};
let spec_of = |n: usize| {
    FftPlanSpec::new(
        n,
        FftDirection::Forward,
        Normalization::Unscaled,
        FftLayout::Complex,
    )
};
// Independent check: divide out 2, 3, 5, and 7 and test whether the remainder is 1 (fast-length criterion)
let smooth = |mut m: usize| {
    for p in [2, 3, 5, 7] {
        while m % p == 0 {
            m /= p;
        }
    }
    m == 1
};
// Name of the algorithm selected by a length-length plan
let algorithm_of = |length: usize| {
    FftPlan::<f32>::build(spec_of(length), cx)
        .map(|plan| format!("{:?}", plan.diagnostics().algorithm()))
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())
};
let mut rows = Vec::new();
let mut fast_rows = Vec::new();
for n in first..first + 12 {
    let plan = FftPlan::<f32>::build(spec_of(n), cx).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let diagnostics = plan.diagnostics();
    let scratch = plan
        .workspace_requirements(())
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?
        .complex_len();
    // Deterministic input x_j = cos(0.7 j) + i sin(1.3 j²/N) (generated with f64 functions)
    let input: Vec<Complex<f32>> = (0..n)
        .map(|j| {
            let t = j as f64;
            Complex::new(
                (0.7 * t).cos() as f32,
                (1.3 * t * t / n as f64).sin() as f32,
            )
        })
        .collect();
    let mut output = vec![Complex::new(0.0_f32, 0.0); n];
    let mut workspace = plan.workspace(()).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    plan.execute_into(&input, &mut output, &mut workspace, cx)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    // Independent reference: sum the defining expression for the same f32 input in f64 (twiddles from f64 cos/sin)
    let twiddle: Vec<Complex<f64>> = (0..n)
        .map(|m| {
            let a = -2.0 * std::f64::consts::PI * m as f64 / n as f64;
            Complex::new(a.cos(), a.sin())
        })
        .collect();
    let (mut difference, mut reference) = (0.0_f64, 0.0_f64);
    for (k, y) in output.iter().enumerate() {
        let mut direct = Complex::new(0.0_f64, 0.0);
        for (j, x) in input.iter().enumerate() {
            direct += Complex::new(x.re as f64, x.im as f64) * twiddle[(j * k) % n];
        }
        difference += (Complex::new(y.re as f64, y.im as f64) - direct).norm_sqr();
        reference += direct.norm_sqr();
    }
    let error = (difference / reference).sqrt();
    let bound = 2.0 * f32::EPSILON as f64 * (n as f64).log2().max(1.0);
    // Bluestein workspace is N + 2L, allowing L to be read from it
    let bluestein = diagnostics.algorithm() == FftAlgorithmKind::Bluestein;
    let convolution = bluestein.then(|| (scratch - n) / 2);
    rows.push((
        n,
        factor(n),
        format!("{:?}", diagnostics.algorithm()),
        diagnostics.stage_count(),
        scratch,
        convolution,
        error,
        error / bound,
    ));
    // Compare fast lengths with an independent one-step search and the algorithms selected at those lengths
    let prev = prev_fast_len(n).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let next = next_fast_len(n).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let prev_search = (1..=n).rev().find(|&m| smooth(m));
    let next_search = (n..).find(|&m| smooth(m));
    fast_rows.push((
        n,
        prev,
        prev_search,
        algorithm_of(prev)?,
        next,
        next_search,
        algorithm_of(next)?,
    ));
}
let sci2 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 2 });
let fixed2 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "N", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Prime factorization" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Algorithm" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Stages", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Workspace [complex values]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Bluestein L", "type": "integer", "nullText": "-" },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Relative L2 difference [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 },
    { "key": "7", "label": "Difference / 2ε₃₂max(1, log₂N) [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "algorithms" })
);
let fast_columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "N", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "prev_fast_len(N)", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Largest ≤ N from search", "type": "integer", "nullText": "-" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Algorithm for prev length" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "next_fast_len(N)", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Smallest ≥ N from search", "type": "integer", "nullText": "-" },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Algorithm for next length" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    fast_columns,
    fast_rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "fast-lengths" })
);
```

For defaults $N = 96$–$107$, 96, 98, 100, and 105 use `MixedRadix`; 97 and 101 use `Rader` ($97-1 = 96$ and $101-1 = 100$ are products of 2, 3, 5, and 7); the remaining 6 use `Bluestein`.
Any prime factor 11 or larger, as in $99 = 3^2\cdot 11$ or $104 = 2^3\cdot 13$, selects Bluestein even for composite lengths.
Bluestein workspace has $N + 2L$ values; for $N = 103$, $L = 210$ (the smallest product of 2, 3, 5, and 7 at least $2N-1 = 205$) gives 523 workspace values, about 2.5 times the mixed-radix $2N$ values.
Relative L2 differences are around $1\times10^{-7}$ for mixed radices and $2\times10^{-7}$ for Rader and Bluestein; all are at most 0.15 times the tested accuracy range.
The roughly doubled differences for Rader and Bluestein compared with mixed radices plausibly reflect passing through 2 convolution FFTs and phase products.
This is an observation for this input, not a general ratio.

For $N = 1$–$12$, length 1 uses `Identity` (0 workspace values), and 11 uses `Rader`.
For $N = 1000$–$1011$, 1009 uses `Rader` ($1008 = 2^4\cdot3^2\cdot7$), 1000 and 1008 use mixed radices, and the rest use Bluestein with $L = 2016$ or $2025$.
Stage counts, workspaces, and error ratios compare storage and accuracy but do not determine execution time.

`prev_fast_len` and `next_fast_len` match the independent search for every $N$; all returned-length plans select `MixedRadix` (`Identity` at length 1).
In the default range, for example, $N = 103$ moves down to 100 or up to 105; the next fast length for $N = 106$ and $107$ is 108.
For $N = 1009$–$1011$, the lower fast length 1008 and upper 1024 are far apart, so truncation and zero-padding change very different numbers of samples.

## Summary

`scioxide-fft` transforms every length in $O(N\log N)$, choosing mixed radices for products of 2, 3, 5, and 7, Rader for qualifying primes, and Bluestein otherwise.
Read the selected algorithm and stage count with `diagnostics()`.
Bluestein uses 2 FFTs of length at least $2N-1$, so when length can be chosen freely, zero-padding to a product of 2, 3, 5, and 7 with `next_fast_len` costs less.
Within the tested range, rounding error is at most $2\varepsilon\max(1,\log_2 N)$, including Rader and Bluestein lengths.
This is a normwise range; check relative error of small bins and external-implementation accuracy separately.
