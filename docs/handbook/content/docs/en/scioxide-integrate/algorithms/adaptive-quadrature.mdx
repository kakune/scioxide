Integrating a function that varies slowly over most of an interval but changes rapidly in narrow boundary layers or resonance peaks with uniform steps wastes many evaluations in flat regions.
Adaptive quadrature uses error estimates on subintervals to decide where to refine.
Since the true error is unknown, two rules of different orders are constructed from the same samples, and their difference substitutes for the error.
These two rules form an embedded pair.
This page covers embedded-pair construction and error-estimation formulas, globally adaptive bisection starting with high-error intervals, stopping conditions, and extrapolation for endpoint singularities.

## Gauss–Kronrod pairs

A Kronrod extension retains the nodes $x_1,\dots,x_n$ of an $n$-node Gauss–Legendre rule and adds $n+1$ nodes to form a $2n+1$-node rule.

$$
G_n = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i^G f(x_i), \qquad K_{2n+1} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i^K f(x_i) + \sum_{j=1}^{n+1} w_j^{*} f(x_j^{*}) .
$$

The added nodes $x_j^{*}$ are zeros of the Stieltjes polynomial $E_{n+1}$.
$E_{n+1}$ is a polynomial of degree $n+1$ orthogonal to all polynomials of degree at most $n$ with respect to the sign-changing weight $P_n$ (a Legendre polynomial).

$$
\int_{-1}^1 E_{n+1}(x)\,P_n(x)\,x^k\,dx = 0, \qquad k=0,1,\dots,n .
$$

Choosing interpolatory weights under this condition makes $K_{2n+1}$ exact through degree $3n+1$ or $3n+2$ when $n$ is even or odd, respectively.
Computing $G_n$ and $K_{2n+1}$ separately requires $3n+1$ evaluations, but shared nodes reduce this to $2n+1$.
`GaussKronrodRule` represents the following five pairs.

| Pair | Gauss nodes | Kronrod nodes (evaluations) | Gauss degree of exactness | Kronrod degree of exactness |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `G7K15` | 7 | 15 | 13 | 23 |
| `G10K21` | 10 | 21 | 19 | 31 |
| `G15K31` | 15 | 31 | 29 | 47 |
| `G20K41` | 20 | 41 | 39 | 61 |
| `G25K51` | 25 | 51 | 49 | 77 |

`GaussKronrod::new(rule)` is a fixed rule that applies the pair once to one interval without subdivision and provides an error estimate.
Nodes and weights are rounded from tables to `S` only once.
The Gauss–Kronrod rule (`AdaptiveRule::GaussKronrod`) in `AdaptiveIntegrator` always uses G7K15.

## Gauss–Kronrod error estimation

The naive estimate is $|K-G|$, but SciOxide uses the following rescaling.
Let $|R|$ be the width of the interval $R$, and let $K$ and $G$ be the Kronrod and Gauss integral values on $R$.
Let $\rho$ be the Kronrod estimate of $\int_R|f(x)-K/|R||\,dx$ (the magnitude of deviation from the mean), and $A$ the Kronrod estimate of $\int_R|f|\,dx$; then the error estimate is

$$
E = \max\Bigl(\rho\,\min\bigl(1,\ (200\,|K-G|/\rho)^{3/2}\bigr),\ 50\,\varepsilon A\Bigr)
$$

where the first term is $|K-G|$ if $\rho=0$.
When $K$ and $G$ agree closely, raising to the power $3/2$ makes the estimate smaller than $|K-G|$.
This is based on the empirical observation that $|K-G|$ mainly represents the error of the less accurate $G$, while the error of $K$ is much smaller.
Conversely, when $G$ is far off, the estimate saturates at $\rho$ and remains large even if $K$ is accurate.
The second term is a rounding error floor for the sum; smaller estimates are meaningless.
Both terms are estimates, not upper bounds.

## Nested Simpson and Boole rules

Let $S_H$ be the Simpson value on an interval of width $H$, and $S_{H/2}$ the sum of Simpson values on its two children bisected at the midpoint.
For sufficiently smooth $f$, $I-S_H=CH^5+O(H^7)$, and the sum of child errors is $I-S_{H/2}=2C(H/2)^5+O(H^7)=CH^5/16+O(H^7)$.
Subtracting the two expressions, $S_{H/2}-S_H=\frac{15}{16}CH^5+O(H^7)$, gives the classical estimate

$$
I - S_{H/2} \approx \frac{S_{H/2}-S_H}{15}
$$


The denominator $15=2^4-1$ follows from Simpson error being proportional to $H^5$.
Adding this correction gives $S_{H/2}+(S_{H/2}-S_H)/15$, equal to Boole's rule.

SciOxide's `AdaptiveRule::Simpson` takes this idea one step further, retaining 9 equally spaced samples on each interval.
It uses the 9-node composite Boole value as the interval integral and four times its difference from the 5-node Boole value on alternate nodes as the error estimate (never below $50\varepsilon A$).
For smooth $f$, the same argument gives an estimate of $1/63$ times the difference; because errors at jumps or corners do not follow a smooth expansion, the much more conservative factor 4 is used.
On bisection, each child reuses 5 of the parent's 9 nodes, giving 8 evaluations per bisection.
Since endpoints are also evaluated, it cannot be used for functions diverging at endpoints.

When all samples lie on a dyadic grid, functions resonating with the grid, such as $\sin^2(2^m\pi x)$, appear to be 0 at every sample.
Thus, along with the first interval's 9 nodes, one off-grid evaluation is made at position $(\sqrt5-1)/2$ in the interval (10 evaluations on the first interval).
If this value differs from the degree-7 interpolant through 8 nearby grid nodes by more than four times the difference between the degree-7 and degree-5 interpolants, the error of the interval containing the point is raised to at least four times that discrepancy multiplied by the interval width, and uniform exploration goes two levels deeper than that interval.
Convergence is not accepted until four levels of uniform exploration are complete, so success requires at least 15 bisections and 130 evaluations.
`AdaptiveIntegrator::new` rejects `Simpson` with a bisection limit below 15 as `InvalidInput`.
A call whose samples are all 0 does not return success.

## The globally adaptive algorithm

`AdaptiveIntegrator` maintains a collection of intervals covering $[a,b]$, each with an integral value $Q_r$ and error estimate $E_r$.
The global criterion checks whether the sums over all intervals, $E=\sum_r E_r$ and $Q=\sum_r Q_r$, satisfy

$$
E \le \varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}\,|Q|
$$


Unlike local criteria that assign tolerances to individual intervals, it does not refine flat regions with small errors.
The procedure is as follows.

1. Apply the rule to the entire interval (15 evaluations for G7K15).
2. If the criterion is satisfied, terminate with `ToleranceReached`.
3. Select the interval with the largest error.
   For real values, compare estimate magnitudes; for multiple real components, divide each component's estimate by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs},i}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}|Q_{r,i}|$ and combine using the tolerance norm.
4. Check the entire work for one midpoint bisection (30 evaluations for the two G7K15 children) against the evaluation and bisection limits together.
   If insufficient, terminate with `BudgetLimit` without starting bisection.
5. Evaluate both children, replace the parent's contribution with theirs, and return to step 2.

A bisection is accepted as one unit before evaluation, so a limit cannot cause only one child to be evaluated.
Even termination with `BudgetLimit` returns an estimate covering the entire interval.
If the integrand returns an error or a non-finite value during evaluation, the entire call returns an error and the output is unchanged.
The evaluation count is therefore $15+30s$ for G7K15 and $10+8s$ for Simpson ($s$ is the bisection count).
Interval values and estimates use compensated summation; the total over all intervals is added exactly and rounded only once.

## Stopping conditions

The `AdaptiveIntegrator` report contains one of the following termination reasons.

- `ToleranceReached`: the error estimate satisfies the tolerance.
- `Stagnation`: further bisection does not improve the estimate.
  This occurs when an interval is so narrow that its midpoint is indistinguishable from an endpoint, all interval estimates reach the rounding error floor, or bisection stops making progress.
  Lack of progress means a total of 6 bisections with a relative value change at most $10^{-5}$ while at least 99% of the error remains, or a total of 20 error increases in bisections after the tenth.
  With extrapolation, it also occurs when extrapolated values fail to improve 6 times consecutively while their estimate is much smaller than the direct estimate.
- `BudgetLimit`: the bisection or evaluation limit prevents the next bisection.
- `ExecutionStopped`: cancellation or deadline.
- `ProbablyDivergent`: occurs only with extrapolation enabled.
- `DegenerateDomain`: $a=b$, so the result is exactly 0 without calling the integrand.

If a limit or cancellation stops the first interval before its evaluation finishes, an error is returned instead of a value.
An evaluation limit below the first interval's evaluation count (15 for G7K15, 10 for Simpson) gives `ConvergenceFailure`.
If the tolerance is below the rounding error floor, the estimate cannot decrease below it, and computation stops with `Stagnation`.

## Options and workspace

The defaults for `SubdivisionOptions` are absolute and relative tolerances both $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ (about $1.49\times10^{-8}$ for `f64`), the maximum norm, 50 bisections, no evaluation limit, and sequential execution.
Change them with `with_tolerance`, `with_max_subdivisions`, `with_max_evaluations`, and `with_execution_policy`.
For vector values, `ErrorTolerance::try_vector` supplies componentwise absolute tolerances; a length differing from the component count gives `DimensionMismatch` before the first evaluation.

Create `AdaptiveWorkspace` with `AdaptiveIntegrator::workspace(components)`; it retains a number of intervals equal to the bisection limit $+1$, their componentwise estimates, and 9 samples per interval for Simpson.
Memory usage is $O(cm)$ for component count $c$ and bisection limit $m$, always including extrapolation storage.
A workspace created for G7K15 can serve both methods if `try_reserve` meets Simpson's requirements.
`component_errors()` returns componentwise error estimates and is `None` before a call, after an error, or after a zero-width interval.

As the width decreases, the bisection depth needed to resolve the peak grows proportionally to $\log(1/w)$, and the evaluation count increases in steps approximately proportional to the logarithm of the width.
At $w=0.01$, G7K15 used 10 bisections and 315 evaluations, with a difference of $6\times10^{-16}$ from the closed form and an error estimate of $9.2\times10^{-9}$.
Simpson used 53 bisections and 434 evaluations, with difference $2.3\times10^{-11}$ and estimate $1.0\times10^{-8}$.
With tolerance $10^{-11}$, G7K15 required 525 evaluations and Simpson 1290.
For smooth peaks, the higher-order G7K15 needs fewer evaluations.

## Endpoint singularities and ε extrapolation

For integrable endpoint singularities such as $f(x)=x^{-\alpha}$ ($0<\alpha<1$), bisection concentrates on the interval containing the singularity.
The total $S_k$ after $k$ bisections of the singular interval converges only linearly (geometrically), approximately as $S_k\approx I-C\,2^{-k(1-\alpha)}$.
`with_extrapolation(true)` extrapolates this sequence with Wynn's ε algorithm.
The ε algorithm constructs the table

$$
\varepsilon_{-1}^{(k)}=0,\qquad \varepsilon_0^{(k)}=S_k,\qquad \varepsilon_{j+1}^{(k)}=\varepsilon_{j-1}^{(k+1)}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_j^{(k+1)}-\varepsilon_j^{(k)}}
$$

and uses even-numbered columns to estimate the limit.
For sequences whose error is a sum of geometric series, the ε algorithm gives the limit with few terms (the theory is covered in [Infinite series acceleration (Levin u and Wynn ε)](../series-acceleration/)).

SciOxide first bisects until the error on large intervals meets the tolerance, then adds the current total to a componentwise ε table of at most 50 terms.
The extrapolated error estimate sums the distances from the previous 3 extrapolated values and adds the large-interval error; it is stored in `extrapolation()` of `error_components()`.
Success occurs when this estimate satisfies the tolerance.
If totals keep moving in the same direction by similar amounts and no usable extrapolated value exists, termination is `ProbablyDivergent`.
Extrapolation is disabled by default in `AdaptiveIntegrator` and does not add evaluations.
Enabling it with `Simpson` gives `InvalidInput`.
The one-shot function `integrate(f, a, b)` uses `AdaptiveIntegrator` with G7K15 and this extrapolation enabled under the default `SubdivisionOptions`.
On success it returns the same value and error estimate in `Integral`; otherwise it converts the termination status into an error.
Thus `integrate` also handles finite-interval integrals with endpoint singularities when default settings suffice.

G7K15 nodes lie inside the interval, so $x=0$ is not evaluated.
The input is the exponent $\alpha$; at $\alpha\ge1$ the integral diverges, so actual error is undefined.

//|   - {"id":"mild","label":"α = 0.5","description":"For a weak singularity, bisection alone meets the tolerance.","inputs":{"exponent":0.5}}

//|       - {"path":"/outputs/1/state","equals":"satisfied"}
//|   - case: mild
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"methods","path":"/rows/0/6","equals":"ToleranceReached"}
//|       - {"artifact":"methods","path":"/rows/1/2","range":{"min":0,"max":1e-12}}
//|   - case: divergent
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"methods","path":"/rows/1/6","equals":"ProbablyDivergent"}
//|       - {"artifact":"methods","path":"/rows/1/2","equals":null}
//|       - {"path":"/outputs/1/state","equals":"failed"}

use scioxide_integrate::{
    AdaptiveIntegrator, AdaptiveRule, IntegrationTermination, SubdivisionOptions,

// ∫₀¹ x^(−α) dx = 1/(1 − α) (α < 1). For α ≥ 1 it diverges, so no exact value is defined.
let exact = (alpha < 1.0).then(|| 1.0 / (1.0 - alpha));

// G7K15 nodes lie inside the interval, so x = 0 is not evaluated.

    .clone()
    .with_extrapolation(true)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

        .integrate(&integrand, interval)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

        .error_components()
        .and_then(|parts| parts.extrapolation())
        .map(|estimate| estimate.magnitude());

        name,
        value,
        exact.map(|exact| (value - exact).abs()),
        report.error_estimate(),
        extrapolation,
        report.evaluations(),
        report.termination().to_string(),
    ));

    { "key": "1", "label": "Value [-]", "type": "number", "format": digits12 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Actual error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": "Divergent" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Error estimate [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": "Undefined" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Extrapolation error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": "None" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Evaluation count [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Termination reason" }
]);

    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "methods" })
);

} else if report.termination() == IntegrationTermination::ProbablyDivergent {

} else {

    "Evaluation count",
    report.evaluations() as f64,

    None => outcome,
});

Enabling extrapolation gave `ToleranceReached` after 165 evaluations, with a difference of about $10^{-13}$ and error estimate about $2\times10^{-12}$.
At $\alpha=0.5$, bisection alone met the tolerance after 1485 evaluations, while extrapolation needed only 165.
At $\alpha=1.1$, bisection alone gave `Stagnation` (915 evaluations), while extrapolation gave `ProbablyDivergent` after 165.
Neither returned value may be used as an integral estimate.

## Limits of detecting features

Adaptive quadrature decides solely from information present in samples.
For a peak narrow enough to hide between nodes (width $w$ much smaller than the initial node spacing), all initial nodes may land on flat regions, causing both $K$ and $G$ to return nearly 0 and the error estimate to be small.
The adaptive method can then miss the peak and return success.

- If peak or material-interface locations are known, split the interval there or supply breakpoints with `WeightedQuadraturePlan::breakpoints`.
  If default settings suffice, the one-shot function `integrate_piecewise(f, a, b, &points)` uses the same subdivision ([Interior singularities and breakpoints](oxref:integrate-de-interior-singularities)).
- If the peak location and width scale are known, spread it with a variable transformation such as $x=c+wu$.

[Narrow peaks](../../examples/narrow-peak/) gives a concrete example.

## Summary

An embedded pair constructs two rules of different orders from the same samples and uses their difference as an error estimate.
The G7K15 estimate is the maximum of the rescaled error and the rounding error floor; the Simpson estimate is four times the difference of nested Boole rules; neither is an upper bound.
`AdaptiveIntegrator` bisects the interval with the largest error after checking a full unit of evaluations against limits, returning an estimate covering the entire interval even when a limit stops computation.
Use ε extrapolation with `with_extrapolation(true)` for endpoint singularities, and interpret `ProbablyDivergent` as signs of divergence.
Features hidden between nodes do not appear in the estimate; subdivide known feature locations in advance.
