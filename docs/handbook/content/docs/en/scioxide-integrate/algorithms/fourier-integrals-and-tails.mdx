For a strongly oscillatory integrand, positive and negative waves cancel, making the integral much smaller than the area of an individual wave.
This page explains analytical treatment of the linear-phase oscillatory factors $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ as weights, and repetition of this treatment over cycles to extrapolate Fourier integrals on a half-line.

| Problem | Plan | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Weights $\cos(\omega x)$ and $\sin(\omega x)$ on a finite interval | `WeightedQuadraturePlan::fourier` | Clenshaw–Curtis with modified Chebyshev moments |
| Fourier integral on a half-line or the entire real line | `FourierTailPlan` | Cycle integration and Wynn ε extrapolation |

Nonlinear phases are covered in [Oscillatory integrals with nonlinear phases (Levin and Filon)](../phase-aware-oscillatory/); nonoscillatory endpoint singularities and infinite intervals in [Double-exponential transformations for improper integrals and endpoint singularities](../improper-singular-and-oscillatory/).

## Cancellation in oscillatory integrals

For the high-frequency oscillatory integral

$$
I = \int_a^b A(x)\cos(\omega x + \varphi)\,dx \qquad (\omega \gg 1)
$$

positive and negative waves individually have large areas, but their cancellation reduces the integral to $O(\omega^{-1})$.
Integration by parts shows that the leading term is the endpoint value $A(b)\sin(\omega b + \varphi)/\omega - A(a)\sin(\omega a + \varphi)/\omega$.

A uniform grid or ordinary Gaussian quadrature misidentifies oscillations (aliasing) if too few nodes resolve each wavelength.
Uniform refinement to the shortest wavelength makes the evaluation count proportional to the number of oscillations even where the amplitude $A(x)$ is smooth.
Cancellation of large positive and negative contributions also makes the problem ill-conditioned: every algorithm retains rounding error proportional to the absolute size of those large contributions.

## Fourier weights and modified moments

Separating the oscillatory factor as the weight $w(x) = \cos(\omega x)$ or $\sin(\omega x)$ and interpolating only the smooth amplitude avoids resolving oscillations by sampling.
Map each subdomain to standard coordinates $t \in [-1, 1]$ and interpolate the amplitude at 25 Chebyshev extrema.
The inner product of the interpolant's Chebyshev coefficients with the modified Chebyshev moments $\int_{-1}^{1} T_k(t)\,w(x(t))\,dt$ gives the subdomain integral.
This is called a modified Clenshaw–Curtis rule.
Its difference from the nested 13-node rule estimates discretization error.

`WeightedQuadraturePlan::fourier(interval, points, omega, FourierWeight, options)` prepares the angular frequency and weight; pass only the amplitude to `integrate`.
The one-shot function `integrate_fourier(f, a, b, omega, weight)` performs the same computation without breakpoints using default `WeightedQuadratureOptions` (tolerance $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, 50 bisections, moment depth 16).
Only on success does it return a value and error estimate in `Integral`; it returns neither evaluation count nor termination status.
Use the Plan to change tolerances or limits or receive an estimate after reaching a limit.
This method requires a phase linear in position, such as $\omega x$.
On a subdomain of half-width $h$, use the moment rule when $|\omega| h > 2$; otherwise apply G7K15 to the product $A(x)w(x)$.
Moments depend only on $|\omega| h$, and $h$ halves with each bisection, so they are prepared only for strongly oscillatory depths of each initial subinterval (at most 69 scalars per depth).
`WeightedQuadratureOptions::with_moment_levels` limits the bisection depth using moments and defaults to 16.
Only required depths are prepared, so a large limit does not increase memory usage.
A subdomain still satisfying $|\omega| h > 2$ at the depth limit is not bisected; failure to meet the tolerance gives `RefinementLimit`.

For large $|\omega| h$, the difference between the two nested rules is governed mostly by endpoint amplitude values and misses interior jumps, corners, or cusps.
Each moment subdomain therefore also computes the frequency-independent indicator $2(|c_{23}| + |c_{24}|)\,L$ from the amplitude interpolant's two highest-degree Chebyshev coefficients $c_{23}, c_{24}$ ($L$ is the subdomain width), taking the larger of this indicator and the nested difference as discretization error.
Each subdomain separately retains its discretization estimate, rounding error floor, and propagated moment error.
`component_errors()` returns their sums over all subdomains as `discretization`, `roundoff`, and `moments`.
`error_estimate()` is `total()`: take the larger of discretization and rounding error floor rather than their sum, then add moment error (and extrapolation error if enabled).
The subdomain's $\max(\text{discretization}, \text{rounding}) + \text{moments}$ also determines which one to bisect.

## Fourier-weighted quadrature of damped oscillations

Integrate the damped cosine $f(x) = e^{-a x}\cos(\omega x)$ over $[0, 20]$ m.
Here $x$ is position [m], $\omega$ angular frequency [rad/m], and $a$ decay rate [1/m]; since the integrand is dimensionless, the integral has units of m.
Pass only the amplitude $e^{-ax}$ to `WeightedQuadraturePlan::fourier` and evaluate it through `RealScalar::exp`.

The independent check uses the antiderivative

$$
F(x) = \frac{e^{-ax}\bigl(-a\cos\omega x + \omega\sin\omega x\bigr)}{a^2 + \omega^2}
$$

to compute $F(20) - F(0)$ with f64 standard functions.
The first table row uses a Plan with tolerance $10^{-10}$, 4096 bisections, and moment depth 32; the second uses `integrate_fourier` for the same integral with defaults.
The one-shot row leaves evaluation count, subdivision count, and termination status blank because they are not returned.
The outcome reports the Plan's termination status.
The plot shows the cumulative integral $F(x) - F(0)$ from $0$ to $x$, illustrating cancellation of positive and negative waves toward the small full-interval value (horizontal line).

At $a = 0.5$ /m, the amplitude varies more, and the Plan used 125 evaluations after subdividing twice.
Here the error estimate $1.3 \times 10^{-10}$ m from `integrate_fourier` is larger than the Plan's, but its difference from the exact value was $3 \times 10^{-17}$ m.

## Fourier integrals on infinite intervals

For the Fourier integral on a half-line

$$
I = \int_a^{\infty} f(x)\cos(\omega x)\,dx \quad\text{or}\quad \int_a^{\infty} f(x)\sin(\omega x)\,dx
$$

if $f$ tends slowly to 0, as with $1/x$, the integrand need not be absolutely integrable.
The integral then converges conditionally through cancellation of positive and negative waves.
Truncating to a finite interval reduces error only on the order of $f(R)/\omega$, and nodes of a [double-exponential (DE) transformation](../improper-singular-and-oscillatory/) cannot resolve distant oscillations.

### Cycle integration and extrapolation

Following Longman's method, `FourierTailPlan` divides the half-line $[x_0, \infty)$ into cycles of length

$$
L = \frac{(2\lfloor|\omega|\rfloor + 1)\,\pi}{|\omega|}
$$


Since $L$ is an odd multiple of a half-period, integrals over adjacent cycles alternate in sign when the amplitude varies slowly.
For $|\omega| \ge 1$, $L$ is greater than $3\pi/2$ and at most $3\pi$; the $\lfloor|\omega|\rfloor$ term increases the number of half-periods per cycle with frequency, keeping cycle length on the order of $2\pi$ even at high frequencies.
For $|\omega| < 1$, $L = \pi/|\omega|$, making a cycle one half-period.

The integral $c_k$ over cycle $k$ uses the same rule as `WeightedQuadraturePlan::fourier` with tolerance allocation $0.05 \times 0.9^k$.
These allocations sum to 0.5, using half the tolerance for cycle-local errors and reserving the rest for extrapolation and the tail.
The partial-sum sequence $S_K = \sum_{k<K} c_k$ behaves like an alternating series, so Wynn's ε algorithm extrapolates its limit ([Infinite series acceleration (Levin u and Wynn ε)](../series-acceleration/)).
The first cycle is split at distance $\min(2/|\omega|, L/4)$ from its finite endpoint to resolve an integrable endpoint singularity with Gauss–Kronrod nodes.

### Criteria for establishing decay

Extrapolation alone cannot establish convergence.
If cycle contributions approach a nonzero limit $\ell$, alternating partial sums continue oscillating by about $\ell$, yet Wynn ε returns their Abel mean with a small error estimate.
Cosine cycle integrals also eliminate the constant part of the amplitude, so failure of the amplitude to approach 0 can only be detected from the amplitude itself.

The Plan therefore records both the cycle contribution magnitudes $m_k = |c_k|$ and the maximum sampled amplitude $v_k$ within each cycle.
For either sequence, a cycle counts as evidence of decay only if its decrease $d_k = v_{k-1} - v_k$ satisfies all three conditions below.

1. $d_k$ exceeds the sum of both values' uncertainties (the error estimate for $m_k$ and rounding uncertainty for $v_k$).
2. With $x_k$ the distance from the origin to the cycle center, $L\,v_k \le 5\,|x_k|\,d_k$ holds.
   This means consistency with algebraic decay $|x|^{-p}$, $p \ge 1/5$.
3. If the preceding cycle also counted, the curvature is no greater than that of a shifted power law $(x - x_0)^{-p}$, $p \ge 2/7$.
   In formulas, $v_{k-1}(d_{k-1} - d_k - u) \le \frac{9}{2}\,d_{k-1}\,d_k$, where $u$ is curvature uncertainty.

Decay is considered established once 3 consecutive cycles count and at least 6 cycles have been integrated.
Geometric sequences, power laws, and $\ln x/\sqrt{x}$ pass this check.
A sequence approaching a nonzero limit fails because its two ratios grow without bound.
However, amplitudes decaying more slowly than $|x|^{-2/7}$, or limits falling below value resolution within a few cycles (such as $10^{-6} + 1/(1+x)$), cannot be distinguished from decay.

Once decay is established, estimate the tail from the last contribution as $m_K / (1 - \rho)$.
Here $\rho$ is the largest adjacent-contribution ratio over the latest 3 cycles.
Use the extrapolated value if its error estimate is smaller than this tail; otherwise use the partial sum.
The error breakdown consists of discretization, rounding, moments, extrapolation (when the extrapolated value is used), and tail (`ErrorReliability::Heuristic`).

### Termination status and tail diagnostics

For each half-line, truncate at the cycle where the total error estimate meets the tolerance and set the tail status to `HeuristicallyTruncated`.
After truncating all half-lines, recheck the tolerance using the total value and error; terminate with `HeuristicToleranceReached` if satisfied and `Stagnation` otherwise.
If `max_cycles` cycles are reached without truncation, terminate with `RefinementLimit` while the tail status remains `Open`.
The returned value is then the last partial sum or extrapolated value and should be treated as an estimate.
If cycle quadrature stops without meeting its tolerance (for example, at the evaluation limit), return that termination status.
However, `Stagnation` caused solely by reaching the rounding and moment error floors is accepted as that cycle's best value, and computation continues.

For Fourier integrals, `tail_diagnostics()` in `FourierTailWorkspace::component_errors()` has the following meanings.

- `extent()` is the length integrated from the finite endpoint (cycle count × $L$), in the same units as $x$.
  It differs from the transformed coordinate $t$ in DE integration.
- `shells()` is the number of integrated cycles.
- `status()` takes only `Open` or `HeuristicallyTruncated`.
  `Saturated` and `Stagnated` are returned only for DE tails (including finite-interval tanh–sinh).

`FourierDomain` selects the integration range.
`Upper { lower }` denotes $[\text{lower}, \infty)$ and `Lower { upper }` denotes $(-\infty, \text{upper}]$; the latter maps to an upper half-line by $x \to -x$ (reversing the sign of sine weights).
`RealLine` sums the integrals on $(-\infty, 0]$ and $[0, \infty)$, each of which must converge separately.
It is not the symmetric principal value $\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}$.
For `RealLine`, the two half-lines share the tolerance; diagnostics use the larger `extent()`, the sum for `shells()`, and the untruncated side's status for `status()`.

Tolerances and limits in `FourierTailOptions::new(SubdivisionOptions)` apply to the entire call.
`with_max_cycles` limits cycles per half-line and defaults to 128; `FourierTailPlan::new` rejects values below 6 with `InvalidInput`.
`with_moment_levels` replaces the moment depth of each cycle; `with_quadrature_options` replaces all integration settings for each cycle.
$\omega = 0$ is also `InvalidInput`.
`FourierTailWorkspace` created by `workspace(components)` retains one cycle's subdomains, the extrapolation table, and componentwise cycle history, and can be passed repeatedly to `integrate_into`.

### A Fourier integral with algebraically decaying amplitude

Compute the integral

$$
I(\omega) = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{\cos(\omega x)}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}\,e^{-\omega}
$$

with `FourierTailPlan` and compare it with its closed form.
Here $x$ and $\omega$ are dimensionless, and the amplitude $1/(1+x^2)$ decays as $x^{-2}$.
For comparison, integrate the same integrand $\cos(\omega x)/(1+x^2)$ using `DoubleExponentialIntegrator` with an exp–sinh transformation.
The comparison DE computation is limited to 10 levels (finest step $2^{-9}$) to keep evaluations within a few thousand.
Inputs are angular frequency $\omega$ and the cycle-count limit; absolute and relative tolerances are both $10^{-10}$.
The DE row evaluates the cosine with SciOxide's `RealScalar::cos`; the reference exponential uses f64 standard functions.

“Tail extent” is the length $x$ in the Fourier row and transformed coordinate $|t|$ in the DE row.
The outcome reports the termination status of `FourierTailPlan`.

## Summary

Oscillatory integrals cancel positive and negative contributions; since every algorithm retains rounding error proportional to the absolute size of large contributions, specify an absolute tolerance as well.
For linear phases, pass only the amplitude to `WeightedQuadraturePlan::fourier` and integrate with modified moments without resolving the oscillations.
For finite intervals where the default tolerance $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ suffices, use `integrate_fourier`, which returns an error on failure.
For Fourier integrals on half-lines, `FourierTailPlan` integrates by cycles and extrapolates; success requires evidence that both the amplitude and cycle contributions decay.
Read a result that reaches `max_cycles` as an estimate with `RefinementLimit`, checking tail status `Open` and the error estimate.
For slowly decaying oscillatory integrands, DE transformations on half-lines tend to stagnate.
