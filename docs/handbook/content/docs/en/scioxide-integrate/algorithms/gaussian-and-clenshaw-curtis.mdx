Newton–Cotes formulas fix equally spaced nodes, so their degree of polynomial exactness with $n$ nodes is limited to $n-1$ or $n$.
Freely choosing all $2n$ quantities consisting of the nodes $x_i$ and weights $w_i$ allows $n$ nodes to integrate polynomials through degree $2n-1$ exactly.
This is Gaussian quadrature.
This page covers the exactness proof, the Golub–Welsch eigenvalue method for nodes and weights, weight functions, variants including endpoints, and Clenshaw–Curtis rules using Chebyshev extrema.

## The Gauss–Christoffel theorem

For a positive weight function $w$ on $(a,b)$, let $\pi_0,\pi_1,\dots$ be polynomials with leading coefficient 1 (monic orthogonal polynomials) orthogonal under the inner product $\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int_a^b f g\,w\,dx$.
Choose nodes $x_1,\dots,x_n$ as zeros of $\pi_n$ and interpolatory weights

$$
w_i = \int_a^b \ell_i(x)\,w(x)\,dx, \qquad \ell_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}
$$

Then $\int_a^b p\,w\,dx \approx \sum_i w_i\,p(x_i)$ is exact for every polynomial $p$ of degree at most $2n-1$.
No $n$-node rule can integrate every polynomial of degree $2n$ exactly.

The proof follows.
Dividing $p$ with $\deg p\le 2n-1$ by $\pi_n$ gives $p=q\,\pi_n+r$ ($\deg q,\deg r\le n-1$).
Orthogonality gives $\int q\,\pi_n\,w\,dx=0$, hence $\int p\,w\,dx=\int r\,w\,dx$.
At the nodes, $\pi_n(x_i)=0$, so $\sum_i w_i p(x_i)=\sum_i w_i r(x_i)$.
The interpolatory rule integrates $r$ of degree at most $n-1$ exactly, so these expressions agree.
At degree $2n$, choose $s=\pi_n^2$: then $\int s\,w\,dx>0$, yet $s(x_i)=0$ at every node, making the sum 0.

If $f\in C^{2n}$, the error is

$$
\int_a^b f\,w\,dx - \sum_{i=1}^n w_i f(x_i) = \frac{f^{(2n)}(\xi)}{(2n)!}\,\langle\pi_n,\pi_n\rangle_w
$$

where $\xi\in(a,b)$.
`GaussianRulePlan::remainder_derivative_order()` returns this derivative order (degree of exactness $+1$) but does not compute the remainder itself.

## Three-term recurrence and Golub–Welsch

Monic orthogonal polynomials satisfy the three-term recurrence

$$
\pi_{k+1}(x) = (x-\alpha_k)\,\pi_k(x) - \beta_k\,\pi_{k-1}(x), \qquad \pi_0=1,\ \pi_{-1}=0
$$


The coefficients are $\alpha_k=\langle x\pi_k,\pi_k\rangle_w/\langle\pi_k,\pi_k\rangle_w$ and $\beta_k=\langle\pi_k,\pi_k\rangle_w/\langle\pi_{k-1},\pi_{k-1}\rangle_w$, with the convention $\beta_0=\mu_0=\int_a^b w\,dx$.
Rewriting in normalized polynomials turns the recurrence into the eigenvalue problem for the symmetric tridiagonal matrix (Jacobi matrix)

$$
J_n = \begin{pmatrix}
\alpha_0 & \sqrt{\beta_1} & & \\
\sqrt{\beta_1} & \alpha_1 & \ddots & \\
 & \ddots & \ddots & \sqrt{\beta_{n-1}} \\
 & & \sqrt{\beta_{n-1}} & \alpha_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
$$


Nodes $x_i$ are eigenvalues of $J_n$, and the first components of normalized eigenvectors $\boldsymbol v_i$ give weights $w_i=\mu_0\,v_{1,i}^2$.
Since polynomial roots are not computed directly, their ill-conditioning does not affect the computation.

`GaussianRulePlan` solves this eigenvalue problem by implicit QL, tracking only the first eigenvector components.
The computational cost is $O(n^2)$ and memory usage $O(n)$, with all arithmetic in scalar type `S`.
For Hermite families, outer-node weights are tiny and can be lost from eigenvector components during deflation.
Weights are therefore computed from the Christoffel-type formula $w_i=\mu_0/\bigl(n\,p_{n-1}(x_i)^2\bigr)$.
Here $p_k$, with $p_0=1$, are polynomials orthonormal under the measure $w/\mu_0$, evaluated by a scaled recurrence to avoid overflow.
Every family finally rescales weights to sum to $\mu_0$; families with symmetric weight functions also enforce exact symmetry of nodes and weights.

Prepare `GaussianRulePlan::with_options(family, n, variant, options)` by specifying the family, node count, variant (Radau and Lobatto below), and `GaussianRuleOptions`.
`GaussianRulePlan::new(family, n)` abbreviates the Gauss variant with default settings; `GaussianRulePlan::gauss_legendre(n)` abbreviates the Gauss–Legendre rule for $[-1,1]$.
The defaults of `GaussianRuleOptions` use relative machine epsilon for deflation without an absolute floor and at most $30\max(n,1)$ QL sweeps.
Change them with `with_deflation_tolerance` and `with_execution_options`; the deflation tolerance is not an error bound for nodes or weights.
Reaching the iteration limit gives `ConvergenceFailure`; weights that are not positive and finite or nodes that are not strictly monotonic give `NumericalInstability`.
No partial Plan is returned.

## Classical weight families and integration intervals

Select the family with `OrthogonalFamily` in `scioxide-poly`.
Each family's standard interval is a `GaussianDomain` (`UnitInterval`, `NonnegativeRealLine`, or `RealLine`), returned by `domain()`.
`nodes()` and `weights()` refer to this standard domain, and `zeroth_moment()` returns $\mu_0$.

| Family | Domain | Weight function $w(t)$ | $\mu_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Legendre` | $[-1,1]$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| `ChebyshevT` | $[-1,1]$ | $(1-t^2)^{-1/2}$ | $\pi$ |
| `ChebyshevU` | $[-1,1]$ | $(1-t^2)^{1/2}$ | $\pi/2$ |
| `Jacobi { alpha, beta }` | $[-1,1]$ | $(1-t)^\alpha(1+t)^\beta$ | $2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta+1)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+2)$ |
| `Gegenbauer { lambda }` | $[-1,1]$ | $(1-t^2)^{\lambda-1/2}$ | $\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\lambda+\frac12)/\Gamma(\lambda+1)$ |
| `Laguerre` | $[0,\infty)$ | $e^{-t}$ | $1$ |
| `GeneralizedLaguerre { alpha }` | $[0,\infty)$ | $t^\alpha e^{-t}$ | $\Gamma(\alpha+1)$ |
| `HermiteH` | $(-\infty,\infty)$ | $e^{-t^2}$ | $\sqrt\pi$ |
| `HermiteHe` | $(-\infty,\infty)$ | $e^{-t^2/2}$ | $\sqrt{2\pi}$ |

Use `integrate_weighted(f, support)` or `integrate_weighted_into(f, support, out, &mut workspace, scope)` to integrate with a weighted family.
Choose `GaussianSupport` to match the family's domain.

- `GaussianSupport::Interval([a, b])` is for $[-1,1]$ families; it maps with $x=c+ht$ (midpoint $c$, half-width $h$) to compute $\int_a^b f(x)\,w(t(x))\,dx$.
  Weights are multiplied by $h$.
  The exception is `ChebyshevT`, which computes the integral $\int_a^b f(x)/\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}\,dx$ with physical weight $1/\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}$ without multiplying weights by $h$.
- `GaussianSupport::HalfLine(c)` is for Laguerre families and computes integrals with weight $(x-c)^\alpha e^{-(x-c)}$.
  Nodes shift by $c$ and weights remain unchanged.
- `GaussianSupport::RealLine` is for Hermite families; neither nodes nor weights change.

The callback returns only the factor excluding the weight.
`FixedRule::integrate` and `integrate_into` accept only families with weight identically 1 (`Legendre`, $\alpha=\beta=0$ of `Jacobi`, and $\lambda=1/2$ of `Gegenbauer`); other families give `InvalidInput`.
Create the workspace with `FixedRule::workspace(components)` for either form.
[Quantum harmonic oscillator expectations and degree of exactness of Gauss–Hermite quadrature](../../examples/quantum-oscillator-expectations/) gives a detailed exactness check.

## Radau and Lobatto rules

Collocation and boundary value problems may require endpoints among the nodes.
Each prescribed node removes one degree of freedom and lowers the degree of exactness by one.

- The Gauss rule (`GaussianRuleVariant::Gauss`) excludes endpoints and has degree of exactness $2n-1$.
- Radau rules (`RadauLeft`, `RadauRight`) include $-1$ or $+1$ and have degree of exactness $2n-2$.
- The Lobatto rule (`Lobatto`) includes both endpoints, has degree of exactness $2n-3$, and requires $n\ge2$.

These variants support only `Legendre` and `Jacobi` families; other families give `InvalidInput`.
Construction uses Golub's method, changing only the last row of the Jacobi matrix.
Radau changes only $\alpha_{n-1}$ to make the prescribed endpoint an eigenvalue.
Lobatto determines both $\alpha_{n-1}$ and $\beta_{n-1}$ from two simultaneous linear equations making $\pm1$ eigenvalues together.
Since computed eigenvalues agree with endpoints only to rounding accuracy, first check that their discrepancy is within $8\,n\max(\tau,\varepsilon)\|T\|_\infty$ ($\tau$ is the deflation tolerance and $T$ the modified matrix), then set nodes exactly to $\pm1$.
A larger discrepancy causes preparation to return `NumericalInstability`.

## Stability and error estimation

Gaussian weights are all positive, so their sum of absolute values is $\Lambda_n=\sum_i w_i=\mu_0$, and function-value errors $\delta$ are amplified by summation to at most $\mu_0\delta$.
The amplification bound is $\mu_0$ independently of $n$.
The weighted sum $\sum_i w_i f(x_i)$ uses compensated summation; products that overflow or underflow trigger summation with an extended exponent range and a single final rounding.
`GaussianRulePlan` returns no error estimate and terminates with `FixedRuleCompleted`.
Success means the sum at the specified nodes has been computed, without an accuracy claim.
Check accuracy by comparing node counts, or use a [Gauss–Kronrod pair](../adaptive-quadrature/) if an error estimate is needed.

The next cell integrates the monomial $x^k$ over $[-1,1]$ with an $n$-node Legendre rule, plotting the difference from the exact value $2/(k+1)$ (even $k$) or 0.
Inputs are node count $n$ and variant.
The shaded region extends through `exactness_degree()`; error should be at rounding level inside it and increase at the first even degree outside.
The table shows degree of exactness, maximum error through that degree, error at the next even degree, `zeroth_moment()`, smallest node, and the difference between the weight sum and $\mu_0$.
Degrees with exactly zero error are omitted from the logarithmic plot and counted in the last table column.

```rust
use scioxide_integrate::{FixedRule, GaussianRulePlan, GaussianRuleVariant};
use scioxide_poly::OrthogonalFamily;

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let digits16 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 16 });
let n = node_count as usize;
let variant = match endpoint_variant.as_str() {
    "RadauLeft" => GaussianRuleVariant::RadauLeft,
    "RadauRight" => GaussianRuleVariant::RadauRight,
    "Lobatto" => GaussianRuleVariant::Lobatto,
    _ => GaussianRuleVariant::Gauss,
};
let plan =
    GaussianRulePlan::with_options(OrthogonalFamily::Legendre, n, variant, Default::default())
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let interval = Interval::new(-1.0, 1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let degree = plan.exactness_degree();

// Integrate monomials x^k on [−1, 1] and compare with the exact value 2/(k+1) (even k) or 0.

let mut errors = Vec::new();
let mut dropped = 0_usize;
for k in 0..=2 * n + 8 {
    let monomial = |x: f64| Ok(RealScalar::powi(x, k as i32));
    let (value, _) = plan
        .integrate(&monomial, interval)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let exact = if k % 2 == 0 {
        2.0 / (k as f64 + 1.0)
    } else {
        0.0
    };
    let error = (value - exact).abs();
    // Do not plot degrees with exactly zero error (such as odd degrees of symmetric rules) logarithmically; show their count in the table.
    if error > 0.0 {
        curve.push([k as f64, error]);
    } else {
        dropped += 1;
    }
    errors.push(error);
}

        "annotations": [
            { "kind": "range", "axis": "x", "from": 0.0, "to": degree as f64, "label": "Through the degree of exactness" }
        ]
    })
);

// The first even degree with error after the degree of exactness is m + 1 or m + 2.
let next_even = if degree % 2 == 0 {
    degree + 2
} else {
    degree + 1
};
let within = errors[..=degree].iter().fold(0.0_f64, |m, &e| m.max(e));
// The displayed weight sum uses f64 summation; Plan quadrature uses compensated summation.
let weight_sum: f64 = plan.weights().iter().sum();
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Degree of exactness [-]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Maximum error through that degree [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Error at the next even degree [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "μ₀ [-]", "type": "number", "format": digits16 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Smallest node [-]", "type": "number", "format": digits16 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Σw_i − μ₀ [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Degrees omitted for zero error [count]", "type": "integer" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    vec![(
        degree,
        within,
        errors[next_even],
        plan.zeroth_moment(),
        plan.nodes()[0],
        weight_sum - plan.zeroth_moment(),

    )],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "exactness" })

```

For symmetric rules, odd-degree error is exactly 0, so most omitted degrees are odd.
The weight sum agreed with $\mu_0=2$ to rounding accuracy.
Selecting Lobatto makes the smallest node $-1$ and lowers the degree of exactness to 5.
With 8-node RadauLeft, error remained below $10^{-15}$ through degree 14.

## Clenshaw–Curtis rules

The Clenshaw–Curtis rule sets $x=\cos\theta$ and interpolates at the Chebyshev extrema

$$
x_k = \cos\frac{k\pi}{n}, \qquad k=0,1,\dots,n
$$

using $n+1$ nodes for $f$, then integrates the interpolating polynomial.
Writing the interpolant as $f(x)\approx\sum_{j=0}^{n}{}''\,a_j T_j(x)$ ($\sum''$ halves the first and last terms), the integral follows from

$$
\int_{-1}^1 T_j(x)\,dx = \begin{cases}\dfrac{2}{1-j^2} & (j\text{ even})\\ 0 & (j\text{ odd})\end{cases}
$$


All weights are positive, both endpoints are included, and doubling $n$ reuses every previous node (nesting).
Nesting also makes it useful as a one-dimensional rule for [sparse grids](../sparse-grids-and-qmc/).

`ClenshawCurtis::new(n)` constructs a rule with $n+1$ nodes.
Weights are computed once from a closed-form cosine sum using `f64` compensated summation ($O(n^2)$), then rounded to the scalar type.
Computing coefficients $a_j$ by a discrete cosine transform (DCT-I) generally reduces this to $O(n\log n)$, but this crate does not use that method.
The degree of exactness is $n$ (`degree()`); for even $n$, symmetry also makes odd functions of degree $n+1$ exact.
No error estimate is returned.

Its degree of exactness is about half that of Gauss, but the convergence-rate difference depends on the smoothness of $f$.
If $f$ is analytic inside the ellipse with foci $\pm1$ and sum of semimajor and semiminor axes $\rho>1$ (the Bernstein ellipse $E_\rho$), the errors of an $n$-node Gauss rule and Clenshaw–Curtis decay as $O(\rho^{-2n})$ and $O(\rho^{-n})$, respectively (Trefethen, *SIAM Review* 50(1), 2008).
For such very smooth $f$, Gauss is advantageous at sufficiently large node counts.
If $f$ is only $C^k$, or $E_\rho$ is thin (a singularity lies near the real axis), their errors are comparable.

The next cell computes the characteristic function of the semicircle density $\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^2}$,

$$
\varphi(\kappa) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^1 \sqrt{1-x^2}\,\cos(\kappa x)\,dx = \frac{2J_1(\kappa)}{\kappa}
$$

using three methods.
Gauss–Legendre and Clenshaw–Curtis receive the whole density; Gauss–Chebyshev U (weight $\sqrt{1-x^2}$) receives only $\frac2\pi\cos(\kappa x)$ through `integrate_weighted`.
The input is the wavenumber $\kappa$.
The reference is $J_1(\kappa)$ using `bessel_j` from `scioxide-special`.
Exactly zero-error points are omitted from the plot and counted by rule in the second table.
Since $\sqrt{1-x^2}$ is not differentiable at endpoints, the two unweighted rules are expected to converge only algebraically.

```rust
//| id: integrate-gaussian-weight-function
//| ref: integrate-gaussian-weight-function
//| title: "Compare convergence with and without putting the endpoint square root into the Gaussian weight"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-integrate, scioxide-poly, scioxide-special]
//| inputs:
//|   wave_number: {"type":"range","label":"Wavenumber κ [-]","description":"Increase the oscillation frequency of cos(κx).","min":1,"max":40,"step":1,"value":10}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"fast","label":"κ = 40","description":"Faster oscillation requires more nodes even for the weighted rule, but after resolving it, error falls to rounding level.","inputs":{"wave_number":40}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/2/3","range":{"min":0,"max":1e-14}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/2/1","range":{"min":1e-7,"max":1e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/2/2","range":{"min":1e-7,"max":1e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"dropped","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"min":0,"max":63}}
//|   - case: fast
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/3/3","range":{"min":0,"max":1e-13}}
use scioxide_core::{Interval, RealScalar};
use scioxide_integrate::{ClenshawCurtis, FixedRule, GaussianRulePlan, GaussianSupport};
use scioxide_poly::OrthogonalFamily;

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let kappa = wave_number;
// Characteristic function φ(κ) = 2 J₁(κ)/κ of the semicircle density (2/π)√(1 − x²) (reference from scioxide-special).
let exact = 2.0 * scioxide_special::bessel_j(1.0, kappa).map_err(|e| e.to_string())? / kappa;
let interval = Interval::new(-1.0, 1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let scale = 2.0 / std::f64::consts::PI;
// Pass the whole density to unweighted rules; pass the factor excluding √(1 − x²) to Chebyshev U.
let full = |x: f64| Ok(scale * RealScalar::sqrt(1.0 - x * x) * RealScalar::cos(kappa * x));
let smooth = |x: f64| Ok(scale * RealScalar::cos(kappa * x));

let mut curves = [Vec::new(), Vec::new(), Vec::new()];
let mut dropped = [0_usize; 3];
let mut rows = Vec::new();
for n in 2..=64_usize {
    let legendre = GaussianRulePlan::gauss_legendre(n).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let chebyshev =
        GaussianRulePlan::new(OrthogonalFamily::ChebyshevU, n).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let clenshaw = ClenshawCurtis::new(n - 1).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let values = [
        legendre
            .integrate(&full, interval)
            .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?
            .0,
        clenshaw
            .integrate(&full, interval)
            .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?
            .0,
        chebyshev
            .integrate_weighted(&smooth, GaussianSupport::Interval(interval))
            .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?
            .0,
    ];
    let errors = values.map(|value| (value - exact).abs());
    for ((curve, count), &error) in curves.iter_mut().zip(&mut dropped).zip(&errors) {
        // Do not plot zero-error points logarithmically; show their counts in the second table.
        if error > 0.0 {
            curve.push([n as f64, error]);
        } else {
            *count += 1;
        }
    }
    if [8, 16, 32, 64].contains(&n) {
        rows.push((n, errors[0], errors[1], errors[2]));
    }
}
let names = [
    "Gauss–Legendre (unweighted)",
    "Clenshaw–Curtis (unweighted)",
    "Gauss–Chebyshev U (weighted)",
];
let series: Vec<_> = names
    .iter()
    .zip(curves)
    .map(|(name, points)| serde_json::json!({ "name": name, "points": points }))
    .collect();
emit_line_chart!(
    &series,
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Node count n [nodes]",
        "yLabel": "|Q − φ(κ)| [-]",
        "yType": "log",
        "xMin": 2.0,
        "xMax": 64.0,
        "yMin": 1e-17,
        "yMax": 1.0
    })
);
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Node count n [nodes]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Gauss–Legendre error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Clenshaw–Curtis error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Gauss–Chebyshev U error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "errors" })
);
let dropped_columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Rule" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Points omitted for zero error [points]", "type": "integer" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    dropped_columns,
    names.iter().zip(dropped).collect::<Vec<_>>(),
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "dropped" })
);
```


At the default $\kappa=10$, Gauss–Legendre and Clenshaw–Curtis errors were $1.3\times10^{-5}$ and $1.6\times10^{-5}$ with 32 nodes and $1.6\times10^{-6}$ and $1.9\times10^{-6}$ with 64; doubling nodes reduced error to about $1/8$ ($O(n^{-3})$).
For integrands with nonsmooth endpoints, the two rules are comparable despite their different degrees of exactness.
Gauss–Chebyshev U, with the weight handled by the rule, gave error $4\times10^{-14}$ with 16 nodes and reached rounding level with 32.
At $\kappa=40$, all rules have large errors until oscillations are resolved (around 16 nodes), but the weighted rule gives $3\times10^{-9}$ with 32 nodes and reaches rounding level with 64.

## Summary

An $n$-node Gauss rule places nodes at orthogonal polynomial zeros and integrates exactly through degree $2n-1$, with positive weights summing to $\mu_0$.
`GaussianRulePlan` prepares nodes and weights once from a Jacobi eigenvalue problem, using a Christoffel-type formula to retain small weights for Hermite families.
Use weighted families by passing `GaussianSupport` to `integrate_weighted` and returning only the factor excluding the weight from the callback.
Radau and Lobatto variants support only Legendre and Jacobi families; each included endpoint lowers the degree of exactness by one.
Clenshaw–Curtis has nested nodes; it converges more slowly than Gauss for analytic $f$ but gives comparable accuracy for $f$ of limited smoothness.
