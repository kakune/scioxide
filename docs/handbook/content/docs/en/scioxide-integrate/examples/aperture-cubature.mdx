Optical flux through an aperture, heat flux from a radiating surface, and flow through a channel cross-section are double integrals of intensity over a surface.
When intensity is close to a polynomial, a product of one-dimensional Gauss rules integrates it with few points, but adding a narrow structure not aligned with the coordinate axes can make product-rule errors nonmonotonic as the point count increases.
This example computes transmission through an aperture with a rotated narrow obstruction using tensor-product Gauss–Legendre and adaptive Genz–Malik quadrature, comparing against closed forms and a higher-order product rule.

## Aperture intensity and transmitted power

For aperture intensity $I(x, y)$ [W/m²] and coordinates in [m], the transmitted power is

$$
P = \iint_\Omega I(x, y)\,dx\,dy
$$

with units [W].
Below, take a square aperture $\Omega = [-1, 1]^2$ and nondimensionalize coordinates by its half-width and intensity by the central intensity.
Transmitted power is also dimensionless, in units of central intensity times squared half-width.

Define the unobstructed intensity, which decreases smoothly to 0 at the edges, as

$$
B(x, y) = (1 - x^2)(1 - y^2)
$$


Since $B$ is a product of functions of $x$ and $y$, Fubini's theorem gives

$$
P_0 = \left(\int_{-1}^{1}(1 - x^2)\,dx\right)^2 = \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{16}{9}
$$


Because $B$ has degree 2 in each variable, Gauss–Legendre with $n$ points per axis (exact through degree $2n - 1$) reproduces $P_0$ to rounding error for $n \ge 2$.

## Rotated obstruction

Place a narrow obstruction in the aperture, rotated by angle $\theta$.
Define coordinates $u$ along and $v$ across the obstruction by

$$
u = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta, \qquad v = -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta
$$

and let the transverse half-width be $w$, longitudinal scale $\ell = 0.65$, and central obstruction depth $d \in [0, 1)$.
The transmittance and intensity are

$$
T(x, y) = 1 - d\exp\left[-\left(\frac{v}{w}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{u}{\ell}\right)^2\right], \qquad I(x, y) = B(x, y)\,T(x, y)
$$


For $0 < \theta < 90^\circ$, an $xy$ cross term appears in the exponent, so $I$ is neither separable nor polynomial.

At $\theta = 0$, $u = x$ and $v = y$, making the obstruction term also a product of functions of $x$ and $y$.
$J(s) = \int_{-1}^{1}(1 - x^2)e^{-x^2/s^2}\,dx$ is

$$
J(s) = s\sqrt{\pi}\,\operatorname{erf}(1/s)\left(1 - \frac{s^2}{2}\right) + s^2 e^{-1/s^2}
$$

in terms of the error function, giving the closed form for transmitted power

$$
P(\theta = 0) = \frac{16}{9} - d\,J(\ell)\,J(w)
$$


At $\theta = 90^\circ$, the roles of $\ell$ and $w$ simply interchange, so symmetry of $B$ gives the same $P$ as at $\theta = 0$.
For rotated cases, use a product rule with 96 points per axis (9216 total) as the reference.
This is also an estimate: check its accuracy against the closed form at $\theta = 0$ and against adaptive quadrature for rotated cases.

## Product rules and adaptive Genz–Malik quadrature

`ProductRule` forms a Cartesian product of one-dimensional fixed rules applied along each axis; $n$ points per axis require $n^2$ grid evaluations.
The Gauss–Legendre rule from `GaussianRulePlan` has no embedded rule, so the product rule returns no error estimate and terminates with `FixedRuleCompleted`.
Grid locations are independent of the integrand shape, and changing $n$ moves every point.
The number of grid points inside the narrow obstruction varies with $n$, so error need not decrease monotonically with $n$.

`CubaturePlan` performs adaptive quadrature in two or more dimensions using a degree-7 Genz–Malik rule and its embedded degree-5 rule.
In two dimensions it evaluates 17 points per region and estimates the regional error as four times the difference between the two rules.
Until the sum of error estimates meets the tolerance, it bisects the region with the largest estimated error along the axis with the largest fourth difference.
This error estimate is heuristic (`ErrorReliability::Heuristic`), not an upper bound.
Meeting the tolerance gives termination `HeuristicToleranceReached`.

The following block provides definitions shared by three cells.
`Aperture` holds obstruction parameters $(d, w, \theta)$; `baseline` returns $B$, and `intensity` returns $I = BT$.
`product_gauss` integrates with a Gauss–Legendre product rule using $n$ points per axis, and `axis_aligned_power` computes the closed form for $\theta = 0$ using `erf` from `scioxide-special`.

```rust
//| id: integrate-aperture-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Aperture intensity and tensor-product Gauss–Legendre quadrature"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-integrate, scioxide-special]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, Interval, RealScalar};
use scioxide_integrate::{GaussianRulePlan, IntegrationReport, ProductRule};

/// Longitudinal scale ℓ along the obstruction [-].
pub(crate) const LONG_SCALE: f64 = 0.65;

/// Obstruction depth d, transverse half-width w, and angle θ [rad].
#[derive(Clone, Copy)]
pub(crate) struct Aperture {
    pub(crate) depth: f64,
    pub(crate) width: f64,
    pub(crate) angle: f64,
}

impl Aperture {
    /// Unobstructed intensity B(x, y) = (1 − x²)(1 − y²).
    pub(crate) fn baseline(x: f64, y: f64) -> f64 {
        (1.0 - x * x) * (1.0 - y * y)
    }

    /// Intensity I(x, y) = B(x, y) T(x, y), T = 1 − d exp(−(v/w)² − (u/ℓ)²).
    pub(crate) fn intensity(&self, x: f64, y: f64) -> f64 {
        let (sine, cosine) = RealScalar::sin_cos(self.angle);
        let (u, v) = (cosine * x + sine * y, -sine * x + cosine * y);
        let exponent = RealScalar::powi(v / self.width, 2) + RealScalar::powi(u / LONG_SCALE, 2);
        Self::baseline(x, y) * (1.0 - self.depth * RealScalar::exp(-exponent))
    }

    /// Closed form at θ = 0: 16/9 − d J(ℓ) J(w).
    pub(crate) fn axis_aligned_power(&self) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
        Ok(16.0 / 9.0 - self.depth * moment(LONG_SCALE)? * moment(self.width)?)
    }
}

/// J(s) = ∫₋₁¹ (1 − x²) exp(−x²/s²) dx = s√π erf(1/s)(1 − s²/2) + s² exp(−1/s²).
fn moment(s: f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    let root_pi = RealScalar::sqrt(core::f64::consts::PI);
    let error_function = scioxide_special::erf(1.0 / s)?;
    Ok(
        s * root_pi * error_function * (1.0 - 0.5 * s * s)
            + s * s * RealScalar::exp(-1.0 / (s * s)),
    )
}

/// Square [−1, 1]².
pub(crate) fn square() -> Result<[Interval<f64>; 2], SciOxideError> {
    let side = Interval::new(-1.0, 1.0)?;
    Ok([side, side])
}

/// Integrate f with a Gauss–Legendre product rule using n points per axis. No error estimate is returned.
pub(crate) fn product_gauss(
    n: usize,
    f: &(dyn Fn(&[f64]) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> + Sync),
) -> Result<(f64, IntegrationReport<f64>), SciOxideError> {
    let rule = GaussianRulePlan::gauss_legendre(n)?;
    ProductRule::new(&rule, Default::default()).integrate(f, &square()?)
}
```

## Compare adaptive quadrature and product rules

The next cell takes the obstruction and reference from the previous cell and selects product-rule points per axis $n$ and adaptive Genz–Malik tolerance $\varepsilon$ (both absolute and relative).
The bisection limit is fixed at 4096.
The table gives transmitted power from both methods, differences from the reference, error estimates, evaluation counts, and terminations.
The outcome reports the adaptive Genz–Malik termination.

```rust
//| id: integrate-aperture-cubature
//| ref: integrate-aperture-cubature
//| title: "Compute power through a rotated obstruction with product and adaptive rules"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [integrate-aperture-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-integrate, scioxide-special]
//| dependsOn:
//|   field: integrate-aperture-reference

//|   product_order: {"type":"range","label":"Product-rule points per axis n","description":"Change the product-rule evaluation count n².","min":2,"max":16,"step":1,"value":6}
```

At the defaults ($n = 6$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$), the product rule with 6 points per axis (36 total) differs from the reference by $3.0 \times 10^{-2}$.
Termination is `FixedRuleCompleted` with no error estimate, so this difference would go unnoticed without a reference.
Adaptive Genz–Malik reaches `HeuristicToleranceReached` after 31739 evaluations, with a difference from the reference of $4 \times 10^{-11}$.
Agreement to this difference between methods with different node placements suggests the reference error is also of this order or smaller.
The error estimate is $2.6 \times 10^{-7}$, and the method stops just below tolerance $\varepsilon(1 + |P|)$.
The actual difference is four orders of magnitude smaller than the estimate, so this heuristic estimate overestimates the observed difference.

Setting $\theta = 0$ in the previous cell aligns the obstruction with the grid axes and increases the difference for 6 points per axis to $0.12$.
There are no nodes on the obstruction centerline $y = 0$, so integration uses only nodes straddling the intensity trough.
Adaptive quadrature then needs only 7225 evaluations.
An axis-aligned obstruction can apparently be covered with fewer regions using only axis-aligned bisections.
Choosing a narrow, deep obstruction ($w = 0.04$, $d = 0.9$) in the previous cell increases adaptive evaluations to 73695, with difference $9 \times 10^{-12}$ from the reference.
At $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$, adaptive quadrature reaches the 4096-bisection limit after 139281 evaluations and stops with `BudgetLimit(Subdivision)`, giving outcome partial.
The reference difference is still only $6 \times 10^{-14}$, but the computation cannot claim accuracy.

At $\theta = 0$, the 96-point-per-axis product rule (9216 total) agrees with the closed form to $2.2 \times 10^{-16}$, more accurately than adaptive quadrature with 7225 evaluations (difference $6.6 \times 10^{-11}$).
Because the integrand is analytic, Gauss-rule error decreases rapidly once the point count is sufficiently large.
Adaptive quadrature's advantage is that it refines without prior knowledge of the required point count and returns an error estimate and termination.

## Product-rule point count and error

The next cell takes the first cell's obstruction parameters and reference and integrates both the obstructed field $I$ and unobstructed field $B$ with product rules having $n = 1, \ldots, 16$ points per axis.
The error for $I$ is its difference from the reference; for $B$, it is the difference from $16/9$.
Exactly zero errors cannot appear on the logarithmic axis and are omitted from the chart but retained in the table.

```rust
//| id: integrate-aperture-order-study
//| ref: integrate-aperture-order-study
//| title: "Tensor-product Gauss–Legendre point count and error"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [integrate-aperture-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-integrate, scioxide-special]
//| dependsOn:
//|   field: integrate-aperture-reference
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"orders","path":"/rows/1/3","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"orders","path":"/rows/2/2","approx":{"value":0.3847,"atol":1e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"orders","path":"/rows/15/2","approx":{"value":1.0009e-5,"rtol":1e-3}}
use scioxide_core::RealScalar;

let number = |key: &str| field[key].as_f64().ok_or(format!("Missing {key}"));
let aperture = Aperture {
    depth: number("depth")?,
    width: number("width")?,
    angle: RealScalar::to_radians(number("angle")?),
};
let reference = number("reference")?;
let intensity = |p: &[f64]| Ok(aperture.intensity(p[0], p[1]));
let baseline = |p: &[f64]| Ok(Aperture::baseline(p[0], p[1]));

let (mut obstructed, mut polynomial, mut rows) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new(), Vec::new());
for n in 1_usize..=16 {
    let obstructed_error =
        (product_gauss(n, &intensity).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?.0 - reference).abs();
    let polynomial_error =
        (product_gauss(n, &baseline).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?.0 - 16.0 / 9.0).abs();
    if obstructed_error > 0.0 {
        obstructed.push([n as f64, obstructed_error]);
    }
    if polynomial_error > 0.0 {
        polynomial.push([n as f64, polynomial_error]);
    }
    rows.push((n, n * n, obstructed_error, polynomial_error));
}
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Obstructed field I", "points": obstructed }),
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Unobstructed field B", "points": polynomial }),
    ],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Points per axis n",
        "yLabel": "Error [-]",
        "yType": "log",
        "xMin": 1.0,
        "xMax": 16.0
    })
);
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Points per axis n", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Evaluations n² [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Difference of I from reference [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Difference of B from 16/9 [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "orders" })
);
```

For the default field, the error in unobstructed $B$ reaches the $10^{-15}$ rounding level at $n = 2$ (4 points) and remains there.
The error in obstructed $I$ fluctuates as $0.25$, $0.098$, $0.38$, $0.028$, $0.094$, and $0.030$ for $n = 1$ through 6.
Changing $n$ moves every node and changes how many fall in the obstruction's trough.
From $0.024$ at $n = 7$ (49 points), error decreases monotonically to $1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ at $n = 16$ (256 points).
The decrease is approximately geometric.
Gauss-rule error is governed by the error of polynomial approximation to the integrand; a smaller obstruction half-width $w$ requires higher degree, so narrowing $w$ slows convergence.
At $n = 16$, setting $w = 0.24$ in the first cell reduces error to $4.3 \times 10^{-12}$, while setting $w = 0.04$ leaves it at $7.8 \times 10^{-4}$.
Close results at two low-order $n$ values are not evidence of convergence.

## Summary

A separable polynomial field can be integrated to rounding level with few points when the Gauss rule on each axis has sufficient degree of exactness.
For fields with narrow structures not aligned with the axes, low-order product-rule errors are nonmonotonic and lack an estimate; check them against a higher-point reference such as 96 points per axis.
Adaptive `CubaturePlan` refines regions to follow local variation and returns a heuristic error estimate and `HeuristicToleranceReached`, but a high-order product rule can be more accurate with fewer evaluations for analytic integrands.
Treat `BudgetLimit(Subdivision)` from a bisection limit as partial and review the tolerance or limit.
Provide at least one independent check of the reference itself, such as the axis-aligned closed form or agreement with adaptive quadrature using different node placements.
