The total power radiated by an antenna is the power flux through a distant sphere integrated over all directions.
This example computes radiated power from a thin center-fed dipole as a unit-sphere integral using `LebedevRule` and `SphereProductRule`.
It compares length-dependent power, radiation resistance, and directivity with closed forms and examines how tilting the antenna axis changes each rule's accuracy.

See [simplex and sphere quadrature rules](oxref:integrate-sphere-rules) for their construction, degree of exactness, and node-count estimates.

## Radiation intensity and power

Assume a thin center-fed wire of total length $\ell$ carries sinusoidal current with wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, where $\lambda$ is wavelength:

$$
I(z) = I_0 \sin\!\left(k\left(\frac{\ell}{2} - |z|\right)\right), \qquad |z| \le \frac{\ell}{2}
$$


Here $I_0$ is the standing-wave current amplitude; below take $I_0 = 1$ A.
For $\ell \ge \lambda/2$, $I_0$ equals the maximum current along the wire; for $\ell < \lambda/2$, the maximum is $I_0\sin(k\ell/2)$ at the feed point, less than $I_0$.
The thin-dipole approximation neglects wire thickness and fixes the current distribution to this form.
For angle $\theta$ between the antenna axis and observation direction, the far-field power per unit solid angle (radiation intensity) is

$$
U(\theta) = \frac{\eta I_0^2}{8\pi^2}\,F(\theta)^2, \qquad F(\theta) = \frac{\cos\!\left(\frac{k\ell}{2}\cos\theta\right) - \cos\frac{k\ell}{2}}{\sin\theta}
$$

with units W/sr.
Here $\eta = 376.73\ \Omega$ is the free-space wave impedance.
Define radiated power $P$, directivity $D$, and radiation resistance $R$ by

$$
P = \oint_{S^2} U\,d\Omega, \qquad D = \frac{4\pi\,U_{\max}}{P}, \qquad R = \frac{2P}{I_0^2}
$$


The directivity $D$ is the ratio of maximum intensity to isotropic intensity; $10\log_{10}D$ is expressed in dBi.
The resistance $R$ is referenced to amplitude $I_0$; at $\ell = \lambda/2$, the feed-point current is exactly $I_0$.

At the poles $\theta = 0, \pi$, both numerator and denominator of $F$ vanish.
Writing $c = \cos\theta$ and using $\cos(ac) - \cos a = 2\sin\frac{a(1 + c)}{2}\sin\frac{a(1 - c)}{2}$ ($a = k\ell/2$) together with $\sin^2\theta = (1 - c)(1 + c)$ gives

$$
F^2 = 4\,(1 - c)(1 + c)\,S(1 - c)^2\,S(1 + c)^2, \qquad S(t) = \frac{\sin(at/2)}{t}
$$


Since $S$ extends continuously to $a/2$ at $t = 0$, $F^2$ is an entire function of $c$ and vanishes at the poles.
Lebedev rules include the regular octahedron's vertices, including poles $(0, 0, \pm1)$, so evaluating without this form produces 0/0.
Since $F^2$ is not polynomial, neither rule is exact at any finite degree, but error decreases rapidly with degree.

## Closed form

The power $P$ is a one-dimensional integral in $\theta$ with a closed form in sine integral $\mathrm{Si}$ and cosine integral $\mathrm{Ci}$.
Using $\mathrm{Cin}(x) = \gamma + \ln x - \mathrm{Ci}(x)$, where $\gamma$ is Euler's constant, and $x = k\ell$ gives

$$
P = \frac{\eta I_0^2}{4\pi}\left[\mathrm{Cin}(x) + \frac{\sin x}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{Si}(2x) - 2\,\mathrm{Si}(x)\bigr) + \frac{\cos x}{2}\bigl(2\,\mathrm{Cin}(x) - \mathrm{Cin}(2x)\bigr)\right]
$$

(the expression in C. A. Balanis, *Antenna Theory: Analysis and Design*, 4th ed., Wiley, 2016, §4.5, rewritten using $\mathrm{Cin}$).
For a half-wave dipole $\ell = \lambda/2$, $x = \pi$, $\sin x = 0$, and $\cos x = -1$, giving

$$
P = \frac{\eta I_0^2}{8\pi}\,\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi), \qquad R = \frac{\eta}{4\pi}\,\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi) \approx 73.08\ \Omega, \qquad D = \frac{4}{\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi)} \approx 1.641
$$

as in §4.6 of the same reference.
Here $\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi) = 2.43765$ and $D$ corresponds to 2.15 dBi.
The maximum intensity occurs at $\theta = \pi/2$, where $F = 1$.
scioxide-special provides `si` and `ci` but not $\mathrm{Cin}$; construct it from `ci` using the definition above.
For small $x$, subtracting $\mathrm{Ci}(x)$ from $\gamma + \ln x$ loses digits, but at most 2 digits are lost over this example's range ($\ell/\lambda \ge 0.1$).

The maximum $U_{\max}$ needed for directivity occurs at $\theta = \pi/2$ for $\ell \le \lambda$, but longer antennas have main beams that split obliquely.
The following cells use the maximum from a 4001-point scan of $c = \cos\theta$ as the reference.

The following definitions block is shared by three cells.
`pattern(a, c)` gives $F^2$; `intensity` gives $U$ from an observation unit vector and the antenna axis; `max_intensity` gives the scanned $U_{\max}$; `closed_form_power` gives the closed form of $P$; and `tilted_axis` gives an axis tilted from $z$ toward $x$.

```rust
//| id: integrate-dipole-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Dipole radiation intensity and closed-form radiated power"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-special]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, RealScalar};

/// Free-space wave impedance η [Ω].
pub(crate) const IMPEDANCE: f64 = 376.730_313_668;

/// Euler's constant γ.
const EULER_GAMMA: f64 = 0.577_215_664_901_532_9;

/// S(t) = sin(a t / 2) / t, with limit a / 2 at t = 0.
fn half_sine_ratio(a: f64, t: f64) -> f64 {
    if t == 0.0 {
        0.5 * a
    } else {
        RealScalar::sin(0.5 * a * t) / t
    }
}

/// F² = 4 (1 − c)(1 + c) S(1 − c)² S(1 + c)², where c is the cosine of the angle to the axis and a = kℓ/2.
pub(crate) fn pattern(a: f64, c: f64) -> f64 {
    // Rounding can make the dot product of unit vectors slightly exceed ±1.
    let c = RealScalar::clamp(c, -1.0, 1.0);
    let (minus, plus) = (1.0 - c, 1.0 + c);
    let s = half_sine_ratio(a, minus) * half_sine_ratio(a, plus);
    4.0 * minus * plus * s * s
}

/// Radiation intensity U = η I₀² F² / (8π²) [W/sr] (I₀ = 1 A, length = ℓ/λ).
pub(crate) fn intensity(length: f64, axis: &[f64; 3], direction: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let pi = <f64 as RealScalar>::PI;
    let c = axis[0] * direction[0] + axis[1] * direction[1] + axis[2] * direction[2];
    IMPEDANCE / (8.0 * pi * pi) * pattern(pi * length, c)
}

/// Reference U_max: maximum from a 4001-point scan of c = cos θ.
pub(crate) fn max_intensity(length: f64) -> f64 {
    (0..=4000)
        .map(|i| {
            let c = -1.0 + f64::from(i) / 2000.0;
            let s = RealScalar::sqrt(1.0 - c * c);
            intensity(length, &[0.0, 0.0, 1.0], &[s, 0.0, c])
        })
        .fold(0.0, |a, b| RealScalar::max(a, b))
}

/// Cin(x) = γ + ln x − Ci(x).
fn cin(x: f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    Ok(EULER_GAMMA + RealScalar::ln(x) - scioxide_special::ci(x)?)
}

/// Closed-form radiated power P [W] (I₀ = 1 A, length = ℓ/λ).
pub(crate) fn closed_form_power(length: f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    let x = <f64 as RealScalar>::TAU * length;
    let (sine, cosine) = RealScalar::sin_cos(x);
    let si_terms = scioxide_special::si(2.0 * x)? - 2.0 * scioxide_special::si(x)?;
    let cin_terms = 2.0 * cin(x)? - cin(2.0 * x)?;
    let bracket = cin(x)? + 0.5 * sine * si_terms + 0.5 * cosine * cin_terms;
    Ok(IMPEDANCE / (4.0 * <f64 as RealScalar>::PI) * bracket)
}

/// Unit vector tilted by tilt [°] from the z axis toward the x axis.
pub(crate) fn tilted_axis(tilt: f64) -> [f64; 3] {
    let (sine, cosine) = RealScalar::sin_cos(RealScalar::to_radians(tilt));
    [sine, 0.0, cosine]
}
```

## Radiation power and resistance versus length

The next cell selects antenna length $\ell/\lambda$ and Lebedev degree $L$ and computes $P$ for an antenna aligned with the $z$ axis using `LebedevRule` and `SphereProductRule`.
For the same degree of exactness, the product rule uses $p = (L + 1)/2$ points in $\cos\theta$ and $m = L + 1$ azimuthal points.
The integrand receives a unit-sphere point $\boldsymbol u$ as a three-component slice; `integrate(&f, &[0.0; 3], 1.0)` applies it to a sphere of radius 1 centered at the origin.
Use radius 1 to integrate over solid angle.
On a sphere of radius $r$, weights scale by $r^2$.
Nonfinite center or radius returns `NonFinite`, and $r \le 0$ returns `InvalidInput`; nodes, area, or weights outside floating-point range, or nonzero area or weights rounding to 0, return `NumericalInstability`.
The table lists $P$, $R$, $D$, and relative errors against the closed form.

);

At the defaults (half wavelength, $L = 11$), the closed form gives $R = 73.079\ \Omega$ and $D = 1.6409$ (2.151 dBi), agreeing with the previous section.
The 50-node Lebedev rule has relative error $4\times10^{-9}$; the 72-node product rule of the same degree has $3\times10^{-8}$.
Since $D$ divides by $P$, the relative error in $D$ has the same magnitude as that in $P$.

The `full-wave` case ($\ell = \lambda$) gives $R = 198.95\ \Omega$ and $D = 2.411$.
This $R$ is referenced to the maximum current; a full-wave dipole has zero feed-point current, so it differs from the resistance seen at the feed.
The `long-coarse` case ($\ell = 3\lambda$) develops finer radiation lobes, and degree-11 rules have relative error of order $10^{-1}$.
In `long-fine`, increasing degree to 59 (1202 nodes) reduces relative error to the rounding level.
Longer antennas have higher-degree spherical-harmonic components in $F^2$.

## Accuracy under axis tilt

Rotating coordinates leaves a spherical polynomial of degree at most $L$ a polynomial of degree at most $L$.
A degree-$L$ rule therefore integrates the part of $F^2$ of degree at most $L$ exactly for every antenna-axis orientation.
Only the error from the remaining higher-degree components changes with tilt.

The product rule has an additional property.
Equally spaced azimuthal quadrature integrates azimuth-independent functions exactly for every $m$.
With the antenna aligned to the $z$ axis, $U$ depends only on $\cos\theta$, so product-rule error comes solely from Gauss–Legendre quadrature in $\cos\theta$, and even $m = 1$ suffices for azimuth.
Tilting makes $U$ depend on azimuth; with few azimuthal points, the product-rule error stagnates at a level set by that count.

The next cell selects antenna length $\ell/\lambda$ and axis tilt $\alpha$ (from $z$ toward $x$), then plots relative error in $P$ against node count for four rules.

- Lebedev rule (tilt $\alpha$): every tabulated degree from 3 to 131.
- Product rule $m = 2p$ (tilt $\alpha$): $p = 2, 3, \dots, 54$.
  Its degree of exactness is $2p - 1$.
- Product rule $m = 4$ (tilt 0) and product rule $m = 4$ (tilt $\alpha$): rules for axisymmetric integrands with azimuth fixed at 4 points.

The table gives the first node count where each rule reaches relative error at most $10^{-10}$, and its error at the largest node count.

At the default inputs ($\ell = 1.5\lambda$, $\alpha = 30°$), relative error $10^{-10}$ is reached at 230 nodes by Lebedev and at 242 nodes by the $m = 2p$ product rule.
Both rules treat every axis orientation alike, and their errors decrease rapidly with node count.
The four-azimuth-point product rule reaches $10^{-10}$ with 52 nodes when aligned, but at a tilt of $30°$ its relative error stagnates at $3.4\times10^{-2}$ and does not decrease with $p$.
An equally spaced $m$-point rule integrates $e^{ij\varphi}$ exactly to 0 when $j$ is not a multiple of $m$, but aliases components with $j$ a multiple of $m$ to the constant component; thus the $j = 4, 8, \dots$ components of tilted $U$ remain as error.

In the `aligned` case ($\alpha = 0°$), the four-azimuth-point and $m = 2p$ product rules agree in error at the same $p$, showing that azimuthal point count has no effect.
In `half-wave`, the 74-node Lebedev rule reaches $10^{-10}$, while the tilted four-azimuth-point rule stagnates at $2.7\times10^{-3}$.
For an axisymmetric integrand aligned with a coordinate axis, the product rule can be reduced to exploit that symmetry.
When the axis orientation changes with input, use a Lebedev rule or $m \ge 2p$ product rule with the same degree of exactness for every orientation.

## Lebedev nodes and negative weights

Some rules in the Lebedev–Laikov tables have negative weights.
In this implementation's tables, these are degrees 13 (74 nodes), 25 (230 nodes), and 27 (266 nodes).
Negative weights give $\sum_j |w_j| > 4\pi$, so rounding errors in integrand values accumulate with a factor greater than the weight sum $4\pi$.
At this example's scale the effect remains at rounding level, but it provides a reason to choose a positive-weight degree when available.

The next cell plots the selected Lebedev rule's nodes on the unit sphere.
It groups nodes into negative weights, positive weights inside the half-power beam ($U \ge U_{\max}/2$), and positive weights outside the beam.
It also draws the antenna axis using two points and an edge.
All three axes use the sphere radius as their unit.
Selecting two nodes displays their straight-line distance.
The table gives node count, negative-weight count, weight sum minus $4\pi$, $\sum_j |w_j|/(4\pi)$, in-beam node count, and relative error in $P$ for that rule.

//|   scene_length: {"type":"range","label":"Antenna length ℓ/λ [-]","description":"Change the half-power beam shape.","min":0.1,"max":3,"step":0.05,"value":0.5}
//|   scene_tilt: {"type":"range","label":"Axis tilt α [°]","description":"Tilt from the z axis toward the x axis.","min":0,"max":90,"step":5,"value":30}
//| cases:

//|   - {"id":"long-tilted","label":"ℓ = 1.5λ, α = 60°","description":"The main beam splits into oblique cones, moving in-beam nodes to either side of the axis.","inputs":{"scene_length":1.5,"scene_tilt":60}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"nodes","path":"/rows/0/1","equals":230}
//|       - {"artifact":"nodes","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":6}
//|       - {"artifact":"nodes","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"min":-1e-12,"max":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"nodes","path":"/rows/0/4","range":{"min":1.0001,"max":2}}
//|       - {"artifact":"nodes","path":"/rows/0/6","range":{"min":0,"max":1e-13}}
//|   - case: positive-weights
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"nodes","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":0}
//|       - {"artifact":"nodes","path":"/rows/0/4","approx":{"value":1,"atol":1e-12}}
//|   - case: long-tilted
//|     expect:

use scioxide_core::RealScalar;
use scioxide_integrate::{GeometricOptions, LebedevRule};

let degree: usize = scene_degree.parse().map_err(|_| "Degree is not an integer")?;
let rule =
    LebedevRule::<f64>::new(degree, GeometricOptions::default()).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let axis = tilted_axis(scene_tilt);
let half_power = 0.5 * max_intensity(scene_length);
let four_pi = 2.0 * <f64 as RealScalar>::TAU;
let (mut points, mut negative, mut in_beam) = (Vec::new(), 0_usize, 0_usize);
for (index, (u, &w)) in rule.nodes().chunks_exact(3).zip(rule.weights()).enumerate() {
    let group = if w < 0.0 {
        negative += 1;
        "negative"
    } else if intensity(scene_length, &axis, u) >= half_power {
        in_beam += 1;
        "beam"
    } else {
        "other"
    };
    points.push(serde_json::json!({
        "id": format!("node-{index}"),
        "position": [u[0], u[1], u[2]],
        "label": format!("w = {w:.4e}"),
        "group": group
    }));
}
// Show the antenna axis with two points at radius 1.3 and an edge.
for (id, sign) in [("axis-plus", 1.3), ("axis-minus", -1.3)] {
    points.push(serde_json::json!({
        "id": id,
        "position": [sign * axis[0], sign * axis[1], sign * axis[2]],
        "group": "axis"
    }));
}
let unit = |label: &str| serde_json::json!({ "label": label, "unit": "Sphere radius" });
emit_scene3d!(
    serde_json::json!({
        "points": points,
        "edges": [{ "from": "axis-plus", "to": "axis-minus" }],
        "groups": [
            { "id": "negative", "label": "Negative weight" },

            { "id": "axis", "label": "Antenna axis" }

    }),

);

let f = |u: &[f64]| Ok(intensity(scene_length, &axis, u));
let (power, _): (f64, _) = rule
    .integrate(&f, &[0.0; 3], 1.0)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let exact = closed_form_power(scene_length).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let sig6 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 6 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Degree L", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Nodes [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Negative weights [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Σw − 4π [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Σ|w| / 4π [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Nodes inside beam [count]", "type": "integer" },

]);

    degree,
    rule.node_count(),
    negative,
    weight_sum - four_pi,
    absolute_sum / four_pi,
    in_beam,
    (power - exact).abs() / exact,
);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    vec![row],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "nodes" })
);

At the default degree 25 (230 nodes), the six octahedron vertices have negative weights $-0.694$, with $\sum_j|w_j|/(4\pi) = 1.66$.
The weight sum differs from $4\pi$ by about $10^{-14}$, and half-wave $P$ agrees with the closed form at rounding level.
At degree 13 (74 nodes), the eight cube vertices have negative weights and $\sum_j|w_j|/(4\pi) = 1.47$; at degree 27 (266 nodes), 18 weights are negative and the ratio is 1.08.
For `positive-weights`, degree 41 (590 nodes), every weight is positive and the ratio is 1.
If integrand-value errors are bounded by $\delta$, the integral-error bound is $\delta\sum_j|w_j|$, so this ratio measures how much larger the bound is than for positive-weight rules.
This difference matters when the integrand contains measured values or results of another numerical calculation and $\delta$ exceeds rounding error.

For the half-wave dipole, the half-power-beam nodes form a broad band perpendicular to the axis, containing 142 of the 230 nodes.
In `long-tilted` ($\ell = 1.5\lambda$, $\alpha = 60°$), the main beam splits into two oblique cones and the in-beam node count falls to 92.

## Summary

A thin dipole's radiated power is the unit-sphere integral of intensity $U$; `integrate(&f, &[0.0; 3], 1.0)` on `LebedevRule` and `SphereProductRule` computes it over solid angle.
Rewriting $F^2$ as a product involving $(1 - c)(1 + c)$ and $\sin(at/2)/t$ avoids 0/0 even at the poles included among Lebedev nodes.
For a half-wave dipole, rules with a few dozen nodes agree with closed-form $R = \eta\,\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi)/(4\pi) = 73.08\ \Omega$ and $D = 1.641$ to about $10^{-8}$; longer antennas require higher degree.
Both rules exactly integrate components through degree $L$ regardless of axis orientation, but product rules with reduced azimuthal counts apply only to aligned axisymmetric cases.
Lebedev rules with negative weights (degrees 13, 25, 27) have $\sum_j|w_j|$ greater than $4\pi$, so choose a positive-weight degree for integrands whose values contain errors.
