Spectroscopic absorption lines and resonant-scattering cross-sections are represented by Lorentzian line shapes concentrated near their centers.
Line strength is determined by the area under the line shape, so numerical integration of narrow lines is common.
This example checks against a closed-form area how narrowing a line makes fixed rules miss its peak, how adaptive quadrature refines around it, and how supplying the peak location and width changes evaluation counts and missed features.

## Lorentzian line shape and closed-form area

Represent the measurement window with dimensionless coordinate $x \in [-1, 1]$.
For example, for a frequency $\nu$ window centered at $\nu_0$ with half-width $\Delta$, set $x = (\nu - \nu_0)/\Delta$.
Write the Lorentzian with center $c$, half-width at half-maximum $w > 0$, and peak value 1 as

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \left(\dfrac{x - c}{w}\right)^2}
$$


Since $f(c \pm w) = 1/2$, the full width at half-maximum is $2w$.
Both $x$ and $f$, and hence the area, are dimensionless.

Setting $u = (x - c)/w$ gives $dx = w\,du$ and antiderivative $w \arctan u$.
The area over the window is therefore

$$
I(c, w) = \int_{-1}^{1} f(x)\,dx = w\left[\arctan\frac{1 - c}{w} - \arctan\frac{-1 - c}{w}\right]
$$


If $c$ is far from the window edges and $w \ll 1 - |c|$, the two $\arctan$ values approach $\pm\pi/2$, giving $I \approx \pi w$.
As $c$ approaches an edge, one tail leaves the window and the area becomes smaller than $\pi w$.
The checks below use this closed form evaluated with the standard `f64` function `atan`.

## What fixed quadrature misses

An $n$-point Gauss–Legendre rule integrates polynomials through degree $2n - 1$ exactly ([Gaussian quadrature and Clenshaw–Curtis rules](../../algorithms/gaussian-and-clenshaw-curtis/)).
Since $f$ is not polynomial, the quality of polynomial approximation depends on how far $f$ extends analytically into the complex plane.
The poles of $f$ at $x = c \pm i w$ approach the real axis as $w$ decreases, rapidly worsening polynomial approximation.

If node spacing exceeds peak width $w$, few nodes lie inside the peak.
If every node falls in the flat tails, quadrature produces a value unrelated to area $\pi w$.
Fixed-rule termination `FixedRuleCompleted` only says that summing the prescribed nodes finished and does not reveal this error.

## Adaptive Gauss–Kronrod refinement

Passing `AdaptiveRule::GaussKronrod` to `AdaptiveIntegrator` uses the G7K15 pair: a seven-point Gauss rule and a 15-point Kronrod rule containing its nodes.
Regional error estimates come from the rule difference $|K - G|$ with scale correction.
Bisect the region with the largest estimate until the sum of all regional estimates is at most tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}|Q|$.
The initial region uses 15 evaluations; each bisection uses 30 evaluations across two children.

For a narrow peak, its containing region retains a large estimated error, concentrating bisections around the peak.
The bisection depth needed to shrink a region to peak width is approximately proportional to $\log_2(1/w)$.
Thus each tenfold reduction in $w$ adds an approximately constant number of evaluations.

Read termination as follows.

- `ToleranceReached`: the error estimate satisfies the tolerance.
  The estimate is not an upper bound.
- `BudgetLimit(Subdivision)`: the bisection-count limit stopped computation.
  An estimate over the whole interval is returned, but meeting tolerance cannot be claimed.
- `Stagnation`: rounding dominates the error estimate, or bisection no longer changes values.

No termination proves the absence of narrow features hidden between nodes.
If $w$ is so small that none of the initial 15 nodes touches the peak, both $K$ and $G$ integrate only small tail values, and the small error estimate permits `ToleranceReached`.
Without knowing a narrow feature's location, adaptive termination cannot distinguish this missed peak from success.

## Two ways to use peak location and width

If peak center $c$ and width $w$ are known, supply them to quadrature.

The first method is prior subdivision.
In one dimension, `CubaturePlan` uses the same G7K15 refinement as `AdaptiveIntegrator`; `with_breakpoints` supplies an initial partition.
Splitting at $x = c \pm w$ makes one initial region span the full width at half-maximum, ensuring that the central part of the peak is sampled.
However, in outer regions $f$ still varies on scales proportional to distance from the breakpoint, so bisections remain necessary there.
The outer regions become separate narrow structures with peak tails concentrated near an endpoint.

The second method is a change to local coordinates:

$$
x = c + w \sinh t, \qquad dx = w \cosh t\,dt
$$

This gives spacing of scale $w$ near $c$ that expands exponentially farther away.
The integral becomes

$$
I = \int_{t_-}^{t_+} f(c + w \sinh t)\, w \cosh t\,dt, \qquad t_\pm = \operatorname{arsinh}\frac{\pm 1 - c}{w}
$$

and for a Lorentzian the transformed integrand is the smooth function $w/\cosh t$ of width about 1.
The interval length grows only as about $2\ln(2/w)$, making evaluation count almost independent of $w$.
Keep the integrand code as $f(c + w\sinh t)\,w\cosh t$; simplification to $1/\cosh t$ is unnecessary.
This transformation applies generally to width-$w$ peaks with algebraically decaying tails.

## Tolerance scale

Since area $I \approx \pi w$, narrowing the peak reduces the integral itself.
With relative tolerance alone, the required absolute error $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}|I|$ also shrinks in proportion to $w$, requiring deeper refinement.
Conversely, if absolute tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is comparable to or greater than area $\pi w$, tolerance can be met without resolving the area at all.
If the smallest meaningful area is known, set $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}$ well below it.
The following cells use $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{abs}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}} = \varepsilon$, giving criterion $\varepsilon(1 + |Q|)$.

The following block defines helpers shared by two cells.
`lorentz` gives line shape $f$, `exact_area` the closed-form area, `subdivision_options` tolerance and bisection limit, `split_at_peak` prior subdivision at $x = c \pm w$, and `stretched` integration in $x = c + w\sinh t$.

```rust
//| id: integrate-narrow-peak-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Lorentzian line shape and three quadrature configurations"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-integrate]
use scioxide_core::{Budget, ErrorTolerance, SciOxideError, Interval, NormSelection, RealScalar};
use scioxide_integrate::{
    AdaptiveIntegrator, AdaptiveRule, CubaturePlan, IntegrationReport, SubdivisionOptions,
};

/// Line shape f(x) = 1 / (1 + ((x − c)/w)²).
pub(crate) fn lorentz(x: f64, center: f64, width: f64) -> f64 {
    let u = (x - center) / width;
    1.0 / (1.0 + u * u)
}

/// Closed-form area w [atan((1 − c)/w) − atan((−1 − c)/w)]. Reference using standard f64 functions.
pub(crate) fn exact_area(center: f64, width: f64) -> f64 {
    width * (((1.0 - center) / width).atan() - ((-1.0 - center) / width).atan())
}

/// Absolute and relative tolerance ε (infinity norm) and bisection-count limit.
pub(crate) fn subdivision_options(
    tolerance: f64,
    max_subdivisions: usize,
) -> Result<SubdivisionOptions<f64>, SciOxideError> {
    let tolerance = ErrorTolerance::scalar(tolerance, tolerance, NormSelection::Infinity)?;
    Ok(SubdivisionOptions::default()
        .with_tolerance(tolerance)
        .with_max_subdivisions(Budget::try_from(max_subdivisions)?))
}

/// Split at x = c ± w, then refine with G7K15 (one-dimensional CubaturePlan).
pub(crate) fn split_at_peak(
    center: f64,
    width: f64,
    options: SubdivisionOptions<f64>,
) -> Result<(f64, IntegrationReport<f64>), SciOxideError> {
    // Supply breakpoints as interval fractions (x + 1)/2 on [−1, 1], excluding fractions outside (0, 1).
    let fractions: Vec<f64> = [center - width, center + width]
        .iter()
        .map(|x| 0.5 * (x + 1.0))
        .filter(|&t| t > 0.0 && t < 1.0)
        .collect();
    let domain = [Interval::new(-1.0, 1.0)?];
    CubaturePlan::new(&domain, options)?
        .with_breakpoints(0, &fractions)?
        .integrate(|x: &[f64]| Ok(lorentz(x[0], center, width)))
}

/// Evaluate the transformed integral ∫ f(c + w sinh t) w cosh t dt with G7K15.
pub(crate) fn stretched(
    center: f64,
    width: f64,
    options: SubdivisionOptions<f64>,
) -> Result<(f64, IntegrationReport<f64>), SciOxideError> {
    let lower = RealScalar::asinh((-1.0 - center) / width);
    let upper = RealScalar::asinh((1.0 - center) / width);
    let integrand = |t: f64| {
        let x = center + width * RealScalar::sinh(t);
        Ok(lorentz(x, center, width) * width * RealScalar::cosh(t))
    };
    AdaptiveIntegrator::new(AdaptiveRule::GaussKronrod, options)?
        .integrate(&integrand, Interval::new(lower, upper)?)
}
```

## Compare methods on one line

The next cell computes area in four ways at selected $c$, $w$, $\varepsilon$, and bisection limit, then tabulates differences from the closed form.
The 15-point Gauss–Legendre rule is a fixed rule applied once over the whole interval for comparison.
For adaptive quadrature starting from the whole interval, record all evaluated $(x, f(x))$ and overlay them on the line shape.
The curve uses dense points near the peak, preserving its shape even for small $w$.
The bisection limit affects whole-interval adaptive quadrature and prior subdivision; the sinh-coordinate integral finishes before reaching it.
The final outcome reports termination of whole-interval adaptive quadrature.

```rust
//| id: integrate-narrow-peak
//| ref: integrate-narrow-peak
//| title: "Compute narrow-peak area four ways and inspect evaluation locations"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [integrate-narrow-peak-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-integrate]
//| inputs:
//|   width_exponent: {"type":"range","label":"Width exponent q (w = 10^q)","description":"Change half-width at half-maximum w.","min":-3.5,"max":-0.5,"step":0.25,"value":-2}

//|   tolerance_exponent: {"type":"range","label":"Tolerance exponent p (ε = 10^p)","description":"Vary absolute and relative tolerances together.","min":-12,"max":-5,"step":1,"value":-9}

            "points": points,
            "style": { "symbol": "circle" }
        }
    ]
}));

    ("15-point Gauss–Legendre (one application)", &fixed),
    ("Adaptive G7K15 (whole interval)", &plain),
    ("Adaptive G7K15 (split at x = c ± w)", &split),
    ("Adaptive G7K15 (x = c + w sinh t)", &local),
]
.into_iter()
.map(|(name, (value, report))| {
    (
        name,
        *value,
        (value - reference).abs(),
        report.error_estimate(),
        report.evaluations(),
        report.termination().to_string(),
    )
})
.collect();
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Method" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Area [-]", "type": "number", "format": digits12 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Closed-form discrepancy [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Error estimate [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": "None (fixed rule)" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Evaluations [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Termination status" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "methods" })
);

// Outcome: termination of adaptive quadrature starting from the whole interval.
let (value, report) = &plain;
let termination = report.termination();

    Outcome::satisfied("Whole-interval adaptive quadrature meets the estimated tolerance")
} else {
    Outcome::partial("Whole-interval adaptive quadrature stopped before meeting tolerance")
        .explanation("Read the returned area as an estimate; increase the bisection limit or transform to local coordinates.")
};
let mut outcome = outcome
    .status(termination.to_string())
    .diagnostic("Evaluations", report.evaluations() as f64, None)
    .diagnostic("Closed-form discrepancy", (value - reference).abs(), None);
if let Some(estimate) = report.error_estimate() {
    outcome = outcome.diagnostic("Error estimate", estimate, None);
}
emit_outcome!(outcome);
```

At the defaults ($w = 10^{-2}$, $c = 0.15$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$), the closed-form area is $3.1211 \times 10^{-2}$, about 0.65 % below $\pi w$ because tails leave the window.
The 15-point Gauss–Legendre value is $8.97 \times 10^{-3}$, less than 30 % of the area, yet termination is `FixedRuleCompleted`.
Whole-interval adaptive quadrature reaches `ToleranceReached` after 405 evaluations ($15 + 30 \times 13$), with a difference from the closed form of order $10^{-16}$.
The reported error estimate $2.7 \times 10^{-11}$ is five orders of magnitude larger than the actual difference.
Evaluation points cluster around $x = c$: 211 of 405 lie in $|x - c| < 0.1$, one tenth of the window width.
Prior subdivision takes 375 evaluations and sinh coordinates 165; both agree with the closed form to within $10^{-15}$.

At width $w = 10^{-3.5}$, the fixed rule returns about 1 % of the area.
Whole-interval adaptive evaluations rise to 525; prior subdivision instead requires even more, 675.
The sinh coordinates need only 135.

With a limit of eight bisections, whole-interval adaptive quadrature reaches `BudgetLimit(Subdivision)` after 255 evaluations ($15 + 30 \times 8$), giving outcome partial.
The closed-form difference remains only $7.5 \times 10^{-13}$, but error estimate $1.1 \times 10^{-6}$ fails tolerance, so the calculation cannot claim accuracy.
Prior subdivision also reaches that limit, while sinh-coordinate integration reaches `ToleranceReached` within it.

At $c = 0.8$ and $w = 10^{-0.5}$, area $0.62007$ is clearly below $\pi w = 0.9935$.
Because the peak is broad, even the 15-point fixed rule differs from the closed form by only $2.0 \times 10^{-6}$.

## Evaluation counts and missed peaks versus width

The next cell evaluates three adaptive methods at 25 widths from $w = 10^{-6}$ to $1$ in factors of $10^{0.25}$, recording evaluation counts and relative differences from the closed form.
The bisection limit is fixed at 1024.

At the defaults ($c = 0.15$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$), whole-interval adaptive evaluations grow approximately in proportion to $\log w$, from 45 at $w = 1$ to 585 at $w = 10^{-5}$.
But at $w \le 1.8 \times 10^{-6}$ there are only 15 evaluations, termination remains `ToleranceReached`, and relative difference is nearly 1.
None of the initial 15 points touches the peak, and the tolerance check passes while missing its area.
Prior subdivision finds the peak at every width, but for $w \le 3.2 \times 10^{-3}$ takes more evaluations than whole-interval quadrature, up to 1065.
Sinh coordinates use 45–165 evaluations at every width, with relative differences at most $1.5 \times 10^{-9}$.

Setting $c = 0$ aligns the peak center with the initial Kronrod node $x = 0$.
Whole-interval adaptive quadrature then finds the peak even at $w = 10^{-6}$ and converges after 1185 evaluations.
Missing a peak depends on its location relative to the nodes, not just its width.

Relaxing to $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$ makes whole-interval adaptive quadrature miss the peak for $w \le 5.6 \times 10^{-5}$.
Prior subdivision has relative difference nearly 0.5 for $w \le 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$.
The central region $[c - w, c + w]$ contains exactly half the area ($\int_{-1}^{1} du/(1 + u^2) = \pi/2$), while the initial 15 nodes in each outer region miss the tails concentrated near the breakpoints.
Sinh-coordinate relative differences remain at most $1.7 \times 10^{-6}$.

## Summary

Adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature finds narrow-peak areas by repeated bisection around the peak, with evaluation counts growing approximately as the logarithm of inverse width.
If a peak is too narrow for the initial nodes to touch it, adaptive quadrature can return `ToleranceReached` while missing the area, so do not accept results from termination alone.
When location and width are known, local coordinates such as $x = c + w\sinh t$ make evaluation counts almost width independent.
Prior subdivision ensures sampling of the peak center but leaves refinement of the outer tails, so it need not reduce evaluation counts.
Treat `BudgetLimit(Subdivision)` from the bisection limit as partial and increase the limit or change coordinates.
For small areas, choose an absolute tolerance well below the area's magnitude.
