Integrating the simple-pendulum period over angle gives an integrand that becomes infinite at the upper endpoint.
This example compares quadrature treatments of that singularity.
[Compute the period of a large-amplitude pendulum](../../../scioxide-special/examples/pendulum-period/) primarily covers the derivation in terms of the complete elliptic integral of the first kind $K(m)$, small-angle expansion, and Jacobi-function trajectories.
Here compute $K(m)$ with scioxide-special's `elliptic_k` only as a reference to check quadrature results.
Use pendulum length $L = 1.2$ m and standard gravitational acceleration $g = 9.80665$ m/s², matching that example.

## Two integral forms of the period

For a length-$L$ pendulum oscillating with maximum angle $\theta_0 \in (0, \pi)$, energy conservation gives angular velocity $\dot\theta = \sqrt{2g/L}\,\sqrt{\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0}$.
The time from the lowest point to $\theta_0$ is one quarter of the period, giving

$$
T = 2\sqrt2\,\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\int_0^{\theta_0}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0}}
$$


Call this form A.
Dividing by the small-angle period $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ gives $T/T_0 = (\sqrt2/\pi)\,I_A$, where $I_A$ is the integral above.

Legendre's substitution $\sin(\theta/2) = \sin(\theta_0/2)\sin\varphi$ gives

$$
T = 4\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1 - m\sin^2\varphi}} = 4\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\,K(m), \qquad m = \sin^2\frac{\theta_0}{2}
$$


Call this form B.
The reference is $T/T_0 = (2/\pi)\,I_B = (2/\pi)\,K(m)$; pass parameter $m = k^2$ to `elliptic_k` rather than modulus $k = \sin(\theta_0/2)$.

## Inverse-square-root singularity at the upper endpoint

The form-A integrand diverges as $\theta \to \theta_0$.
Since $\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0 \approx \sin\theta_0\,(\theta_0 - \theta)$, it behaves near the upper endpoint like $(\sin\theta_0)^{-1/2}(\theta_0 - \theta)^{-1/2}$.
The exponent $-1/2 > -1$ makes the integral finite, but polynomial quadrature does not approximate the singularity well.
For a function with endpoint singularity $(1 - t)^{\alpha}$, an $n$-point Gauss–Legendre rule with interior nodes has error decreasing only as $O(n^{-2-2\alpha})$, or $O(n^{-1})$ for $\alpha = -1/2$.

Integrand evaluation also requires care.
Directly computing $\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0$ near the upper endpoint loses relative digits by subtracting nearly equal numbers.
Using distance $d = \theta_0 - \theta$, rewrite with the trigonometric product identity as

$$
\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0 = 2\sin\Bigl(\theta_0 - \frac{d}{2}\Bigr)\sin\frac{d}{2}
$$

to avoid subtraction.
However, constructing $d$ from coordinate $\theta$ through $\theta_0 - \theta$ loses digits in $d$ when $\theta$ is rounded.

### Double-exponential transformation and endpoint distance

The double-exponential (tanh–sinh) transformation maps the interval to $t \in (-\infty, \infty)$ and applies the trapezoidal rule at nodes clustering double exponentially near endpoints ([double-exponential transforms for improper integrals and endpoint singularities](../../algorithms/improper-singular-and-oscillatory/)).
The transformed integrand decays double exponentially as $|t| \to \infty$, so error decreases rapidly with point count even for algebraic endpoint singularities.
Nodes approach endpoints much closer than floating-point spacing, so coordinate $\theta$ rounds to $\theta_0$.
`DoubleExponentialPoint::nearest_endpoint()` returns the nearest endpoint and its distance without subtracting rounded coordinates.
Where the upper endpoint is nearest, pass this distance as $d$ to the product identity above; elsewhere use $d = \theta_0 - \theta$.

### Endpoint weight and smooth factor

Separating the singularity as a weight leaves a smooth factor:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0}} = (\theta_0 - \theta)^{-1/2}\,g(\theta), \qquad
g(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sin(\theta_0 - d/2)\,\operatorname{sinc}(d/2)}}
$$


Here $\operatorname{sinc}x = \sin x / x$, with $\operatorname{sinc}0 = 1$.
Although $g$ has form $0/0$ at $\theta = \theta_0$, its limit is $g(\theta_0) = (\sin\theta_0)^{-1/2}$, and the sinc expression directly returns that value there.
`WeightedQuadraturePlan::endpoint` computes modified Chebyshev moments of weight $(\theta - a)^{\alpha}(b - \theta)^{\beta}$ once during plan preparation, then integrates with modified Clenshaw–Curtis quadrature that interpolates only $g$.
The callback returns $g$.
This rule evaluates $g$ at endpoints $a$ and $b$, requiring a formula finite at those endpoints.
Here use $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = -1/2$.

Gauss–Jacobi applies the same separation with a fixed point count.
Mapping the Jacobi family with weight $(1 - t)^{-1/2}$ ($\alpha = -1/2$, $\beta = 0$) to interval $[0, \theta_0]$ gives $1 - t = (\theta_0 - \theta)/h$, with $h = \theta_0/2$.
For reference nodes $t_i$ and weights $w_i$ on $[-1, 1]$, this gives $\int_0^{\theta_0} g(\theta)(\theta_0 - \theta)^{-1/2}\,d\theta = \sqrt h\sum_i w_i\,g(\theta_i)$.
Passing $[0, \theta_0]$ to `integrate_weighted` through `GaussianSupport::Interval` returns $h\sum_i w_i\,g(\theta_i)$ with a factor $h$ in the weights; divide that value by $\sqrt h$ for the desired integral.

Form B is smooth on $[0, \pi/2]$, allowing ordinary Gauss–Legendre and adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature directly.
However, as $\theta_0 \to \pi$, $m \to 1$ and the zero $\varphi = \pi/2 + iy$ of $1 - m\sin^2\varphi$ ($\cosh y = 1/\sin(\theta_0/2)$) approaches endpoint $\varphi = \pi/2$ in the imaginary direction.
Its distance is about $(\pi - \theta_0)/2$; convergence slows as that distance shrinks.

The following definitions block is shared by two cells.
`form_a(θ₀, d)` evaluates form A using upper-endpoint distance $d$; `form_a_smooth(θ₀, d)` gives smooth factor $g$, and `form_b(m, φ)` gives form B.
`reference_ratio(θ₀)` returns reference $(2/\pi)K(m)$, with $m$ computed directly from degrees using `sin_degrees`.

```rust
//| id: integrate-pendulum-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Two pendulum-period integrals and a K(m) reference"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-special]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, RealScalar, sin_degrees, sinc};
use scioxide_special::elliptic_k;

/// Pendulum length L [m] and standard gravitational acceleration g [m/s²], matching the special example.
pub(crate) const LENGTH: f64 = 1.2;
pub(crate) const GRAVITY: f64 = 9.80665;

/// Parameter m = sin²(θ₀/2). Receive θ₀ in degrees and evaluate directly with sin_degrees.
pub(crate) fn parameter(theta0_deg: f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    Ok(RealScalar::powi(sin_degrees(0.5 * theta0_deg)?, 2))
}

/// Reference T/T₀ = (2/π) K(m).
pub(crate) fn reference_ratio(theta0_deg: f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    Ok(2.0 / <f64 as RealScalar>::PI * elliptic_k(parameter(theta0_deg)?)?)
}

/// Evaluate form A, 1/√(cos θ − cos θ₀), using distance d = θ₀ − θ from the upper endpoint.
/// cos θ − cos θ₀ = 2 sin(θ₀ − d/2) sin(d/2), avoiding subtractive cancellation.
pub(crate) fn form_a(theta0: f64, d: f64) -> f64 {
    let gap = 2.0 * RealScalar::sin(theta0 - 0.5 * d) * RealScalar::sin(0.5 * d);
    1.0 / RealScalar::sqrt(gap)
}

/// Smooth form-A factor g = 1/√(sin(θ₀ − d/2) sinc(d/2)), equal to 1/√(sin θ₀) at d = 0.
pub(crate) fn form_a_smooth(theta0: f64, d: f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    let product = RealScalar::sin(theta0 - 0.5 * d) * sinc(0.5 * d)?;
    Ok(1.0 / RealScalar::sqrt(product))
}

/// Form-B integrand 1/√(1 − m sin²φ).
pub(crate) fn form_b(m: f64, phi: f64) -> f64 {
    1.0 / RealScalar::sqrt(1.0 - m * RealScalar::powi(RealScalar::sin(phi), 2))
}
```

## Compare five approaches

The next cell selects amplitude $\theta_0$ and computes period ratio $T/T_0$ five ways, comparing against reference $(2/\pi)K(m)$.
For form A, use double-exponential quadrature with endpoint distances, double-exponential quadrature using coordinate differences, and weighted quadrature with endpoint exponent $\beta = -1/2$.
For form B, use $n$-point Gauss–Legendre and adaptive Gauss–Kronrod (G7K15).
All adaptive methods use absolute tolerance 0 and relative tolerance $10^{-12}$.
Inputs are amplitude, Gauss–Legendre node count $n$, and double-exponential level limit $L$.
At level $L$, the finest spacing is $2^{-(L-1)}$.

The table's “Estimated relative error” converts the reported estimate to a relative error in $T/T_0$ and is not an upper bound.
Gauss–Legendre is fixed, has no error estimate, and terminates with `FixedRuleCompleted`.
Methods ending in an error show “—” for the value and the error kind in the termination column.

At the default $\theta_0 = 150^\circ$, the reference is $T/T_0 = 1.76220$ and $T = 3.8732$ s.
Double-exponential quadrature with endpoint distances reaches `HeuristicToleranceReached` after 137 evaluations, with relative error $2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ against the reference.
Apart from `DegenerateDomain` for zero-width intervals, this is its only successful termination; it does not return `ToleranceReached`.
Its tail-truncation estimate is heuristic, based on observed decay.
The endpoint-weight plan reaches `ToleranceReached` after 40 evaluations and form-B adaptive Gauss–Kronrod after 105; both errors are at rounding level.
Eight-node Gauss–Legendre retains error $1.3 \times 10^{-5}$ even on form B.

Double-exponential quadrature forming $\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0$ through coordinate differences stops with a `NonFinite` error at every amplitude.
Near the upper endpoint, coordinates round to $\theta_0$, making the difference 0 and $1/\sqrt{0}$ infinite; alternatively rounding makes the difference negative and its square root NaN.
At the same nodes, receiving endpoint distance $d$ and using the product identity gives finite values even when $d$ is below the floating-point spacing at $\theta_0$.
The comparison shows that handling the upper singularity with double-exponential quadrature depends on integrand evaluation, rather than the transformation alone.

At $\theta_0 = 30^\circ$, $m = 0.067$ is small and eight-node Gauss–Legendre has relative error $1.5 \times 10^{-13}$.
At $\theta_0 = 179.9^\circ$, the same eight-node error grows to 0.33.
All adaptive methods meet tolerance, but evaluation counts rise to 265 for double exponential, 400 for endpoint weighting, and 345 for adaptive Gauss–Kronrod.
With level limit $L = 4$ ($\theta_0 = 179^\circ$), double-exponential quadrature reaches `RefinementLimit` after 73 evaluations and returns its last value as an estimate.
Actual relative error is then $1.9 \times 10^{-13}$, but reported estimated error $2.8 \times 10^{-6}$ correctly indicates insufficient refinement levels.

## Node count and error decay

The next cell varies fixed-rule node count $n = 1, 2, \dots, 128$ and plots relative errors in $T/T_0$ on logarithmic axes.
There are three series.
They are Gauss–Legendre directly on form A, Gauss–Jacobi on smooth factor $g$ of form A (weight $(1 - t)^{-1/2}$), and Gauss–Legendre on form B.
Omit exactly zero errors from logarithmic axes and give their count in the table.
The dashed horizontal line marks double-precision machine epsilon $\varepsilon \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$.

        alpha: -0.5,

    let (smooth, _) = legendre
        .integrate(|phi: f64| Ok(form_b(m, phi)), quarter)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let errors = [scale_a * direct, jacobi_scale * weighted, scale_b * smooth]
        .map(|ratio| (ratio / reference - 1.0).abs());
    for (error, curve) in errors.iter().zip(curves.iter_mut()) {
        // Omit and count zero errors, which cannot appear on a logarithmic axis.
        if *error > 0.0 {
            curve.push([n as f64, *error]);
        } else {
            dropped += 1;
        }
    }
    if [4, 16, 64, 128].contains(&n) {
        rows.push((n, errors[0], errors[1], errors[2]));
    }
}

Gauss–Legendre directly on form A (circles) approximately halves error whenever node count doubles, following a line of slope $-1$.
This is $O(n^{-1})$ convergence from endpoint singularity $(\theta_0 - \theta)^{-1/2}$; at the default $\theta_0 = 150^\circ$, error remains $4.0 \times 10^{-3}$ even with 128 nodes.
Gauss–Jacobi (triangles) incorporates the same singularity as weight $(1 - t)^{-1/2}$; smooth $g$ gives exponentially decreasing error, reaching rounding level ($3.3 \times 10^{-16}$) at 16 nodes.
Form-B Gauss–Legendre (squares) also converges exponentially, but its 16-node error is $1.1 \times 10^{-10}$, slower than Gauss–Jacobi.

At $\theta_0 = 179.9^\circ$, both exponentially convergent sequences slow down.
For form B, the branch point described above is about $8.7 \times 10^{-4}$ from the endpoint, leaving error $2.1 \times 10^{-5}$ at 128 nodes.
For form-A factor $g$, a point satisfying $\sin(\theta_0 - d/2) = 0$ also approaches the interval from outside at $\theta = 2\pi - \theta_0$, limiting Gauss–Jacobi to $5.3 \times 10^{-9}$ at 128 nodes.
Approaching inversion introduces a nearly singular structure associated with logarithmic period divergence, in addition to the endpoint singularity.
Fixed-point rules cannot resolve it, while the adaptive methods above accommodate it with more evaluations.

## References near inversion

At $\theta_0 = 179.9^\circ$, the two adaptive form-A methods (double exponential and endpoint weighting) agree to relative difference at most $10^{-14}$, but differ from the reference by $2.4 \times 10^{-13}$.
Much of this difference comes from the reference.
Rounding $m = \sin^2(\theta_0/2)$ to double precision introduces relative error of order $\varepsilon/(1 - m)$ into $1 - m \approx 7.6 \times 10^{-7}$; with $K(m) \approx \ln\bigl(4/\sqrt{1 - m}\bigr)$, relative error in $K$ can grow to about $\varepsilon/\bigl(2(1 - m)K\bigr)$, or order $10^{-11}$.
Form A uses $\theta_0$ directly and avoids this rounding.
The arithmetic–geometric-mean reference computed from $\sqrt{1 - m} = \cos(\theta_0/2)$ in [compute the period of a large-amplitude pendulum](../../../scioxide-special/examples/pendulum-period/) avoids this effect.
When comparing references, interpret errors together with the conditioning of the compared quantity.

## Summary

Writing the pendulum period as an angle integral produces an inverse-square-root singularity at the upper endpoint.
For double-exponential quadrature, evaluate using endpoint distance from `nearest_endpoint()` and the product identity for $\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0$.
Coordinate subtraction can round the difference to 0 or a negative value and stop with a `NonFinite` error.
Separating singularity $(\theta_0 - \theta)^{-1/2}$ as a weight lets `WeightedQuadraturePlan::endpoint` or Gauss–Jacobi handle only the smooth factor and reach rounding accuracy with few evaluations.
Gauss–Legendre with the singularity retained converges only as $O(n^{-1})$; even form B, smoothed by Legendre's substitution, converges slowly near amplitude $180^\circ$.
