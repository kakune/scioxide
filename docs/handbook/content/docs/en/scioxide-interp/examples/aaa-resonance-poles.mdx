The infrared response of polar crystals exhibits resonances from lattice vibrations.
Approximating the dielectric function by a rational function from real-axis samples and examining its poles in the complex plane allows separate estimates of resonance positions and damping widths.
However, even a good real-axis approximation can yield analytic continuation into the complex plane that is sensitive to perturbations.
Use a model with closed-form poles to compare AAA results with known values.

## Lorentz model and 6H-SiC constants

Use time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ and define the dielectric function as

$$
\epsilon(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty+
\frac{\Delta\epsilon\,\omega_T^2}{\omega_T^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}
$$

the displayed quotient.
$\epsilon_\infty$ is the permittivity at frequencies well above lattice vibrations (the electronic contribution), $\Delta\epsilon$ is oscillator strength, $\omega_T$ is the transverse optical (TO) phonon angular frequency, and $\gamma>0$ is damping width.
With this time-dependence convention, a causal response is analytic in the upper half-plane and has poles in the lower half-plane.
Below, use spectroscopic wavenumber $\nu$ (in cm⁻¹), dividing angular frequencies by the common factor $2\pi c$.
The same conversion applies to every denominator term, leaving the formula's form unchanged.

Static permittivity is $\epsilon_s=\epsilon(0)=\epsilon_\infty+\Delta\epsilon$.
The longitudinal optical (LO) phonon wavenumber $\nu_L$ is the zero of $\epsilon$ when damping is neglected and satisfies the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation

$$
\frac{\epsilon_s}{\epsilon_\infty}=\left(\frac{\nu_L}{\nu_T}\right)^2
$$

On $\nu_T<\nu<\nu_L$, $\mathrm{Re}\,\epsilon<0$, and light is nearly totally reflected (the Reststrahlen band).

Use constants fitted to the 6H-SiC reflection spectrum by [Spitzer, Kleinman, Walsh, Phys. Rev. 113, 127 (1959)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.113.127) for the component with electric field perpendicular to the c axis (ordinary ray).
The values are $\nu_T=793.9\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, $\gamma=4.8\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, $\Delta\epsilon=3.30$, and $\epsilon_s=10.0$, giving $\epsilon_\infty=\epsilon_s-\Delta\epsilon=6.7$.
The Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation gives $\nu_L=\nu_T\sqrt{\epsilon_s/\epsilon_\infty}\approx969.9\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, agreeing with the roughly 970 cm⁻¹ often quoted for the SiC LO phonon wavenumber.
For small damping, the absorption peak's full width at half maximum in $\mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon$ is approximately $\gamma$, a sharp resonance with $\gamma/\nu_T\approx0.006$.
The c-axis component, free-carrier Drude term, multiphonon absorption, and measured-spectrum noise are excluded.
This model does not fully represent SiC's dielectric function; here it validates rational approximation.

## Closed-form poles and residues

The poles making denominator $\nu_T^2-\nu^2-i\gamma\nu$ zero are

$$
p_\pm=-\frac{i\gamma}{2}\pm\sqrt{\nu_T^2-\frac{\gamma^2}{4}}
$$


As long as damping satisfies $\gamma<2\nu_T$, both poles share imaginary part $-\gamma/2$ and lie symmetrically about the imaginary axis ($p_-=-\overline{p_+}$).
This symmetry reflects the Fourier transform of a real time response satisfying $\epsilon(-\nu)=\overline{\epsilon(\nu)}$.
A pole's imaginary part represents resonance damping, with damping width read as $\hat\gamma=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,p_+$.
The denominator derivative is $-2\nu-i\gamma$, so a simple pole's residue is

$$
\rho_\pm=\frac{\Delta\epsilon\,\nu_T^2}{-2p_\pm-i\gamma}
$$


$\epsilon$ is rational in $\nu$ with quadratic numerator and denominator, and its value at infinity is $\epsilon_\infty$.

Perform approximation in dimensionless coordinates $z=\nu/\nu_T$.
In $z$ coordinates, $\epsilon=\epsilon_\infty+\Delta\epsilon/(1-z^2-igz)$ ($g=\gamma/\nu_T$); multiply pole coordinates and residues by $\nu_T$ to recover values in $\nu$ coordinates.
AAA residual tests concern function values, so scaling coordinates by a constant leaves the approximation unchanged within rounding.
Use $z$ here to keep coordinates around unit magnitude.

## AAA and its stopping conditions

AAA approximates samples $(z_i,f_i)$ with a rational function in barycentric form

$$
r(z)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^m a_j/(z-z_j)}{\sum_{j=1}^m b_j/(z-z_j)},\qquad a_j=b_jf_j
$$


$z_j$ are support points selected from sample points; if $a_j=b_jf_j$, $r$ matches the sample values at support points.
At each stage, add the sample with largest residual $|r(z_i)-f_i|$ as a new support point and find weights $b$ minimizing the error at remaining samples as the right singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value of the Loewner matrix $A_{ij}=(f_i-f_j)/(z_i-z_j)$.
With $m$ support points, the numerator and denominator degrees are at most $m-1$.
The model above is a quadratic rational function, so three support points reproduce it in exact arithmetic.
See [Rational approximation](../../algorithms/rational-approximation/) for algorithm details.

Fit with the detailed form `aaa_with_options(nodes, values, domain, options)`.
Use `AaaOptions::new().with_max_terms(8)` to set the support limit to 8 and `with_tolerance` to specify residual tolerance (defaults are limit 100 and tolerance $\sqrt\varepsilon\approx1.5\times10^{-8}$).
The stopping test checks whether the maximum sample residual is at most $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}+\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}\max_i|f_i|$.
Meeting the test reports `RationalTermination::Converged`; reaching the support limit first reports `SupportLimit`.
Both return the last accepted approximation.
`is_success()` is true only for `Converged`.
The one-shot function `aaa` does not allow choosing support limits or tolerances and returns an error of `ConvergenceFailure` when the test is unmet, so it is not used here.
A stage with $k$ support points computes the SVD of an $N\times k$ matrix, giving total cost $O(NK^3)$ through $K$ support points.

`BarycentricRational::diagnostics` computes poles and residues.
It multiplies the barycentric form by $\prod_j(z-z_j)$ to convert it to a polynomial ratio $p/q$, then obtains numerator and denominator roots as companion-matrix eigenvalues.
This is an $O(m^3)$ diagnostic allocating storage separately from evaluation; the roots are numerical estimates rather than certified enclosures.
The residue of a simple pole $\zeta$ is computed as $p(\zeta)/q'(\zeta)$; if $q'(\zeta)=0$, it returns `None`.

Samples are $N$ equally spaced points on $z\in[-1.4,1.4]$, including negative wavenumbers.
With default $N=81$, sample spacing is about 28 cm⁻¹, much coarser than damping width $\gamma=4.8\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$.
Although the resonance width is hidden between samples, the model is exactly rational, so AAA can recover poles from noiseless data.
The approximation domain is a rectangle with real part $[-1.4,1.4]$ and imaginary part $[-0.2,0.2]$; evaluation occurs only on the real axis.

The following block defines the model and helpers shared by the page's three cells.
`permittivity` defines $\epsilon$ in $z$ coordinates; `exact_pole` gives closed-form poles and residues; `sample_nodes` and `fit_domain` define sample points and domain; `generator` creates a seeded random generator; `noisy_samples` gives noisy samples; `unused_error` measures errors at points not coinciding with samples.

```rust
//| id: interp-aaa-resonance-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Lorentz model and sampling and validation helpers"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-interp, scioxide-random]
use scioxide_core::{Complex, SciOxideError, Interval, RealScalar};
use scioxide_interp::{
    BarycentricRational, ExtrapolationPolicy, Interpolant, RationalDomain, RationalPole,
};
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Normal, SeedableRng, SplitMix64};

/// Ordinary ray of 6H-SiC (Spitzer et al. 1959): ν_T [cm⁻¹], ε∞, Δε.
pub(crate) const TRANSVERSE: f64 = 793.9;
pub(crate) const EPSILON_INF: f64 = 6.7;
pub(crate) const STRENGTH: f64 = 3.3;

/// ε(z) = ε∞ + Δε/(1 − z² − i g z), z = ν/ν_T, g = γ/ν_T.
pub(crate) fn permittivity(z: Complex<f64>, g: f64) -> Complex<f64> {
    let denominator = Complex::new(1.0, 0.0) - z * z - Complex::new(0.0, g) * z;
    Complex::new(EPSILON_INF, 0.0) + Complex::new(STRENGTH, 0.0) / denominator
}

/// Closed-form poles p = ±√(1 − g²/4) − i g/2 and residues Δε/(−2p − i g) (z coordinates).
pub(crate) fn exact_pole(sign: f64, g: f64) -> (Complex<f64>, Complex<f64>) {
    let pole = Complex::new(sign * RealScalar::sqrt(1.0 - g * g / 4.0), -g / 2.0);
    let residue = Complex::new(STRENGTH, 0.0) / (pole * (-2.0) - Complex::new(0.0, g));
    (pole, residue)
}

/// The estimated pole nearest target.
pub(crate) fn nearest_pole(
    poles: &[RationalPole<f64>],
    target: Complex<f64>,
) -> Option<&RationalPole<f64>> {
    poles.iter().min_by(|a, b| {
        let da = (a.location() - target).norm();
        da.total_cmp(&(b.location() - target).norm())
    })
}

/// count equally spaced points in z ∈ [−1.4, 1.4] (on the real axis).
pub(crate) fn sample_nodes(count: usize) -> Vec<Complex<f64>> {
    let last = (count - 1) as f64;
    (0..count)
        .map(|i| Complex::new(-1.4 + 2.8 * i as f64 / last, 0.0))
        .collect()
}

/// Rectangle [−1.4, 1.4] × [−0.2, 0.2] i containing the poles.
pub(crate) fn fit_domain() -> Result<RationalDomain<f64>, SciOxideError> {
    Ok(RationalDomain::Complex {
        real: Interval::new(-1.4, 1.4)?,
        imaginary: Interval::new(-0.2, 0.2)?,
    })
}

/// Samples with independent N(0, σ²) noise added to real and imaginary parts at each point.
pub(crate) fn noisy_samples(
    nodes: &[Complex<f64>],
    g: f64,
    noise: f64,
    rng: &mut SplitMix64,
) -> Result<Vec<Complex<f64>>, SciOxideError> {
    let normal = Normal::new(0.0_f64, 1.0)?;
    let mut values = Vec::with_capacity(nodes.len());
    for &z in nodes {
        let re = normal.sample(rng)?;
        let im = normal.sample(rng)?;
        values.push(permittivity(z, g) + Complex::new(noise * re, noise * im));
    }
    Ok(values)
}

/// Maximum |r − ε| at 240 nonsample points (interval midpoints).
pub(crate) fn unused_error(
    model: &BarycentricRational<Complex<f64>>,
    g: f64,
) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    let mut largest = 0.0_f64;
    for i in 0..240 {
        let x = -1.4 + 2.8 * (i as f64 + 0.5) / 240.0;
        let value = model.evaluate([x, 0.0], ExtrapolationPolicy::Reject)?;
        largest = largest.max((value - permittivity(Complex::new(x, 0.0), g)).norm());
    }
    Ok(largest)
}

/// SplitMix64 initialized from a random seed.
pub(crate) fn generator(seed: f64) -> SplitMix64 {
    SplitMix64::from_seed(seed as u64)
}
```

With defaults, AAA reaches `Converged` with three support points and finds only two poles.
Both pole positions agree with closed forms within $3\times10^{-13}$ cm⁻¹ and residues (magnitude about 1310 cm⁻¹) within $2\times10^{-11}$ cm⁻¹; the estimated damping width reproduces 4.8 cm⁻¹ at relative level $10^{-13}$.
Unused-point error is $3.5\times10^{-11}$, at rounding level relative to maximum sample value $|f_i|\approx100$.
Changing damping width to the endpoints 1 and 8 cm⁻¹ and sample count to 41 and 121 leaves convergence at three support points unchanged.
At $\gamma=1$ cm⁻¹ and $N=41$, sample spacing is about 56 times damping width, but poles are recovered with the same accuracy.

At default $\tau=10^{-9}$, threshold $1.0\times10^{-7}$ is well above the three-support residual $4.2\times10^{-12}$.
Setting $\tau=10^{-14}$ places threshold $1.0\times10^{-12}$ below the three-support residual, so AAA increases support points to four, introducing an extra pole beyond the physical poles.
At $\tau=10^{-16}$, it reaches the support limit of 8 without meeting the threshold, stopping with `SupportLimit` and seven poles.
The position error of $p_+$ then increases to $4.9\times10^{-12}$ cm⁻¹, about 18 times the default error.
Unused-point error instead decreases to $3.2\times10^{-12}$, so real-axis accuracy alone cannot identify extra poles.
A table residue labeled “Cannot compute” means the denominator derivative vanished at that pole; it does not mean a zero residue.

## Noisy samples and doublets

Applying a small tolerance to noisy samples makes AAA add support points to reproduce noise, creating poles unrelated to physical resonances.
Many are pole–zero pairs with nearby zeros and small residues.
Such pairs are called Froissart doublets.
The poles and zeros nearly cancel, barely affecting real-axis values, but interpreting these poles as resonances is misleading.

For a pole $\zeta$, `diagnostics` suspects a doublet when both quantities below fall within doublet tolerance $(\tau_{\mathrm{abs}},\tau_{\mathrm{rel}})$.
The first is distance to the nearest zero, with scale $\max(|\zeta|,h)$.
The second is absolute residue, with scale $h\max_j|a_j|/\max_j|b_j|$ (a function-value magnitude indicator times coordinate scale $h$).
$h$ is the coordinate scale that places support points within the unit disk.
Either condition alone can hold for genuine poles, so both are required.
`cleanup_doublets` removes support points nearest the suspect poles and refits weights once on the remaining support points.
It leaves the original approximation unchanged and returns a new approximation with sample residuals before and after removal, leaving the adoption decision to the caller.

## Repeated experiments with noise and tolerance

The previous cell uses one random sequence and cannot distinguish its result from chance variation.
Generate independent noise repeatedly and compare two tolerance settings through statistics.
One uses $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}=\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}=10^{-9}$, matching the previous cell's default; the other matches the noise level with $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}=k\sigma$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}=0$.
Residuals at samples other than support points are approximately noise magnitudes $|\eta_i|$; with independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$ real and imaginary parts, $|\eta_i|$ follows a Rayleigh distribution of scale $\sigma$.
Its distribution function is $1-e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$, so the median of the maximum of $N$ values is $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(N/\ln2)}$, about $3.1\sigma$ at $N=81$.
$k$ defaults to 5, larger than that value.

The next cell receives $\gamma$, $N$, $\sigma$, and random seed from the previous cell.
Starting from the same seed makes the first repetition's samples identical to the previous cell's samples.
Inputs are coefficient $k$ and repetition count $R$; both settings fit the same noisy samples in each repetition.
Outputs for each setting are the convergence fraction, mean support count, and mean number of poles beyond the two physical poles.
For damping width $\hat\gamma=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\hat p$ derived from the estimated pole nearest $p_+$, also report its mean, 95% confidence-interval half-width 1.96 SE, and difference between mean and true value.
SE is the sample standard deviation of the $R$ estimates divided by $\sqrt R$.
Each setting's termination status is reflected in its convergence-fraction column; no verdict is given for the whole cell.

## How to read the results

First inspect termination status and sample residuals to determine whether the approximation met tolerance or stopped at the support limit.
Then inspect unused-point errors, pole positions, and residues.
Doublet removal reduces support points and can increase sample residuals or worsen unused-point errors.
Compare unused-point errors too, rather than deciding solely from sample residuals before and after removal.
With measured values alone and unknown true poles, check pole-position stability when changing sample density and range, noise, and tolerance.
Question poles outside the lower half-plane or those with small residues and nearby zeros before interpreting them as physical resonances.

## Summary

The Lorentz-model dielectric function is a quadratic rational function with closed-form poles $p_\pm=-i\gamma/2\pm\sqrt{\nu_T^2-\gamma^2/4}$ and residues.
From noiseless samples, AAA recovers damping widths narrower than sample spacing with three supports, with poles and residues matching closed forms at rounding level.
Tolerance below rounding level introduces extra poles even if real-axis errors decrease; do not trust poles solely on real-axis accuracy.
With noise, tolerance above its maximum reduces extra poles, but physical-pole estimation accuracy barely changes under these conditions.
`cleanup_doublets` only returns a new approximation; compare sample residuals and unused-point errors after removal before deciding whether to use it.
Compare estimates through means and standard errors across repetitions rather than one random sequence.
