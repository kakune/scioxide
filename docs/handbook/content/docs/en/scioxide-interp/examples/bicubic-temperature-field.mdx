Numerical heat-transfer calculations and gridded weather data provide temperatures $T(x,y)$ at points of a rectangular grid.
Interpolate the grid values to find temperatures between grid points or the gradient $\nabla T = (\partial T/\partial x, \partial T/\partial y)$ needed for heat flux $\mathbf q=-k\nabla T$.
This example defines an analytic temperature field at coordinates $x,y$ [m] on a 1 m square plate, reconstructs values and gradients using only grid-point values, and compares them with the analytic solution.
Vary grid resolution and temperature-field frequency to inspect error magnitudes, distributions, and aliasing.

## Grid and value ordering

Grid coordinates are $x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{n_x-1}$ and $y_0 < y_1 < \dots < y_{n_y-1}$; spacing need not be uniform.
`BilinearInterpolator::new(x, y, z)` and `BicubicInterpolator::new(x, y, z)` take two coordinate axes and row-major flattened values `z`.
`z[j * nx + i]` is the value at $(x_i, y_j)$, with the $x$ index varying fastest.
Binary searches on both axes find the cell $[x_i, x_{i+1}] \times [y_j, y_{j+1}]$ containing the query point in $O(\log n_x+\log n_y)$, and computation within the cell takes constant work.
No global system of equations is solved.

## Bilinear interpolation and convex combinations

Let the cell widths be $h_{x,i} = x_{i+1} - x_i$ and $h_{y,j} = y_{j+1} - y_j$, and define local coordinates within the cell as

$$
s = \frac{x - x_i}{h_{x,i}}, \qquad t = \frac{y - y_j}{h_{y,j}} \qquad (0 \le s,t \le 1)
$$


Bilinear interpolation constructs the following from the four corner values $z_{00}, z_{10}, z_{01}, z_{11}$ (indices indicate corners at $(s,t)$):

$$
B(s, t) = (1 - s)(1 - t) z_{00} + s(1 - t) z_{10} + (1 - s)t z_{01} + st z_{11}
$$


All weights are nonnegative and sum to 1, so $B$ is a convex combination of the four corner values.
Its value therefore stays between the minimum and maximum corner values, without overshoot.
For a smooth field, error is proportional to the square of grid spacing $h$ ($O(h^2)$).

$B$ is continuous across cell boundaries ($C^0$), but its partial derivative normal to a boundary jumps across it.
`BilinearInterpolator` does not implement `Differentiable` and does not return gradients.
For quantities requiring gradients themselves, such as heat flux, use the following bicubic interpolation.

## Bicubic Hermite interpolation

Bicubic interpolation represents each cell by a bivariate cubic polynomial

$$
C(s, t) = \sum_{p=0}^3 \sum_{q=0}^3 a_{pq} s^p t^q
$$


The 16 coefficients are uniquely determined by specifying the following four quantities at each of the four corners.

1. Value $T$
2. The $x$ partial derivative expressed in local coordinates, $T_s = h_{x,i}\,\partial T/\partial x$
3. The $y$ partial derivative expressed in local coordinates, $T_t = h_{y,j}\,\partial T/\partial y$
4. The mixed partial derivative expressed in local coordinates, $T_{st} = h_{x,i} h_{y,j}\,\partial^2 T/\partial x \partial y$

Taking the tensor product of one-dimensional cubic Hermite interpolation along the axes gives

$$
C(s, t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & s & s^2 & s^3 \end{bmatrix} M \begin{bmatrix}
T_{00} & T_{01} & T_{t,00} & T_{t,01} \\
T_{10} & T_{11} & T_{t,10} & T_{t,11} \\
T_{s,00} & T_{s,01} & T_{st,00} & T_{st,01} \\
T_{s,10} & T_{s,11} & T_{st,10} & T_{st,11}
\end{bmatrix} M^\mathsf{T} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ t \\ t^2 \\ t^3 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
M = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
-3 & 3 & -2 & -1 \\
2 & -2 & 1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
$$


$M$ is the matrix that converts endpoint values and slopes $(p_0,p_1,d_0,d_1)$ to cubic coefficients.
Adjacent cells use the same values and derivatives on their shared edge, so the surface has continuous values and first partial derivatives throughout ($C^1$).
Second partial derivatives can jump at cell boundaries.

### Estimating grid-point derivatives

`BicubicInterpolator::new` takes only grid-point values, without supplied derivatives.
Partial derivatives along each axis are estimated by differentiating the quadratic through three neighboring nodes.
At an interior node $x_i$,

$$
T_x(x_i, y_j) \approx \frac{h_+}{h_- + h_+} \frac{T(x_i, y_j) - T(x_{i-1}, y_j)}{h_-} + \frac{h_-}{h_- + h_+} \frac{T(x_{i+1}, y_j) - T(x_i, y_j)}{h_+}
$$


where $h_- = x_i - x_{i-1}$ and $h_+ = x_{i+1} - x_i$.
At an endpoint, it uses a one-sided quadratic through three inward nodes; on an axis with only two nodes, it uses a first-order difference.
The mixed partial derivative $T_{xy}$ is estimated by the tensor product obtained by applying the same differences successively along both axes.

This estimate reproduces quadratics exactly and has error $O(h^2)$ for smooth fields.
At equally spaced interior nodes, it becomes the centered difference $\bigl(T(x_{i+1})-T(x_{i-1})\bigr)/(2h)$, whose leading error term is $h^2 T_{xxx}/6$.
The error in the local-coordinate slope $T_s=h\,T_x$ is $O(h^3)$, larger than the $O(h^4)$ error of Hermite interpolation itself.
Bicubic interpolation with estimated derivatives is therefore expected to have value error $O(h^3)$ and gradient error $O(h^2)$.
In interior cells of an equally spaced grid, this construction equals the tensor product of Keys's (1981) cubic convolution interpolation (Catmull–Rom type, $a=-1/2$); Keys also shows that the value error is $O(h^3)$.
`BicubicInterpolator` implements `Differentiable` and `TwiceDifferentiable`, returning physical gradients/Hessians.

## Analytic temperature field and sampling

Define the following analytic temperature field on the unit square $[0, 1]^2$ [m]:

$$
T(x, y) = 20 + A \sin(m \pi x) \cos(m \pi y) + 0.8 x y\quad[{}^\circ\mathrm C]
$$


$A=4$ °C is the oscillation amplitude, and $m$ is the number of half-wavelengths along each axis, with half-wavelength $1/m$ m.
Its gradient is

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial x} = A m \pi \cos(m \pi x) \cos(m \pi y) + 0.8 y, \qquad \frac{\partial T}{\partial y} = -A m \pi \sin(m \pi x) \sin(m \pi y) + 0.8 x\quad[{}^\circ\mathrm C/\mathrm m]
$$

The oscillatory part grows in proportion to $m$.
Both bilinear and bicubic interpolation reproduce the constant 20 and $0.8xy$ exactly, so both errors arise only from the oscillatory part and are proportional to $A$.

With $n$ nodes per axis, the number of grid intervals per half-wavelength is $(n - 1)/m$.
When this value is at most 1, the grid cannot represent the oscillation.
For example, with $m=4$ and $n=5$, $\sin(4\pi x_i)=0$ at every node $x_i=i/4$, so the oscillation is absent from grid-point values.
This is called aliasing; changing the interpolation method cannot recover the lost oscillation.
The sampling-theorem condition $(n-1)/m>1$ is only necessary; accurate values and gradients from local polynomial interpolation require more intervals per half-wavelength.
Determine the required grid resolution by checking how error decreases under refinement.

The following block defines quantities shared by the two cells on this page.
`temperature` and `temperature_gradient` define the analytic field $T$ and $\nabla T$; `grid_models` constructs bilinear and bicubic interpolants from values on an equally spaced $n\times n$ grid, and `validation_grid` supplies the $41\times41$ validation grid.
The analytic field's sine and cosine are computed with `RealScalar::sin` and `RealScalar::cos`.

```rust
//| id: interp-bicubic-field-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Analytic temperature field and two grid interpolants"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-interp]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, RealScalar};
use scioxide_interp::{BicubicInterpolator, BilinearInterpolator};

/// Oscillation amplitude A [°C].
pub(crate) const AMPLITUDE: f64 = 4.0;

/// T(x, y) = 20 + A sin(mπx) cos(mπy) + 0.8xy [°C].
pub(crate) fn temperature(m: f64, x: f64, y: f64) -> f64 {
    let w = m * std::f64::consts::PI;
    20.0 + AMPLITUDE * RealScalar::sin(w * x) * RealScalar::cos(w * y) + 0.8 * x * y
}

/// ∇T = (∂T/∂x, ∂T/∂y) [°C/m].
pub(crate) fn temperature_gradient(m: f64, x: f64, y: f64) -> [f64; 2] {
    let w = m * std::f64::consts::PI;
    [
        AMPLITUDE * w * RealScalar::cos(w * x) * RealScalar::cos(w * y) + 0.8 * y,
        -AMPLITUDE * w * RealScalar::sin(w * x) * RealScalar::sin(w * y) + 0.8 * x,
    ]
}

/// Two interpolants from values on an equally spaced grid with n points per axis (row-major z[j·n + i] = T(x_i, y_j)).
pub(crate) fn grid_models(
    n: usize,
    m: f64,
) -> Result<(BilinearInterpolator<f64>, BicubicInterpolator<f64>), SciOxideError> {
    let nodes: Vec<f64> = (0..n).map(|i| i as f64 / (n - 1) as f64).collect();
    let values: Vec<f64> = nodes
        .iter()
        .flat_map(|&y| nodes.iter().map(move |&x| temperature(m, x, y)))
        .collect();
    let bilinear = BilinearInterpolator::new(nodes.clone(), nodes.clone(), values.clone())?;
    let bicubic = BicubicInterpolator::new(nodes.clone(), nodes, values)?;
    Ok((bilinear, bicubic))
}

/// 41×41 validation grid [x, y] (x varies fastest).
pub(crate) fn validation_grid() -> Vec<[f64; 2]> {
    (0..41 * 41)
        .map(|k| [(k % 41) as f64 / 40.0, (k / 41) as f64 / 40.0])
        .collect()
}
```

## Experiments with cross sections and error magnitudes

```rust
//| id: interp-bicubic-temperature-field
//| ref: interp-bicubic-temperature-field
//| title: "Compare the methods by varying grid temperature-field resolution and frequency"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [interp-bicubic-field-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-interp]
//| exports: [error_maps]
//| inputs:
//|   grid_node_count: {"type":"range","label":"Nodes per axis n [points]","description":"Change the grid spacing 1/(n − 1).","min":4,"max":14,"step":1,"value":7}
//|   spatial_cycles: {"type":"range","label":"Number of half-wavelengths m [-]","description":"Change the spatial frequency of the temperature oscillation the grid must represent.","min":1,"max":4,"step":1,"value":2}
//|   temperature_section_y: {"type":"range","label":"Cross-section position y* [m]","description":"Move the cross section being compared.","min":0,"max":1,"step":0.05,"value":0.35}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"fine-grid","label":"n = 14","description":"Refining the grid increases the advantage of bicubic interpolation.","inputs":{"grid_node_count":14}}
//|   - {"id":"aliased","label":"n = 5, m = 4","description":"Aliasing makes the oscillation zero at grid points, giving both RMSEs nearly A/2 = 2 °C.","inputs":{"grid_node_count":5,"spatial_cycles":4}}
//|   - {"id":"under-resolved","label":"n = 4, m = 4","description":"At 0.75 intervals per half-wavelength, bicubic RMSE exceeds bilinear RMSE.","inputs":{"grid_node_count":4,"spatial_cycles":4}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":0.348,"atol":1e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":0.097,"atol":1e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"resolution","path":"/rows/0/0","approx":{"value":3,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"section","path":"/rows/0/2","approx":{"value":3.146,"atol":0.01}}
//|   - case: fine-grid
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":0.0071,"atol":1e-4}}
//|   - case: aliased
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":1.999,"atol":1e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":1.999,"atol":1e-3}}
//|   - case: under-resolved
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"resolution","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"min":1}}
use scioxide_core::RealScalar;
use scioxide_interp::{Differentiable, ExtrapolationPolicy, Interpolant};

let n = grid_node_count.round() as usize;
let m = spatial_cycles.round();
let (bilinear, bicubic) = grid_models(n, m).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let reject = ExtrapolationPolicy::Reject;

// Independent check: measure signed errors against the analytic field on the validation grid [x, y, interpolated − analytic value]
let mut maps = [Vec::new(), Vec::new()];
for p in validation_grid() {
    let exact = temperature(m, p[0], p[1]);
    let linear = bilinear.evaluate(p, reject).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let cubic = bicubic.evaluate(p, reject).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    maps[0].push([p[0], p[1], linear - exact]);
    maps[1].push([p[0], p[1], cubic - exact]);
}
let rmse = maps.each_ref().map(|map| {
    RealScalar::sqrt(map.iter().map(|e| e[2] * e[2]).sum::<f64>() / map.len() as f64)
});
let maximum = maps
    .each_ref()
    .map(|map| map.iter().fold(0.0_f64, |a, e| a.max(e[2].abs())));
let sig3 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 3 });
emit_table_with_columns!(
    serde_json::json!([
        { "key": "0", "label": "Method" },
        { "key": "1", "label": "RMSE [°C]", "type": "number", "format": sig3 },
        { "key": "2", "label": "Maximum error [°C]", "type": "number", "format": sig3 }
    ]),
    vec![("Bilinear", rmse[0], maximum[0]), ("Bicubic", rmse[1], maximum[1])],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "errors" })
);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    serde_json::json!([
        {
            "key": "0",
            "label": "Grid intervals per half-wavelength (n − 1)/m [-]",
            "type": "number",
            "format": sig3
        },
        {
            "key": "1",
            "label": "RMSE ratio (bicubic / bilinear) [-]",
            "type": "number",
            "format": sig3
        }
    ]),
    vec![((n - 1) as f64 / m, rmse[1] / rmse[0])],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "resolution" })
);

// Temperature and ∂T/∂x on the cross section y = y*
let y = temperature_section_y;
let (mut exact, mut linear, mut cubic) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new(), Vec::new());
let (mut exact_slope, mut cubic_slope) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new());
for i in 0..=200 {
    let x = f64::from(i) / 200.0;
    exact.push([x, temperature(m, x, y)]);
    linear.push([x, bilinear.evaluate([x, y], reject).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?]);
    cubic.push([x, bicubic.evaluate([x, y], reject).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?]);

}
// Maximum cross-section errors: temperature (both methods) and ∂T/∂x (bicubic)
let worst = |curve: &[[f64; 2]], reference: &[[f64; 2]]| {
    curve
        .iter()
        .zip(reference)
        .fold(0.0_f64, |w, (c, r)| w.max((c[1] - r[1]).abs()))
};
emit_table_with_columns!(
    serde_json::json!([
        { "key": "0", "label": "Maximum bilinear temperature error [°C]", "type": "number", "format": sig3 },
        { "key": "1", "label": "Maximum bicubic temperature error [°C]", "type": "number", "format": sig3 },
        {
            "key": "2",
            "label": "Maximum bicubic ∂T/∂x error [°C/m]",

            "format": sig3

        "xLabel": "Position x [m]",

emit_line_chart!(

        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Analytic ∂T/∂x", "points": exact_slope, "style": dashed }),
```

With the defaults $n=7$ and $m=2$ (3 intervals per half-wavelength), bilinear RMSE is 0.348 °C and bicubic RMSE is 0.0966 °C, giving a ratio of 0.28.
Maximum errors are 0.88 °C for bilinear and 0.30 °C for bicubic interpolation.
At $n=14$ (6.5 intervals), the RMSEs become 0.0789 °C and 0.0071 °C, reducing the ratio to 0.090.
Bicubic interpolation gains more advantage as the grid is refined because the methods have different convergence orders, 2 and 3.
On the cross section $y^*=0.35$ m, the maximum bicubic $\partial T/\partial x$ error is 3.15 °C/m, about 20% of the section's oscillation amplitude $Am\pi|\cos(m\pi y^*)|\approx14.8$ °C/m.
The relative gradient error is much larger than the maximum temperature error of 0.11 °C, about 3% of the 4 °C amplitude.
At $n=14$, the maximum gradient error falls to 1.05 °C/m.
On the edge cross section $y^*=0$, one-sided differences increase it to 6.04 °C/m.

At $n=7$ and $m=4$, with 1.5 intervals per half-wavelength, the ratio rises to 0.71; at $n=4$ and $m=2$, it rises to 0.78, largely eliminating the bicubic advantage.
At $n=4$ and $m=4$ (0.75 intervals), the bicubic RMSE of 2.42 °C exceeds the bilinear RMSE of 2.18 °C.
At $n=5$ and $m=4$, both RMSEs equal 1.999 °C.
This is an aliasing example where the oscillation vanishes at grid points: both interpolants reproduce $20+0.8xy$ exactly, and the error is the entire oscillatory part $-A\sin(4\pi x)\cos(4\pi y)$.
Its RMSE over the continuous square is $A/2=2$ °C, agreeing with the table's value.
The cross-section position $y^*$ changes only the plots and third table, leaving the first two tables unchanged.

With the defaults $n=7$ and $m=2$, both maximum errors are negative and occur at $(0.25,0.075)$, near the oscillation peak $(0.25,0)$.
Interpolation underestimates the peak between grid points, concentrating errors near peaks and troughs with large curvature.
Setting $n=14$ in the cell above moves the maximum bicubic error of 0.025 °C to the edge at $(0.025,0)$.
At edge grid points, derivatives are estimated by one-sided quadratics, which have larger errors than interior centered differences.
The maximum bilinear error of 0.22 °C remains at the interior point $(0.275,0.5)$.
At $n=5$ and $m=4$, the plots are identical: the entire oscillation appears as error, with maximum error $-4$ °C $=-A$ at $(0.125,0)$.

## Summary

Measure value and gradient errors separately in grid interpolation.
Bilinear interpolation stays within the range of the four corner values but does not return gradients.
Bicubic interpolation returns gradients and Hessians, but estimates grid-point derivatives with three-node quadratics, making errors more likely to grow near edges.
Oscillations with at most one grid interval per half-wavelength are lost from grid-point values and cannot be recovered by any interpolation.
Choose the required grid resolution by checking error reduction against an analytic solution or under grid refinement.
