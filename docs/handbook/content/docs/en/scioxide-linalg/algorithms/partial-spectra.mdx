For large sparse matrices and matrix-free operators, only a few largest-magnitude or smallest eigenpairs are often needed instead of a full eigendecomposition.
Examples include low natural frequencies of vibration, the second eigenvalue determining the mixing rate of a Markov chain, and the ground state of a quantum system.
This page explains `PartialSpectrumSolver` and `LobpcgSolver`, which compute them using only operator products.
Shared conventions for method objects, workspace requirements, APIs returning owned values and accepting caller-provided output, and the termination reason `SpectralTermination` are in [Applying matrix functions to vectors and method objects](oxref:linalg-action-conventions).

## Partial spectra

### Krylov–Schur and thick-restart Lanczos methods

`PartialSpectrumSolver` computes only `count` eigenpairs of an $n \times n$ operator $A$.
Each cycle extends an Arnoldi basis to dimension $m$ (from the starting vector in the first cycle and retained vectors thereafter), computes Ritz pairs of the small projected $m \times m$ matrix (approximate eigenpairs formed from eigenpairs of the projected problem), and restarts.
Basis orthogonalization uses classical Gram–Schmidt, with a second orthogonalization when the norm drops below $1/\sqrt 2$ times its previous value (Daniel, Gragg, Kaufman, and Stewart, 1976).
The restart strategy depends on the operator structure.

- General operators use the Krylov–Schur method (Stewart, 2001).
  Reorder the Schur form of the projected matrix to put the desired Ritz values first, and retain their invariant subspace.
- Operators marked `OperatorStructure::Hermitian` use thick-restart Lanczos (Wu and Simon, 2000).
  Retain the desired Ritz vectors together with their coupling coefficients to the residual.

A restart retains $\max(\text{count}, d/2)$ vectors, capped at $d - 2$, where $d$ is the basis dimension reached.
Pairs already meeting the tolerance are retained first and checked again in subsequent cycles.
A cycle costs about $m - (\text{retained count})$ products, $O(nm^2)$ for orthogonalization, and $O(m^3)$ for the projected eigenvalue problem.

Ritz pairs are accepted only from residuals computed with fresh operator products.
This check runs in the first and last cycles, when the basis becomes invariant, and in any cycle where the Arnoldi estimate $\beta \lvert e_m^{\mathsf T} y \rvert$ meets the tolerance for a requested pair ($y$ is an eigenvector of the projected matrix, and $\beta$ is the last subdiagonal entry from extending the basis).
In the Hermitian case, Ritz values are recomputed from the same products as Rayleigh quotients $x^{\mathsf H}Ax / x^{\mathsf H}x$ (of the transformed operator for shift-invert).
The Hermitian assumption is checked for each projected matrix; asymmetry exceeding entrywise rounding error and the tolerance stops with `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`).

In the Hermitian case, convergence in an unrestarted Krylov subspace $\mathcal K_m$ can be estimated by Kaniel–Paige–Saad theory.
The angle between the eigenvector $x_1$ for the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$ and $\mathcal K_m$ has the following bound:

```text
tan ∠(x₁, 𝒦ₘ) ≤ tan ∠(x₁, v) / T_{m−1}(1 + 2γ),   γ = (λ₁ − λ₂) / (λ₂ − λₙ)
```

Here $v$ is the starting vector and $T_{m-1}$ is the Chebyshev polynomial of degree $m - 1$ (Saad, 1980).
The same formula applies to the smallest eigenvalue with the ordering reversed.
For small $\gamma$, $T_{m-1}(1 + 2\gamma) \approx \tfrac12 e^{2(m-1)\sqrt\gamma}$, so the bound decreases by only about a factor $e^{-2\sqrt\gamma}$ per added dimension.
This bound is a worst-case estimate determined only by $\gamma$ and the starting vector; actual convergence can be faster.
Nevertheless, when the gap between the desired eigenvalue and the next is narrow relative to the spectral width, even extreme eigenvalues can require many cycles.

### PartialSpectrumSolver settings, results, and reports

Defaults for `PartialSpectrumSolver::new(count)` are basis dimension 30 (`with_basis`), at most 100,000 applications including residual checks, tolerances $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}} = \tau_{\mathrm{rel}} = \sqrt{\varepsilon}$ (pass `ResidualTolerance` to `with_tolerance`), selection `LargestMagnitude`, and structure `General`.
The basis dimension must be at least `count + 2` (there is no restriction when it is at least $n$); violations are rejected as `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`), and `count` exceeding $n$ as `InvalidInput` (`OutOfRange`), when requirements are computed.
For a Ritz pair $(\lambda, x)$ with $\lVert x \rVert_2 = 1$, acceptance requires

```text
‖A x − λ x‖₂ ≤ τ_abs + τ_rel ‖A x‖₂
```


`SpectrumSelection` selects both the desired eigenvalues and output order: largest magnitude (`LargestMagnitude`), smallest magnitude (`SmallestMagnitude`), smallest real part (`SmallestReal`), or largest real part (`LargestReal`); ties are ordered by increasing imaginary part.
The execution options' iteration limit counts restart cycles (100 by default); the small projected eigenvalue problem is solved separately without an iteration limit.

Call the API returning owned values `solve(op, initial)` or accepting caller-provided output `solve_into(op, initial, &mut buffer, &mut workspace, cx)`; pass an operator reference (converted to `Direct`) or a `SpectrumOperator` as `op`.
The starting vector `initial` must be a nonzero vector of length $n$; a zero vector gives `InvalidInput` (`OutOfRange`).
The result `PartialSpectrumBuffer` (prepared with `PartialSpectrumBuffer::new(n, count)` for caller-provided output) holds complex Ritz values `eigenvalues()` even for a real operator, `eigenvectors()` with unit-2-norm Ritz vectors as columns, and residuals `residuals()` computed from fresh products, in selection order.
The buffer may contain fewer pairs than requested; all pairs meet the tolerance only on `Converged`.
On a limit or cancellation, it contains the most recent pairs for which residuals were computed, possibly none.
The report `PartialSpectrumReport` holds the termination reason, application count `applications()` including residual checks, and cycle number `cycles()` for the returned pairs (counted from 1, or 0 if there are no pairs).
The workspace `PartialSpectrumWorkspace` holds $O(nm + m^2)$ scalars and is reserved using `PartialSpectrumRequirements`.

### Shift-invert

For smallest or interior eigenvalues, use `SpectrumOperator::ShiftInvert { a, inverse, mass, shift }`.
For a shift $\sigma$, constructing a Krylov subspace from products with $(A - \sigma M)^{-1}M$ makes the method see eigenvalues $\theta = 1/(\lambda - \sigma)$, and selection also acts on $\theta$.
Thus `LargestMagnitude` finds the eigenvalues nearest $\sigma$, and each Ritz value is transformed back by $\lambda = \sigma + 1/\theta$.
Setting `mass` to `None` gives $M = I$ and the standard eigenvalue problem $Ax = \lambda x$.
Because the Krylov basis is orthonormal in the Euclidean inner product, even with Hermitian $A$ and $M$ and real $\sigma$, $(A - \sigma M)^{-1}M$ is Hermitian only when $M$ commutes with $A$ (for example, $M = I$).
For other mass matrices, select `OperatorStructure::General`; specifying `Hermitian` stops with `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`).
The caller prepares `inverse` by choosing a factorization and solve method; `shift` is the shift used by that inverse operator.
A product with `M` and the inverse action together count as one application.
Residuals use $A$ and $M$ themselves, and the test is $\lVert Ax - \lambda Mx \rVert_2 \le \tau_{\mathrm{abs}} + \tau_{\mathrm{rel}} \max(\lVert Ax \rVert_2, \lvert\lambda\rvert \lVert Mx \rVert_2)$.
A transformed Ritz value of 0 cannot be mapped back to $\lambda$ and stops with `NumericalInstability` (`Breakdown`).

As an example, compute the smaller eigenvalues of the one-dimensional Laplacian $A = \operatorname{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)/h^2$ ($h = 1/(n+1)$ m).
The closed form is $\lambda_j = \tfrac{4}{h^2}\sin^2\tfrac{j\pi h}{2}$, with eigenvectors $\sin(j\pi x_i)$.
For the smallest eigenvalue at $n = 100$, $\gamma = (\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)/(\lambda_n - \lambda_2) \approx 7 \times 10^{-4}$ is small, so the bound decreases by only a factor $e^{-2\sqrt\gamma} \approx 0.95$ per dimension.
For $A^{-1}$, the smallest eigenvalue of the original matrix becomes the largest-magnitude value $1/\lambda_1 \approx 0.10$, with a large $\gamma$ of about 3.0 relative to the next value $1/\lambda_2 \approx 0.025$.
Since $T_{m-1}(7) \approx \tfrac12 (7 + \sqrt{48})^{m-1}$, the bound decreases by about a factor 1/14 per dimension.

The following definitions block shares this example between two cells.
`laplacian` provides tridiagonal $A$ and the dense Cholesky factorization used for its inverse operator, `exact_eigenvalue` the closed form, `compare` verification against that form, and `termination_name` the termination reason name.

```rust
//| id: linalg-laplacian-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "One-dimensional Laplacian and closed-form eigenvalues"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-linalg]
use scioxide_core::SciOxideError;
use scioxide_linalg::{
    CholeskyDecomposition, CholeskyPivoting, HermitianInput, Matrix, SpectralTermination,
    TridiagonalMatrix,
};

/// Tridiagonal form of A = tridiag(−1, 2, −1)/h² (h = 1/(n + 1)) and Cholesky factorization of its dense form.
pub(crate) fn laplacian(
    n: usize,
) -> Result<(TridiagonalMatrix<f64>, CholeskyDecomposition<f64>), SciOxideError> {
    let s = ((n + 1) as f64).powi(2);
    let tridiagonal = TridiagonalMatrix::new(vec![-s; n - 1], vec![2.0 * s; n], vec![-s; n - 1])?;
    let mut dense = Matrix::zeros(n, n)?;
    for (idx, value) in dense.as_mut_slice().iter_mut().enumerate() {
        let (i, j) = (idx / n, idx % n);
        *value = if i == j {
            2.0 * s
        } else if i.abs_diff(j) == 1 {
            -s
        } else {
            0.0
        };
    }
    let factor =
        CholeskyDecomposition::factor(&dense, HermitianInput::Exact, CholeskyPivoting::None)?;
    Ok((tridiagonal, factor))
}

/// Closed form λⱼ = (4/h²) sin²(jπh/2) (reference value using standard f64 functions).
pub(crate) fn exact_eigenvalue(n: usize, j: usize) -> f64 {
    let h = 1.0 / (n + 1) as f64;
    4.0 / (h * h) * (0.5 * j as f64 * std::f64::consts::PI * h).sin().powi(2)
}

/// Smallest value in ascending order and maximum relative difference from λ_first, λ_first+1, … (None if there are no values).
pub(crate) fn compare(values: &[f64], n: usize, first: usize) -> (Option<f64>, Option<f64>) {
    let mut sorted = values.to_vec();
    sorted.sort_by(f64::total_cmp);
    let error = sorted
        .iter()
        .enumerate()
        .map(|(j, &v)| {
            let exact = exact_eigenvalue(n, first + j);
            (v - exact).abs() / exact
        })
        .reduce(f64::max);
    (sorted.first().copied(), error)
}

/// Convert a termination reason to its variant name.
pub(crate) fn termination_name(termination: SpectralTermination) -> &'static str {
    match termination {
        SpectralTermination::Converged => "Converged",
        SpectralTermination::EvaluationLimit => "EvaluationLimit",
        SpectralTermination::IterationLimit => "IterationLimit",
        SpectralTermination::InvariantSubspace => "InvariantSubspace",
        SpectralTermination::AccuracyLimit => "AccuracyLimit",
        SpectralTermination::ExecutionStopped(_) => "ExecutionStopped",
    }
}
```

With the defaults $n = 100$, $k = 3$, and $m = 30$, both methods return 3 eigenvalues with `Converged`.
The direct method requires 159 applications through cycle 9 (8 restarts), while shift-invert requires only 33 in cycle 1.
The relative difference from the closed form is $1.8 \times 10^{-16}$ for the direct method and $1.3 \times 10^{-14}$ for shift-invert.
The difference $8.0 \times 10^{-4}$ m⁻² between the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = 9.8688086789$ m⁻² and the continuous eigenvalue $\pi^2 \approx 9.8696$ m⁻² agrees with the leading term $\pi^4h^2/12$ of the second-difference discretization error.
The direct method object uses Rayleigh quotients of $A$ recomputed from the same products, whereas shift-invert maps Ritz values $\theta$ of the transformed problem back with $\lambda = \sigma + 1/\theta$; $\theta$ includes rounding error from the Cholesky solves.
This difference explains the slightly larger relative discrepancy for shift-invert, whose maximum residual is smaller ($1.3 \times 10^{-11}$ m⁻² versus $5.4 \times 10^{-9}$ m⁻²).
At $n = 200$, the direct method grows to cycle 20 and 333 applications, whereas shift-invert remains at cycle 1 and 33 applications.

Reducing the basis dimension to $m = 8$ leaves less information after each restart, and the direct method reaches the 100-cycle limit with `IterationLimit`.
The maximum residual of its 3 returned pairs is $0.56$ m⁻², far above the tolerance (about $1.3 \times 10^{-6}$ m⁻² for $\lambda_3$); the relative difference remains $8.6 \times 10^{-6}$.
Using this value without reading the termination reason would give eigenvalues correct to only about 5 digits.
Even with the same $m = 8$, shift-invert converges at cycle 3 with 25 applications.
The cost of shift-invert is preparing the inverse operator: here, $n^3/3$ operations for dense Cholesky factorization and $2n^2$ operations for triangular solves per application.

## LOBPCG

### Preconditioned block iteration

`LobpcgSolver` computes the algebraically smallest or largest eigenpairs of the generalized problem $Ax = \lambda Bx$ ($A$ Hermitian, $B$ Hermitian positive definite and the identity by default) using the locally optimal block preconditioned conjugate gradient method (LOBPCG; Knyazev, 2001).
Each iteration uses classical Gram–Schmidt with reorthogonalization to make the current block $X$, preconditioned residual $TR$, and previous search directions $P$ $B$-orthogonal both to one another and to the constraints $Y$, then applies Rayleigh–Ritz in their span.
For block width $k$, the projected eigenvalue problem has order at most $3k$ and is solved with `HermitianEigenDecomposition`.
$A$ and $B$ act after the new columns of $TR$ and $P$ are normalized and made $B$-orthogonal, so their products avoid errors due to cancellation in projection.
Each iteration therefore applies $A$ $2k$ times, $B$ $2k$ times if supplied, and the preconditioner $k$ times.
$AX$ and $BX$ are obtained from the Rayleigh–Ritz updates and recomputed with fresh products after the first iteration, every 50 iterations, on apparent convergence, and at a limit; acceptance uses only residuals from those fresh products.
The Hermitian assumption is checked on the projected matrix; a difference between $v_i^{\mathsf H}Av_j$ and $\overline{v_j^{\mathsf H}Av_i}$ greater than $2\tau\alpha \lVert v_i \rVert_2 \lVert v_j \rVert_2$ stops with `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`).
Here $\tau = \max(\sqrt{\varepsilon}, n\varepsilon)$, and $\alpha$ is the maximum of $\lVert Av \rVert_2 / \lVert v \rVert_2$ over columns $v$ of the trial basis.
This test is independent of the scale of $A$ and unrelated to the residual tolerance, so an exactly Hermitian operator passes, whereas an operator with a skew-Hermitian part larger than about $\tau \lVert A \rVert_2$ on the trial basis stops.
The preconditioner $T$ changes only the convergence rate, leaving the eigenvalue problem unchanged.

Construct the operator with `LobpcgOperator::new(&a)`, add a mass matrix $B$ with `with_mass(&b)`, and add a preconditioner $T$ (scioxide-core's `Preconditioner`) with `with_preconditioner(&t)`.
Passing an operator reference directly gives the standard unpreconditioned problem $Ax = \lambda x$.
Defaults for `LobpcgSolver::new(count)` are the smallest end (`LobpcgSelection::Smallest`; `Largest` is also available, but magnitude ordering from `SpectrumSelection` is rejected), at most 100,000 total applications of $A$, $B$, and the preconditioner, and tolerances $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}} = \tau_{\mathrm{rel}} = \sqrt{\varepsilon}$; acceptance requires $\lVert Ax - \lambda Bx \rVert_2 \le \tau_{\mathrm{abs}} + \tau_{\mathrm{rel}} \lVert Ax \rVert_2$.
The execution options' iteration limit counts outer iterations (100 by default); the small Rayleigh–Ritz eigenvalue problem is solved without an iteration limit.

Call the API returning owned values `solve(op, initial, constraints)` or accepting caller-provided output `solve_into(op, initial, constraints, &mut buffer, &mut workspace, cx)`; `initial` is an $n \times k$ and `constraints` an $n \times c$ `BlockVectorView` (storage with consecutive columns).
The number of constraint columns $c$ is read on each call; prepare the workspace with `workspace(op, c)`.
$k + c$ exceeding $n$ gives `InvalidInput` (`OutOfRange`); numerically dependent constraint columns are discarded.
If the initial block becomes linearly dependent after $B$-orthogonalization against the constraints, the result is `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`); if $B$ is not positive definite on the search subspace, it is `DomainError`.
The result `LobpcgBuffer` holds real Ritz values ordered from the selected end, Ritz vectors normalized to $x^{\mathsf H}Bx = 1$, and residuals $\lVert Ax - \lambda Bx \rVert_2$.
On a limit or cancellation, it holds the last block whose residuals were recomputed with fresh products; if there is none, the buffer is empty and `iterations()` is 0.
The report `LobpcgReport` holds the termination reason, application count `applications()` for $A$, $B$, and the preconditioner, and iteration number `iterations()` for the block in the buffer.
The workspace `LobpcgWorkspace` holds $O(n(c + k))$ scalars, with requirements `LobpcgRequirements`.

//|   - {"id":"no-preconditioner","label":"None","description":"Stops at the outer limit of 100 iterations; the output block does not meet the tolerance.","inputs":{"precondition":false}}

use scioxide_linalg::{LobpcgOperator, LobpcgSolver, SpectralTermination};

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let sig11 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 11 });
let (n, k) = (points.round() as usize, count.round() as usize);
let (matrix, factor) = laplacian(n).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let a = matrix.view();
let inverse = factor.inverse_operator();
let pencil = if precondition {
    LobpcgOperator::new(&a).with_preconditioner(&inverse)
} else {
    LobpcgOperator::new(&a)
};
// Construct the initial n × k block (consecutive columns) using deterministic quasirandom values.
let block: Vec<f64> = (0..n * k)
    .map(|idx| ((idx as f64 + 1.0) * 0.618_033_988_75).fract() - 0.5)
    .collect();
// The constraints have one column, the smallest eigenvector sin(πxᵢ), or zero columns.
let h = 1.0 / (n + 1) as f64;
let mode: Vec<f64> = if constrain {
    (1..=n)
        .map(|i| (std::f64::consts::PI * i as f64 * h).sin())
        .collect()
} else {
    Vec::new()
};
let (output, report) = LobpcgSolver::new(k)
    .solve(
        pencil,
        BlockVectorView::new(&block, n, k).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,
        BlockVectorView::new(&mode, n, usize::from(constrain)).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,
    )
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

    .map(|j| {
        let norm2: f64 = (0..n)
            .map(|i| vectors.get(i, j).map_or(0.0, |v| v * v))
            .sum();
        (norm2 - 1.0).abs()
    })
    .reduce(f64::max);

    { "key": "7", "label": "max |xᴴx − 1| [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": "None" }
]);

    termination_name(report.termination()),
    output.eigenvalues().len(),
    report.applications(),
    report.iterations(),
    smallest,
    error,
    output.residuals().iter().copied().reduce(f64::max),
    normalization,
);

    columns,
    vec![row],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "lobpcg" })
);

    SpectralTermination::Converged => {
        Outcome::satisfied("The k pairs in the block passed the residual check")
    }
    SpectralTermination::AccuracyLimit => Outcome::failed("The block failed the accuracy check"),
    _ => Outcome::partial("Stopped at a limit; the output block does not meet the tolerance")
        .explanation("Supply a preconditioner or increase the iteration limit."),
};
emit_outcome!(
    outcome
        .status(termination_name(report.termination()))
        .diagnostic("Applications", report.applications() as f64, None)
        .diagnostic("Iterations", report.iterations() as f64, None)
);

With the defaults $n = 100$ and $k = 3$ and preconditioner $T = A^{-1}$, LOBPCG reaches `Converged` in 11 iterations, with 96 total applications of $A$ and the preconditioner.
The relative difference from the closed form is $1.3 \times 10^{-14}$, the maximum residual is $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m⁻², and the Ritz vectors are normalized to $\lvert x^{\mathsf H}x - 1 \rvert \le 4.4 \times 10^{-16}$.
The eigenvalue error is much smaller than the residual because, for Hermitian problems, Rayleigh-quotient error is roughly the squared residual divided by the eigenvalue gap.
Removing the preconditioner slows convergence because of the condition number $\lambda_n/\lambda_1 \approx 4 \times 10^{3}$, reaching the 100-iteration limit with `IterationLimit` (603 applications, relative difference $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$, maximum residual $0.26$ m⁻²).
The application count is approximately the $2k = 6$ applications of $A$ per iteration multiplied by 100 iterations.
Supplying $\sin(\pi x_i)$ as a constraint returns 3 values starting at $\lambda_2 = 39.465687280$ m⁻² in 12 iterations (106 applications), with relative difference $5.0 \times 10^{-15}$ from the closed form.
Increasing the block width to $k = 6$ with the preconditioner still yields `Converged` for 6 pairs in 14 iterations (241 applications).
Using a known eigenvector as a constraint obtains subsequent eigenpairs with that direction excluded, without recomputing converged pairs.

## Summary

Compute a few eigenpairs of a general operator with `PartialSpectrumSolver` (Krylov–Schur); when the operator is known to be Hermitian, specify `OperatorStructure::Hermitian` to use thick-restart Lanczos and real eigenvalues.
For smallest and interior eigenvalues, if $A - \sigma M$ can be factored, passing its inverse operator to `SpectrumOperator::ShiftInvert` transforms the problem to one with large $\gamma$ and convergence in fewer cycles; the cost is factorization and a solve per application.
For extreme eigenpairs of a Hermitian definite pair with a good preconditioner, use `LobpcgSolver`; supplying known eigenvectors as constraints obtains subsequent eigenpairs with those directions excluded.
Both methods accept pairs only from residuals computed with fresh products, and a limit can return pairs failing the tolerance in `Ok`, so read the termination reason, pair count, and residuals before using the values.
