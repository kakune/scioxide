The basin of attraction of a local minimum is the set of initial points from which a local solver converges to that minimum; it depends on the local solver's rules as well as the objective function.
For a multimodal function with many local minima, local solvers such as gradient and quasi-Newton methods converge to the minimum of the basin containing the initial point.
Without global information such as a Lipschitz constant or convexity, finitely many evaluations cannot prove that a global minimum has been found.
They cannot exclude a deeper valley hidden very close to an unevaluated point.

Global search seeks candidate global minima subject to this limitation.
This page derives how the required number of evaluations grows with dimension from the probability that a uniformly chosen starting point hits the basin of a global minimum.
It then explains how to combine global search with local optimization and how to choose among SciOxide's global search methods.

None of SciOxide's global search methods claims global optimality.
Global search never terminates with `Converged` or `GradientSmall`, for which `OptimizeTermination::is_success()` is true.
The `SearchBudgetCompleted` produced after exhausting starting points or generations is a partial result returning the best observed point.

## Basin hit probability

Let $\mathcal{B} \subset \Omega$ be the basin of a global minimum within a bounded search region $\Omega = [\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{u}] \subset \mathbb{R}^d$.
For a starting point chosen uniformly from $\Omega$, the probability $p$ of falling in $\mathcal{B}$ equals the volume ratio

$$
p = \frac{\operatorname{Vol}(\mathcal{B})}{\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega)}
$$

.
The probability that none of $M$ independent starting points falls in $\mathcal{B}$ is $(1-p)^M$, so the probability that at least one does (the hit probability) $P_{\mathrm{hit}}$ is

$$
P_{\mathrm{hit}} = 1 - (1 - p)^M
$$

.
To make the hit probability at least $q$ requires $(1-p)^M \le 1-q$, or the following number of starting points:

$$
M \ge \frac{\ln(1 - q)}{\ln(1 - p)} \approx \frac{-\ln(1 - q)}{p} \quad (p \ll 1)
$$


The approximation uses $\ln(1-p) \approx -p$.
At $q = 0.95$, $-\ln 0.05 \approx 3.0$, so approximately $3/p$ starting points are needed.

The difficulty in this expression is the curse of dimensionality.
In a model with search region $[-1, 1]^d$ and a spherical basin of radius $r < 1$, the volume ratio is

$$
p_d = \frac{\pi^{d/2} r^d}{2^d \, \Gamma(d/2 + 1)}
$$

.
Here $\Gamma$ is the gamma function, the numerator is the volume of a $d$-dimensional ball of radius $r$, and the denominator $2^d$ is the box volume.
At $r = 0.5$ the ratio is $p_2 \approx 0.196$, but $p_{10} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-6}$, and the required number of starting points grows exponentially with dimension.
The spherical basin is an illustrative model; actual basin shapes and sizes depend on the problem and local solver.

Quasi-random (low-discrepancy) sequences distribute points evenly throughout a box, covering it with fewer starting points.
However, about $pN$ out of $N$ points fall in a small basin, so reaching it at least once still requires about $1/p$ points, just as with uniform random points.

### Hit probability versus dimension and radius

Vary dimension $d$, radius $r$ and target probability $q$ to examine the probability $P_{\mathrm{hit}} = 1 - (1 - p_d)^M$ that independent uniform starting points hit a spherical basin of radius $r$ in search region $[-1, 1]^d$ at least once.
This cell only evaluates the formulas above; it does not call a solver.
It computes the gamma function with `gamma` from scioxide-special.
For small $p_d$, $1-(1-p_d)^M$ subtracts two numbers close to 1 and suffers cancellation, so compute it in the form $-\operatorname{expm1}(M\ln(1-p_d))$.
Use a logarithmic horizontal axis because the starting point count spans many orders of magnitude.

//|   target_probability: {"type":"range","label":"Target hit probability q [-]","description":"Required hit probability.","min":0.5,"max":0.99,"step":0.01,"value":0.95}

use scioxide_special::gamma;

// Volume of a d-dimensional ball and its volume ratio p_d to the search box [-1, 1]^d.

let half_dim = f64::from(dim) / 2.0;
let ball_volume = RealScalar::powf(std::f64::consts::PI, half_dim)
    * RealScalar::powi(basin_radius, dim)
    / gamma(half_dim + 1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let fraction = ball_volume / RealScalar::powi(2.0, dim);
// Compute P_hit = 1 − (1 − p)^M as −expm1(M ln(1 − p)) to avoid cancellation for small p.

    .map(|i| RealScalar::powf(10.0, decades * f64::from(i) / 200.0))
    .map(|m| [m, hit(m)])
    .collect();

        "yMax": 1.0,

            { "kind": "line", "axis": "y", "value": target_probability, "label": "Target probability q" },

At the default $d = 5$ and $r = 0.4$, the volume ratio is $p_5 = 1.68 \times 10^{-3}$; $q = 0.95$ requires 1778 starting points, while 100 points achieve a hit probability of only 0.155.
At $d = 1$, $p_1 = 0.4$ requires six points; at $d = 12$, $p_{12} = 5.5 \times 10^{-9}$ requires about 550 million.
Increasing the radius to $r = 0.9$ gives $p_5 = 0.0971$ even at $d = 5$, requiring 30 starting points.
The curve rises from 0 to 1 around $M \approx 1/p_d$, so this rise shifts to the right with each added dimension.

## Global search and local optimization

Global search methods are useful for finding deep valleys, but local solvers improve solution accuracy faster near a local minimum.
The computation is therefore often divided into two stages.

1. Global search: use a modest evaluation limit to obtain a point near a deep valley.
2. Local optimization: start a quasi-Newton or Newton method from that point and refine until a local stopping test (such as `GradientSmall`) is met.

[Multistart](../multistart/) applies these two stages to each starting point; dual annealing and CMA-ES can incorporate local optimization at the end or along the way.
Successful local optimization establishes only first-order stationarity within that valley; it does not establish that the valley contains a global minimum.

## Roles of global search methods

All SciOxide global search methods operate on finite boxes and return no proof of global optimality.

| Method | Search mechanism | Suitable problems and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| `MultiStart` ([multistart](../multistart/)) | Run independent local solvers from a starting point design | Problems with fast local solvers and large basins. The volume ratio determines the starting point count |
| `DifferentialEvolution` ([differential evolution](../differential-evolution/)) | Mutate with population difference vectors, then update the population by crossover and pairwise selection | Continuous black-box functions without derivatives. Refine the best point by local optimization |
| `BasinHopping` ([basin hopping](../basin-hopping-and-annealing/)) | Perturb a local solution, minimize locally, and accept by the Metropolis rule | Landscapes with a staircase of local minima. Temperature has objective units |
| `DualAnnealing` ([dual annealing](../basin-hopping-and-annealing/)) | Combine generalized annealing visiting distributions, cooling and reheating with local optimization | Coarse global search and local optimization in one method |
| `Direct` ([DIRECT](../deterministic-and-surrogate-global/)) | Partition the box into rectangles and choose subdivisions using center values and sizes | Low-dimensional problems requiring reproducibility without randomness |
| `Shgo` ([SHGO](../deterministic-and-surrogate-global/)) | Select candidate minima from a Delaunay triangulation of samples and refine with local solvers | Low-dimensional problems requiring a list of multiple local minima |
| `SurrogateSearch` ([surrogate search](../surrogate-search/)) | Fit a kriging or RBF surrogate to observations and evaluate where the acquisition function is largest | Expensive individual evaluations where few evaluations are desired |
| `CmaEs` ([CMA-ES](../cma-es/)) | Generate offspring from a multivariate normal distribution and adapt covariance and step size | Continuous black-box functions with strong correlations between variables |

The one-shot function `minimize_global(f, &bounds, seed)` constructs default differential evolution from a sequence of lower/upper endpoint pairs and a seed, and solves with the box midpoint as the first population member.

## Summary

The probability that a uniform starting point hits a basin is determined by its volume ratio $p$; a hit probability $q$ requires approximately $-\ln(1-q)/p$ starting points.
The volume ratio decreases exponentially with dimension, making small basins in high dimensions difficult to hit using uniform starting points alone.
Because finitely many evaluations cannot prove global optimality, SciOxide's global searches do not return successful termination; they return the best observed point as a candidate.
Refine candidates by local optimization and check them against known properties, other seeds and other methods.
Choose a method from the table according to local solver speed, evaluation expense, dimension and reproducibility requirements.
