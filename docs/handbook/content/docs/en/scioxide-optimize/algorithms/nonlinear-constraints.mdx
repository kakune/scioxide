The constrained problems in this chapter have the following form.

$$
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n} f(\mathbf{x})
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
c_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0\ (i\in\mathcal{E}),\quad
g_j(\mathbf{x}) \le 0\ (j\in\mathcal{I}),\quad
\mathbf{l} \le \mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{u}
$$

$\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{I}$ are the index sets of equalities and inequalities; inequalities use the orientation $g_j\le0$, as in SciOxide's `Constraints`.
`Constraints` holds equalities, inequalities, and variable bounds (a box) together; `ConstrainedProblem` borrows the objective and `Constraints` to form one problem.
Constraint violation and objective improvement are different quantities, so do not judge solution quality from the objective value alone.
This page explains the conditions a solution should satisfy and the meaning of multipliers.
The residuals SciOxide returns at termination and their checks are covered in [KKT residuals and multiplier sensitivity](../kkt-residuals-and-sensitivity/).
The algorithms for each method are covered in [penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods](../penalty-and-augmented-lagrangian/), [sequential quadratic programming](../sqp-and-interior-point/), [nonlinear interior-point methods](../nonlinear-interior-point/), and [derivative-free constrained optimization](../derivative-free-constrained/).

## First-order optimality conditions (KKT conditions)

If $\mathbf{x}^*$ is a local optimum, the objective must not decrease to first order along any direction $\mathbf{d}$ that remains feasible at that point ($\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*)^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{d} \ge 0$).

Let the active set $\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^*) = \{j \in \mathcal{I} \mid g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0\}$ be the index set of inequality constraints that hold with equality at $\mathbf{x}^*$.
The set of directions preserving feasibility in the linearized constraints is

$$
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^*) = \left\{ \mathbf{d} \;\middle|\; \nabla c_i(\mathbf{x}^*)^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{d} = 0 \ (i \in \mathcal{E}), \quad \nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*)^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{d} \le 0 \ (j \in \mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^*)) \right\}
$$


Under a constraint qualification such as the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ), which requires the active constraint gradients $\{\nabla c_i(\mathbf{x}^*)\}_{i \in \mathcal{E}} \cup \{\nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*)\}_{j \in \mathcal{A}}$ to be linearly independent, $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^*)$ equals the set of directions that actually proceed within the constraint set (the tangent cone).
Then Farkas' lemma makes “there exists no $\mathbf{d}$ satisfying both $\mathbf{d} \in \mathcal{F}$ and $\nabla f^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{d} < 0$” equivalent to the representation

$$
-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{E}} \lambda_i^* \nabla c_i(\mathbf{x}^*) + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{A}} \mu_j^* \nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*), \qquad \mu_j^* \ge 0
$$


The objective gradient balances a linear combination of active constraint normals; the inequality normals allow only outward coefficients.
Under LICQ, these coefficients (multipliers) are unique.
Without a constraint qualification, even a minimizer may fail to satisfy the KKT conditions.
For example, minimizing $f(x)=x$ subject to $g(x)=x^2\le0$ has only the feasible point $x^*=0$, but $\nabla g(0)=0$, so no $\mu$ satisfies $1+\mu\cdot0=0$.

Set the multipliers of inactive inequalities to 0 and define the Lagrangian by

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_{i \in \mathcal{E}} \lambda_i c_i(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{I}} \mu_j g_j(\mathbf{x})
$$

The KKT conditions then consist of the following four conditions.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1. \quad & \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda}^*, \boldsymbol{\mu}^*) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) + \sum_{i \in \mathcal{E}} \lambda_i^* \nabla c_i(\mathbf{x}^*) + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{I}} \mu_j^* \nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0} & (\text{stationarity}) \\
2. \quad & c_i(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0 \; (\forall i \in \mathcal{E}), \quad g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) \le 0 \; (\forall j \in \mathcal{I}) & (\text{primal feasibility}) \\
3. \quad & \mu_j^* \ge 0 \; (\forall j \in \mathcal{I}) & (\text{dual feasibility}) \\
4. \quad & \mu_j^* g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0 \; (\forall j \in \mathcal{I}) & (\text{complementarity})
\end{aligned}
$$

Complementarity means that a constraint away from the boundary ($g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) < 0$) has multiplier 0, while a constraint with a positive multiplier ($\mu_j^* > 0$) lies on the boundary ($g_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0$).
Variable bounds can be viewed as the two inequalities $l_k - x_k \le 0$ and $x_k - u_k \le 0$.
`Sqp` and `InteriorPoint` return their multipliers separately from nonlinear row multipliers, as lower- and upper-bound multipliers for each variable (both nonnegative).

Multipliers can also be interpreted as sensitivities of the optimal value.
When the inequality is relaxed to $g_j\le\varepsilon$, the optimal value $f^*(\varepsilon)$ satisfies $\mathrm{d}f^*/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon=-\mu_j^*$ near a KKT point satisfying LICQ, the second-order sufficient condition, and strict complementarity (positive multipliers for active constraints).
$\mu_j^*$ expresses the value of relaxing the constraint (the shadow price).
The scale of a multiplier depends on how the constraint is written.
With $\widetilde c=ac$, the multiplier representing the same Lagrangian is $\widetilde\lambda=\lambda/a$.

## Second-order conditions and the critical cone

Stationarity $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{0}$ is only a first-order necessary condition; establishing a local minimum requires examining the curvature of the Lagrangian Hessian $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^2 \mathcal{L}$.

In constrained problems, the relevant curvature is along directions tangent to the feasible region rather than throughout the space.
Moreover, the objective increases to first order in directions away from constraints with positive multipliers, so only directions with zero first-order term require a curvature check.
Define the critical cone at $\mathbf{x}^*$ by

$$
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\mu}^*) = \left\{ \mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^n \;\middle|\; \begin{aligned} &\nabla c_i(\mathbf{x}^*)^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{d} = 0 && (i \in \mathcal{E}) \\ &\nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*)^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{d} = 0 && (j \in \mathcal{A},\ \mu_j^* > 0) \\ &\nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^*)^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{d} \le 0 && (j \in \mathcal{A},\ \mu_j^* = 0) \end{aligned} \right\}
$$


If every nonzero $\mathbf{d} \in \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\mu}^*)$ satisfies

$$
\mathbf{d}^\mathsf{T} \left( \nabla_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^2 \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda}^*, \boldsymbol{\mu}^*) \right) \mathbf{d} > 0
$$

then $\mathbf{x}^*$ is a strict local minimizer (its objective is strictly smaller than at other nearby feasible points); this is the second-order sufficient condition.
Conversely, at a local minimizer satisfying LICQ, the same quadratic form is nonnegative on the critical cone (the second-order necessary condition).
Even when objective curvature is negative, adding constraint curvature $\mu_j^*\nabla^2g_j$ can make $\nabla^2_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}$ positive on the critical cone, so examining $\nabla^2f$ alone cannot decide optimality.

SciOxide's derivative-based constrained solvers use only first-order KKT residuals for termination and do not check the second-order sufficient condition.
The inertia (numbers of positive, negative, and zero eigenvalues) returned by `SqpDiagnostics::inertia()` or `InteriorPointDiagnostics::inertia()` describes the last factored linear system and does not establish positive definiteness on the critical cone.
To check whether the resulting point is a local minimum, evaluate the original problem's reduced Hessian separately or compare objective values at nearby feasible points.

## Comparing methods and box handling

SciOxide's constrained solvers differ in how they handle multipliers and variable boxes.

| Solver | Method | Derivatives | Multipliers | Trial points and box |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `PenaltyMethod` | Quadratic penalty and BFGS subproblems | First order | Not retained as state | Treats each box face as an inequality row; intermediate trial points can leave the box |
| `AugmentedLagrangian` | PHR-type augmented Lagrangian and BFGS subproblems | First order | Updated at each outer iteration | Same as `PenaltyMethod` |
| `Sqp` | Quadratic programming subproblems and L1 merit function | First order (quasi-Newton Hessian approximation) | Obtained from subproblem solutions | Projects the initial point into the box and keeps every trial point inside |
| `InteriorPoint` | Primal-dual interior-point method and logarithmic barrier | First order; exact Hessian also supported | Retained together with slack variables | Keeps nonfixed variables strictly inside the box |
| `Cobyla`, `Cobyqa` | Interpolation models and trust regions | None | None | Projects trial points into the box |

When an exact Lagrangian Hessian is supplied through `ConstrainedProblem::with_lagrangian_hessian`, only `InteriorPoint` uses it.
If the objective cannot be evaluated outside its domain, choose a method that keeps trial points inside the box.

## Summary

The KKT conditions consist of stationarity, primal and dual feasibility, and complementarity; they are necessary for local optimality when a constraint qualification such as LICQ holds.
Without a constraint qualification, even a minimizer may have no multipliers satisfying the KKT conditions.
Stationarity alone does not establish a local minimum, so check the second-order conditions through the Lagrangian's curvature on the critical cone.
Multipliers are sensitivities of the optimal value to constraint relaxation; scaling a constraint by a constant inversely scales its multiplier.
If evaluation outside the box is impossible, choose `Sqp`, `InteriorPoint`, `Cobyla`, or `Cobyqa`, which keep trial points inside it.
