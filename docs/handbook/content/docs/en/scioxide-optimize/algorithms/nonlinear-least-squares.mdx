Fitting a model to measured data (curve fitting or an inverse problem) produces an optimization problem whose objective has the structure of a sum of squared residuals.

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^n} \Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^m r_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})^2 = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_2^2
$$

Here $\boldsymbol\theta$ contains $n$ parameters, and $r_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = (m_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - y_i)/\sigma_i$ is a dimensionless residual: the difference between observation $y_i$ and model prediction $m_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ divided by measurement uncertainty $\sigma_i$.

We mainly consider overdetermined problems that determine $n$ parameters from $m\ge n$ observations.
Assume that $\mathbf{r}$ is twice continuously differentiable and its Jacobian $\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ ($J_{ij}=\partial r_i/\partial\theta_j$) is available analytically, by automatic differentiation, or by differences.
Passing this problem to general scalar minimization cannot use the residual vector's structure: a rectangular Jacobian and a Hessian approximation at small-residual solutions.
This page derives the Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt methods specialized for nonlinear least squares and explains how SciOxide's `LeastSquares` implements them and when it stops.
Observation weights, robust losses, and complex residuals are covered in [robust losses and observation weights](../robust-and-weighted-least-squares/).

## Deriving Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt methods

The gradient and Hessian of the objective $\Phi=\tfrac12\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2$ are

$$
\nabla\Phi = \mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{r}, \qquad \nabla^2\Phi = \mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J} + \sum_{i=1}^m r_i\,\nabla^2 r_i
$$


The second term is proportional to the residuals $r_i$, so near a solution where the model closely reproduces the data (small residuals), $\nabla^2\Phi\approx\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}$; first derivatives alone then approximate the Hessian.

### Gauss–Newton method

Replacing the residuals by the linear approximation $\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{p})\approx\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}$ makes the step $\mathbf{p}$ the solution of the linear least-squares problem

$$
\min_{\mathbf{p}}\ \tfrac12\|\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{r}\|_2^2
$$

whose stationarity condition is the normal equation $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{p}=-\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{r}$.
This replaces the Hessian in the Newton equation by $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}$.
If $\mathbf{J}$ has full column rank, $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}\succ0$, and $\nabla\Phi^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{p}=-\|\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}\|^2<0$ makes $\mathbf{p}$ a descent direction.
Convergence is quadratic near a zero-residual solution.
For large residuals or strong nonlinearity, local convergence is only linear, and the method may diverge if $\sum_i r_i\nabla^2 r_i$ is large relative to $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}$ (Nocedal–Wright, Section 10.3).

Forming the normal equations gives $\kappa_2(\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J})=\kappa_2(\mathbf{J})^2$, and $\kappa_2(\mathbf{J})=10^8$ loses all significant digits of double precision.
`LeastSquares` avoids the normal equations; for a dense Jacobian it solves $\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{p}=-\mathbf{Q}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{r}$ from the column-pivoted QR factorization $\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\Pi}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}$.
QR is backward stable and does not square the coefficient matrix's condition number, so for small-residual problems the step's relative error remains of order $\kappa_2(\mathbf{J})\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}$.
If QR detects numerical rank deficiency, or if $m<n$, the method constructs the minimum-norm step using only singular values above threshold $\tau$ in the singular value decomposition $\mathbf{J}=\sum_i\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\mathsf{T}$:

$$
\mathbf{p}=-\sum_{\sigma_i>\tau}\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{r}}{\sigma_i}\mathbf{v}_i
$$


The threshold is set by `RankPolicy`; the default `DimensionScaledEpsilon` is $\tau=\min(\max(m,n)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}, 1/2)\,\sigma_1$.
For problems supplying only products $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{w}$ without forming the Jacobian, the linearization is solved by LSMR, a Krylov subspace iterative solver (`LeastSquaresLinearSolver`).

`LeastSquaresGlobalization::gauss_newton()` shortens the undamped Gauss–Newton step by line search.
The step length $\alpha$ starts at 1; it is accepted if the actual reduction $\Phi(\boldsymbol\theta)-\Phi(\boldsymbol\theta+\alpha\mathbf{p})$ is at least $10^{-4}$ times the linearized model's predicted reduction $-\alpha\,\mathbf{r}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}-\tfrac12\alpha^2\|\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}\|^2$, otherwise $\alpha$ is halved.
At most 40 trials are attempted per iteration by default; if all are rejected, the method stops with `Stagnation`.

### Levenberg–Marquardt method and trust regions

The Levenberg–Marquardt method (LM) is a trust-region method that limits the range of trusted linearization by radius $\Delta$ and solves the subproblem

$$
\min_{\mathbf{p}}\ \tfrac12\|\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{r}\|_2^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}\|_2\le\Delta
$$


$\mathbf{D}$ is a positive diagonal matrix representing parameter scales.
The KKT conditions imply that, for some $\lambda\ge0$, the solution satisfies

$$
(\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}+\lambda\mathbf{D}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{D})\,\mathbf{p}=-\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{r}, \qquad \lambda\,(\Delta-\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}\|)=0
$$


At $\lambda=0$ this is the Gauss–Newton step; as $\lambda\to\infty$, it approaches a scaled steepest descent direction.
Since $\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}(\lambda)\|$ decreases monotonically with $\lambda$, choosing radius $\Delta$ is equivalent to choosing damping $\lambda$.
This system also avoids the normal equations and can be solved by QR as the augmented least-squares problem $\min\|[\mathbf{J};\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbf{D}]\mathbf{p}+[\mathbf{r};\mathbf{0}]\|$.

`LeastSquaresGlobalization::levenberg_marquardt()` (the default) uses Moré's (1978) method.
For the given $\Delta$, a safeguarded iteration reusing the Jacobian factors searches at most 10 times for $\lambda$ such that $\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}\|$ lies within 10% of $\Delta$.
Initial damping is automatic by default (`initial_damping` is 0); supplying a positive finite value to `LeastSquaresGlobalization::LevenbergMarquardt { initial_damping }` starts the search from that value.

Each column scale in $\mathbf{D}$ is the largest weighted Jacobian column norm seen so far.
If the problem returns a positive scale $s_j$ through `LeastSquaresProblem::parameter_scale`, that column is fixed at $D_{jj}=1/s_j$.
The initial radius is $\Delta_0=100\|\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\|$ (100 if this is 0).
Until the first trial is accepted, the radius shrinks to the computed step length $\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}\|$ whenever that length is smaller.

Trial acceptance and radius updates are decided separately using the ratio $\rho$ of actual reduction to the linearized model's predicted reduction.

| Condition | Trial | Radius $\Delta_{k+1}$ | Damping $\lambda$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\rho\le0.25$ | Accept if $\rho\ge10^{-4}$ | $\gamma\min(\Delta_k,10\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}\|)$. $\gamma=0.5$; if actual reduction is negative, Moré's radius-update formula gives $0.1\le\gamma<0.5$ | $\lambda/\gamma$ |
| $0.25<\rho<0.75$ and $\lambda>0$ | Accept | Retain $\Delta_k$ | Retain |
| $\rho\ge0.75$, or $\lambda=0$ and $\rho>0.25$ | Accept | $2\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}\|$ | $\lambda/2$ |

Rejected trials reuse the accepted point's Jacobian and factors, searching again only for $\lambda$ at the new radius.
A trial point with nonfinite residuals is also rejected; the radius is halved and the search repeated.

One linearization costs approximately $2mn^2-\tfrac23n^3$ flops for column-pivoted QR of a dense Jacobian when $m\ge n$.
Without an analytic Jacobian, forward differences supply it using $n$ residual evaluations (error $O(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}})$).
Dense storage requires $O(mn+n^2)$ memory.

### Stopping criteria

The default `LeastSquaresStopping::OriginalGradient` stops with `GradientSmall` only when the objective gradient norm at an accepted point ($\|\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{r}\|$ for squared loss) is at most the gradient tolerance $a+r\cdot1$.
The default $a=r=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}}$ gives a threshold of approximately $3\times10^{-8}$, an absolute quantity measured in objective and parameter units.
The step-length criterion $\|\mathbf{p}\|\le a_s+r_s\|\boldsymbol\theta\|$ (`StepTolerance`) is not a success criterion.
Gauss–Newton stops with `StepSmall` before a trial when the computed step meets this criterion.
LM stops with `StepSmall` when the step is small and the trial is rejected.

`LeastSquaresStopping::Minpack` is available only with dense Jacobians; it replaces the gradient norm criterion by criteria on relative objective reduction, the scaled radius, and orthogonality of residuals to Jacobian columns.
`Converged` under this criterion does not mean that the original gradient is small.
The default iteration limit is 1000; reaching it gives `IterationLimit`.

Check success with `LeastSquaresResult::is_success()` (`Converged` or `GradientSmall`).
Results stopped by `StepSmall`, `Stagnation`, or `IterationLimit` also return the last accepted point, but it is an estimate that has not met the gradient criterion.
Check whether such a point lies at the valley bottom using the gradient norm, a profile, or another initial value.

## Solving and retaining results

The one-shot function `least_squares(residual, &x0, m)` accepts a closure `Fn(&[S], &mut [S]) -> Result<(), SciOxideError>` writing residuals into the output and a residual count $m$.
It solves with the default `LeastSquares` (LM, squared loss, forward-difference Jacobian, 1000 iterations).
`curve_fit(model, &xdata, &ydata, &p0)` constructs residuals $r_i = g(x_i; \mathbf{p}) - y_i$ from `Fn(S, &[S]) -> Result<S, SciOxideError>` of the model $y = g(x; \mathbf{p})$ and solves with the same options.
Both return unsuccessful termination as `Err`.

To select a loss, Jacobian, or tolerance, or inspect partial results, use `LeastSquaresFn` and `LeastSquares::new(options)`.
The residual closure of `LeastSquaresFn::new(n, m, residual)` writes into an output of length $m$; the closure of `with_jacobian` writes row-major into an $m \times n$ `Matrix`.
`LeastSquares::new` returns `InvalidInput` if the loss scale is not positive and finite or the initial LM damping is negative or nonfinite.
`solve(&problem, &x0)` returns a result in `Ok` even for unsuccessful termination, so inspect `termination()` or `is_success()`.

For repeated problems of the same shape, specify dimensions and the linearization method (dense or products only) with `LeastSquaresWorkspaceRequest::of(&problem)`, prepare `LeastSquaresWorkspace` using `LeastSquares::workspace`, and pass it to `solve_into`.
Query required capacity with `LeastSquaresWorkspaceRequirements`.
Dense workspace and preallocated output avoid allocation during the solve.
`LeastSquaresWorkspace::last_accepted()` borrows the point accepted in the most recent call as `LeastSquaresAccepted` (parameters, residuals, and the objective value, iteration count, and gradient norm in `LeastSquaresProgress`).

`x()`, `residual()`, `cost()`, and `termination()` of `LeastSquaresResult` describe the same final accepted point.
`residual()` is the raw unweighted residual $\mathbf{r}$ and `cost()` is $\Phi$; use `scaled_cost()` when the value exceeds the scalar range.
`stationarity()` is the gradient norm (if evaluated), and `rank()` is the numerical rank of the last factored Jacobian.
By default, the point meeting the gradient criterion is not factored, so `rank()` can be `None`; request it with `with_condition_estimation(Estimation::Estimate)` when needed.

## Fitting exponential decay and interpreting termination reasons

Fit exponential decay $y(t) = A e^{-k t}$ ($A$ is initial concentration [concentration], $k$ is decay rate [1/h]) to observations generated from the true values $A=2$ and $k=0.35$ h$^{-1}$.
Use 21 equally spaced observations $t_i$ over $[0,T]$ and set $y_i = 2 e^{-0.35 t_i} + \sigma \sin(1.7 i + 0.3)$.
The sine term is a reproducible deterministic observation deviation, and $i$ is the observation index.
Inputs are the observation endpoint $T$ [h] and deviation amplitude $\sigma$ [concentration].

Use `LeastSquares` with Gauss–Newton globalization, an analytic Jacobian, and gradient and step tolerances $10^{-10}$ (absolute and relative) to estimate $(A, k)$ simultaneously from initial values $(1.5, 0.2)$.
For an independent check, fixing $k$ gives the optimal $A$ in closed form $A(k) = \sum_i e^{-k t_i} y_i / \sum_i e^{-2 k t_i}$; use this to calculate the profile objective $\Phi_{\mathrm{prof}}(k) = \min_A \Phi(A, k)$ by formula.
Even if the estimate stops before meeting the gradient criterion, if $\Phi_{\mathrm{prof}}(\hat k)$ matches the objective value and the point is at the valley bottom, $\hat A$ is optimal for $\hat k$.
The plot overlays the profile (dashed curve) and estimate (marker) on a logarithmic axis, with a vertical line at the true $k$.
The table shows termination reason, iteration count, estimates, objective value, formula-based profile value, gradient norm, correlation of the two Jacobian columns (cosine of their angle), and the number of points omitted from the logarithmic axis.
The cell ends with an outcome determined by the termination reason.

use scioxide_differentiate::DerivativeStrategy;
use scioxide_linalg::Matrix;
use scioxide_optimize::{

    .map(|i| observation_end * i as f64 / (count - 1) as f64)
    .collect();
let data: Vec<f64> = times
    .iter()
    .enumerate()
    .map(|(i, &t)| {
        2.0 * RealScalar::exp(-0.35 * t)
            + noise_amplitude * RealScalar::sin(1.7 * i as f64 + 0.3)
    })
    .collect();

let problem = LeastSquaresFn::new::<f64>(2, count, move |p: &[f64], r: &mut [f64]| {
    for ((ri, &t), &y) in r.iter_mut().zip(&rt).zip(&ry) {
        *ri = p[0] * RealScalar::exp(-p[1] * t) - y;
    }
    Ok(())

    for (row, &t) in j.as_mut_slice().chunks_exact_mut(2).zip(&jt) {
        let e = RealScalar::exp(-p[1] * t);
        row[0] = e;
        row[1] = -p[0] * t * e;
    }
    Ok(())

    .with_gradient_tolerance(
        GradientTolerance::new(1.0e-10_f64, 1.0e-10).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,

    let w: Vec<f64> = times.iter().map(|&t| RealScalar::exp(-k * t)).collect();
    let a = w.iter().zip(&data).map(|(u, y)| u * y).sum::<f64>()
        / w.iter().map(|u| u * u).sum::<f64>();

        .map(|(u, y)| 0.5 * (a * u - y) * (a * u - y))

    .collect();

At the defaults $T = 3$ h and $\sigma = 0.04$, the estimates are $A = 2.0046$ and $k = 0.3506$ h$^{-1}$; the objective value $7.98 \times 10^{-3}$ matches the formula-based profile value.
The termination reason is `StepSmall` and the outcome is partial.
The gradient norm $3.3\times10^{-10}$ is slightly above the threshold $10^{-10}+10^{-10}=2\times10^{-10}$.
The step criterion $\|\mathbf{p}\|\le10^{-10}(1+\|\boldsymbol\theta\|)$ was met before the gradient criterion; assigning equal step and gradient tolerances does not give the two criteria equal scales.
The point itself matches the profile minimum and can be used as an estimate, but record that it did not meet the gradient criterion.
With no deviation, $\sigma = 0$ reaches `GradientSmall` in 4 iterations and reproduces the true values $(2, 0.35)$.
The profile minimum is then 0; rounding makes the value at the grid point for the true $k$ zero, so that one point is omitted from the logarithmic axis.
With $\sigma = 0.15$, `GradientSmall` occurs after 6 iterations; the estimate $(2.017, 0.352)$ coincides with the profile minimizer, with objective value 0.112.
The gradient norm $1.2\times10^{-10}$ is then near its threshold, so rounding differences can change the termination reason.
Even with successful termination, deviation of the estimate from the true value is determined by the observation deviation and is not reduced by more accurate iteration.

Column correlations are $-0.846$ for $T = 0.3$ h, $-0.765$ for $T = 3$ h, and $-0.668$ for $T = 8$ h: their absolute values approach 1 as the observation interval shortens.
The profile valley also flattens: the ratio of the value at $k = 0.05$ to the minimum falls from approximately 150 for $T = 3$ h to approximately 4.5 for $T = 0.3$ h.
[Identifiability and statistical fitting](../identifiability-and-fitting/) explains this flatness as difficulty determining parameters through singular values and covariance.

## Summary

Nonlinear least squares uses the structure that provides the gradient $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{r}$ and Hessian approximation $\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{J}$ from the Jacobian alone, computing steps by QR, SVD, or LSMR without forming normal equations.
Choose `LeastSquaresGlobalization::gauss_newton()` for well-conditioned small-residual problems, or the default LM (which determines damping from radius $\|\mathbf{D}\mathbf{p}\|\le\Delta$) for distant initial values or strong nonlinearity.
Check success with `is_success()` (`GradientSmall` or `Converged`); treat points from `StepSmall`, `Stagnation`, or `IterationLimit` as the last accepted estimates and use profiles or other initial values to check whether they lie at the valley bottom.
For repeated solves, reuse memory through `LeastSquaresWorkspace` and `solve_into`, and request condition estimation when `rank()` is needed.
