Hyperbolic conservation laws describe transport of quantities whose totals are conserved, such as mass, momentum, and energy.
Solutions propagate at finite speeds, and discontinuities (shocks) can arise in finite time even from smooth initial data.
This page explains finite-volume (FVM) semidiscretization, Riemann problems, the numerical fluxes provided by `scioxide-pde`, and the types representing them.
Face reconstruction and boundaries are covered in [Conservation-law reconstruction and boundaries](../conservation-reconstruction/); CFL conditions, time integration, and spatial plans are covered in [CFL conditions and time integration for conservation laws](../conservation-time-integration/).

## Finite-volume semidiscretization

Suppose an $N$-component conserved quantity $u(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^N$ satisfies the following equation in a $D$-dimensional domain.

$$
\partial_t u + \nabla\cdot F(u) = 0
$$


$F(u)$ is the $N\times D$ physical flux, whose component in the normal direction $n$ is written $F(u)\,n$.
Integrating over a cell $K$ and applying the divergence theorem gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_K u\,dx + \sum_{f\subset\partial K}\int_f F(u)\,n_{K,f}\,dS = 0
$$


$n_{K,f}$ is the unit normal to face $f$ pointing outward from $K$.
Taking the cell average $\bar u_K = |K|^{-1}\int_K u\,dx$ as the unknown and approximating the face integral by the product of the numerical flux $\hat F$ and face measure $|f|$ gives the semidiscrete system

$$
\frac{d\bar u_K}{dt} = -\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{f\subset\partial K} |f|\,\hat F\bigl(u_f^{-}, u_f^{+}, n_{K,f}\bigr)
$$


$u_f^{-}$ and $u_f^{+}$ are the states on the inside and outside of the face: the cell averages themselves for a first-order method, or reconstructed face values for a second-order method.
Interior face fluxes enter two neighboring cells with opposite signs, so $\sum_K |K|\,d\bar u_K/dt$ contains only the sum of fluxes through exterior faces.
This discrete conservation is also a condition for obtaining the correct shock speed (the Lax–Wendroff theorem: if a consistent conservative method converges, its limit is a weak solution).

Spatial plans in `scioxide-pde` return this right-hand side: the sum of face fluxes divided by the cell measure $|K|$ (the mass-normalized residual).
A face is oriented by the unit normal from the left cell to the right cell, and `NumericalFlux` returns flux per unit face measure.

This page uses the following three equations as examples.

- Linear advection $u_t + v\cdot\nabla u = 0$: $N=1$, $F(u)\,n = (v\cdot n)\,u$.
- Directional Burgers equation: $N=1$, $F(u)\,n = (d\cdot n)\,u^2/2$.
  In one dimension with $d=(1)$, this is $u_t + (u^2/2)_x = 0$.
- Ideal-gas Euler equations: the state is $(\rho, \rho v, E)$, and the pressure is $p = (\gamma-1)\bigl(E - \tfrac12\rho|v|^2\bigr)$.
  Here $\rho$ is density, $v$ is velocity, $E$ is total energy per unit volume, and $\gamma>1$ is the ratio of specific heats.

## Weak solutions and Riemann problems

A smooth solution of the Burgers equation is constant along characteristics $dx/dt = u$.
When faster parts catch up with slower parts, characteristics intersect and the differential equation loses its meaning.
We therefore consider discontinuous solutions satisfying the integral form (weak solutions).
The integral form implies that a discontinuity moving at speed $s$ satisfies the Rankine–Hugoniot condition

$$
s\,(u_R - u_L) = f(u_R) - f(u_L)
$$


$u_L$ and $u_R$ are the values to the left and right of the discontinuity, and $f$ is the one-dimensional flux.
Weak solutions are not unique, so the solution whose characteristics flow into the discontinuity (Lax's entropy condition $f'(u_L) > s > f'(u_R)$) is selected as the physical solution.

A Riemann problem is an initial-value problem with piecewise constant initial data

$$
u(x,0) = \begin{cases} u_L & x < 0 \\ u_R & x > 0 \end{cases}
$$


Its solution depends only on $\xi = x/t$ and has the form $u(x,t) = w(\xi)$ (a self-similar solution).
For the Burgers equation, the solution is a shock with speed $s = (u_L+u_R)/2$ when $u_L > u_R$, and a rarefaction with $w(\xi) = \xi$ for $u_L \le \xi \le u_R$ when $u_L < u_R$.

The solution of a Riemann problem for the Euler equations consists of three waves: a left acoustic wave, a contact discontinuity, and a right acoustic wave.
The two acoustic waves are shocks or rarefactions; in the intermediate states between them, pressure $p^*$ and velocity $u^*$ agree on both sides, and only density jumps across the contact discontinuity.
$p^*$ is the unique root of Toro's pressure function

$$
f(p) = f_L(p) + f_R(p) + (u_R - u_L) = 0
$$


For $p > p_K$, $f_K$ ($K = L, R$) follows from the shock Rankine–Hugoniot conditions as

$$
f_K(p) = (p - p_K)\sqrt{\frac{A_K}{p + B_K}},\qquad A_K = \frac{2}{(\gamma+1)\rho_K},\quad B_K = \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}\,p_K,
$$

For $p \le p_K$, the isentropic rarefaction relation gives

$$
f_K(p) = \frac{2c_K}{\gamma-1}\left[\left(\frac{p}{p_K}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/(2\gamma)} - 1\right]
$$


$c_K = \sqrt{\gamma p_K/\rho_K}$ is the sound speed.
Because $f$ is strictly increasing, there is a unique root when $f(0) < 0$ (the condition excluding vacuum), and $u^* = \tfrac12(u_L+u_R) + \tfrac12\bigl(f_R(p^*) - f_L(p^*)\bigr)$.
The positions of the waves and the states within them follow in closed form from $p^*$ and $u^*$ (Toro, Chapter 4).

`scioxide-pde` does not provide an exact Riemann solver for the Euler equations.
As a reference for comparison with numerical fluxes, the following definitions block computes $p^*$ with Brent's method in `scioxide-rootfind` and returns the state at $\xi$ using the formulas in Toro, Chapter 4.

```rust
//| id: pde-conservation-exact-euler
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Exact ideal-gas Riemann solution (reference values)"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-rootfind]
use scioxide_core::{Interval, RealScalar};
use scioxide_rootfind::{Brent, ScalarFn, ScalarRootOptions, ScalarRootSolver};

/// Ratio of specific heats γ.
pub(crate) const GAMMA: f64 = 1.4;

/// Left and right primitive states (ρ, u, p), and intermediate-state pressure p* and velocity u*.
#[derive(Clone, Copy)]
pub(crate) struct ExactRiemann {
    pub(crate) left: [f64; 3],
    pub(crate) right: [f64; 3],
    pub(crate) pressure: f64,
    pub(crate) velocity: f64,
}

/// Wave function f_K(p). A shock for p > p_K, a rarefaction for p ≤ p_K.
fn wave(state: [f64; 3], p: f64) -> f64 {
    let g = GAMMA;
    let [rho, _, pk] = state;
    if p > pk {
        let a = 2.0 / ((g + 1.0) * rho);
        let b = (g - 1.0) / (g + 1.0) * pk;
        (p - pk) * RealScalar::sqrt(a / (p + b))
    } else {
        let c = RealScalar::sqrt(g * pk / rho);
        2.0 * c / (g - 1.0) * (RealScalar::powf(p / pk, (g - 1.0) / (2.0 * g)) - 1.0)
    }
}

impl ExactRiemann {
    /// Solve f(p*) = 0 with Brent's method. Reject pairs that produce vacuum.
    pub(crate) fn solve(left: [f64; 3], right: [f64; 3]) -> Result<Self, String> {
        let f = |p: f64| wave(left, p) + wave(right, p) + right[1] - left[1];
        let lower = 1e-12 * RealScalar::min(left[2], right[2]);
        if f(lower) >= 0.0 {
            return Err("This pair produces vacuum".into());
        }
        let mut upper = RealScalar::max(left[2], right[2]);
        while f(upper) <= 0.0 {
            upper *= 2.0;
        }
        let bracket = Interval::new(lower, upper).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        let function = ScalarFn::new(|p: f64| Ok(f(p)));
        let root = Brent
            .solve(&function, bracket, &ScalarRootOptions::default())
            .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        if !root.termination().is_success() {
            return Err(format!("Root finding for p* stopped with {}", root.termination()));
        }
        let p = root.point();
        let velocity = 0.5 * (left[1] + right[1]) + 0.5 * (wave(right, p) - wave(left, p));
        Ok(Self {
            left,
            right,
            pressure: p,
            velocity,
        })
    }

    /// (ρ, u, p) at ξ = x/t. Compute the right side with the same formulas by reflection x → −x.
    pub(crate) fn sample(&self, xi: f64) -> [f64; 3] {
        let g = GAMMA;
        let (p, sign) = (self.pressure, if xi <= self.velocity { 1.0 } else { -1.0 });
        let [rho, uk, pk] = if sign > 0.0 { self.left } else { self.right };
        let (xi, uk, us) = (sign * xi, sign * uk, sign * self.velocity);
        let c = RealScalar::sqrt(g * pk / rho);
        let w = if p > pk {
            let shock =
                uk - c * RealScalar::sqrt((g + 1.0) / (2.0 * g) * p / pk + (g - 1.0) / (2.0 * g));
            let m = (g - 1.0) / (g + 1.0);
            if xi <= shock {
                [rho, uk, pk]
            } else {
                [rho * (p / pk + m) / (m * p / pk + 1.0), us, p]
            }
        } else if xi <= uk - c {
            [rho, uk, pk]
        } else if xi >= us - c * RealScalar::powf(p / pk, (g - 1.0) / (2.0 * g)) {
            [rho * RealScalar::powf(p / pk, 1.0 / g), us, p]
        } else {
            // Inside a rarefaction: the Riemann invariant u + 2c/(γ − 1) is constant, and the characteristic slope is u − c = ξ.
            let cf = 2.0 / (g + 1.0) * (c + 0.5 * (g - 1.0) * (uk - xi));
            let uf = 2.0 / (g + 1.0) * (c + 0.5 * (g - 1.0) * uk + xi);
            let ratio = cf / c;
            [
                rho * RealScalar::powf(ratio, 2.0 / (g - 1.0)),
                uf,
                pk * RealScalar::powf(ratio, 2.0 * g / (g - 1.0)),
            ]
        };
        [w[0], sign * w[1], w[2]]
    }
}

/// Physical flux [ρu, ρu² + p, u(E + p)] in the x direction from primitive variables.
pub(crate) fn euler_flux(w: [f64; 3]) -> [f64; 3] {
    let [rho, u, p] = w;
    let energy = p / (GAMMA - 1.0) + 0.5 * rho * u * u;
    [rho * u, rho * u * u + p, u * (energy + p)]
}
```

## Numerical fluxes

A numerical flux $\hat F(u^-, u^+, n)$ returns one normal flux from the states on the two sides of a face.
The following properties are required for a conservative method to converge to the correct weak solution.

- Consistency: $\hat F(u, u, n) = F(u)\,n$.
- Conservation: use the same value for the cells on both sides of the face.
  Reversing orientation gives $\hat F(u^+, u^-, -n) = -\hat F(u^-, u^+, n)$.
- Monotonicity for scalar equations: $\hat F$ is nondecreasing in its first argument and nonincreasing in its second.
  A monotone flux combined with forward Euler satisfies a maximum principle under the CFL condition and converges to the entropy solution (Crandall–Majda).

### Godunov flux

Godunov's method solves an exact Riemann problem at each face and uses the physical flux of the state $w(0)$ at $\xi = 0$ as the numerical flux.
For a scalar conservation law, this value can be written as follows (Osher's formula).

$$
\hat F_{\mathrm{G}}(u_L, u_R) = \begin{cases} \min_{u_L \le u \le u_R} f(u) & u_L \le u_R \\ \max_{u_R \le u \le u_L} f(u) & u_L > u_R \end{cases}
$$


For the Burgers equation $f(u) = u^2/2$ with $u_L < 0 < u_R$, $\xi = 0$ lies inside the rarefaction (at a sonic point), and $\hat F_{\mathrm G} = f(0) = 0$.
Some approximate solvers leave a rarefaction shock (a discontinuity violating the entropy condition) passing through $\xi = 0$ in this case, so the treatment of sonic points is an important property of a flux.

`Godunov` implements this exact flux for linear advection and the Burgers equation.
It is not implemented for the Euler equations.

### Rusanov flux

The Rusanov flux (local Lax–Friedrichs flux) adds artificial viscosity to the average of the physical fluxes of the two states.

$$
\hat F_{\mathrm{Rus}} = \tfrac12\bigl(F(u^-)\,n + F(u^+)\,n\bigr) - \tfrac12\,a\,(u^+ - u^-),\qquad a = \max\bigl(|S_L|, |S_R|\bigr)
$$

$S_L \le S_R$ are the left and right signal speeds returned by the equation, enclosing the waves of the Riemann problem.
For a scalar equation it is monotone if $a$ bounds $|f'|$ from above, and it applies to any conservation law; however, its viscosity is determined by the maximum wave speed, so slow waves such as contact discontinuities are strongly diffused.
`Rusanov` can be used with any `ConservationLaw`.

### HLL and HLLE fluxes

The HLL method (Harten–Lax–van Leer) encloses the wave fan with two waves of speeds $S_L$ and $S_R$ and approximates the region between them with a single average state.
Applying the integral form over the fan gives the following when $S_L < 0 < S_R$.

$$
\hat F_{\mathrm{HLL}} = \frac{S_R\,F_L - S_L\,F_R + S_L S_R\,(u^+ - u^-)}{S_R - S_L}
$$


Here $F_L = F(u^-)\,n$ and $F_R = F(u^+)\,n$; use $F_L$ when $S_L \ge 0$, and $F_R$ when $S_R \le 0$ (upwinding).
When $S_L$ and $S_R$ enclose the true signal speeds, the average state equals the average over the exact solution's fan, and density and pressure remain positive for the Euler equations (Einfeldt et al., 1991).
HLLE is the HLL method using Einfeldt's wave speeds

$$
S_L = \min\bigl(v_L - c_L,\ \tilde v - \tilde c\bigr),\qquad S_R = \max\bigl(v_R + c_R,\ \tilde v + \tilde c\bigr)
$$


$v_K$ is the normal velocity, and $\tilde v$ and $\tilde c$ are the Roe-averaged velocity and sound speed.
The wave speeds in `IdealGasEuler` also include $v_R - c_R$ and $v_L + c_L$ in the minimum and maximum, giving a slightly wider enclosure.
`Hlle` is the HLL flux using these wave speeds and is implemented only for `IdealGasEuler`.
For the Euler equations, `Rusanov` uses the same wave speeds with $a = \max(|S_L|, |S_R|)$.

### HLLC flux

The HLL average state cannot represent a contact discontinuity, so density diffuses even at a stationary contact.
The HLLC method (Toro–Spruce–Speares) adds a contact wave within the fan with speed

$$
S_M = \frac{p_R - p_L + \rho_L v_L (S_L - v_L) - \rho_R v_R (S_R - v_R)}{\rho_L (S_L - v_L) - \rho_R (S_R - v_R)}
$$

and places two intermediate states on either side of it.
The intermediate states are

$$
\rho^*_K = \rho_K \frac{S_K - v_K}{S_K - S_M},\qquad
(\rho v)^*_K = \rho^*_K\bigl(v^{\mathrm{vec}}_K + (S_M - v_K)\,n\bigr),\qquad
E^*_K = \rho^*_K\left[\frac{E_K}{\rho_K} + (S_M - v_K)\left(S_M + \frac{p_K}{\rho_K (S_K - v_K)}\right)\right]
$$


$v^{\mathrm{vec}}_K$ is the velocity vector; tangential velocity is unchanged in the intermediate states.
The numerical flux is $F_L + S_L(u^*_L - u^-)$ when $S_M \ge 0$, and $F_R + S_R(u^*_R - u^+)$ when $S_M < 0$.
For an isolated contact discontinuity ($v_L = v_R$, $p_L = p_R$), $S_M = v_L$ and $u^*_K = u_K$, so the HLLC flux equals the exact flux.

If $S_M$ lies outside $(S_L, S_R)$, the contact pressure $p_K + \rho_K(S_K - v_K)(S_M - v_K)$ is at or below its admissible minimum, or an intermediate-state density or pressure is inadmissible, `Hllc` first repeats the same computation with extended-range arithmetic to avoid intermediate overflow.
If these conditions still occur, it switches to the HLLE flux and sets `used_hlle_fallback` to true on that face.
This switch occurs in near-vacuum rarefactions, where the HLLC contact pressure can become negative.

## Equation and flux types

An equation is represented by a type implementing `ConservationLaw<R, D, N>`.
`R` is the real scalar type, `D` is the spatial dimension (1–3), and `N` is the number of conserved components.
Its methods are listed below; each must be a pure function returning finite values.

- `validate`: checks dimensions and equation constants before preparing a plan.
- `admissible`: returns whether a state belongs to the admissible set (a convex set).
- `normal_flux`: physical flux $F(u)\,n$ in the unit normal direction $n$.
- `wave_bounds`: signal speeds $(S_L, S_R)$ enclosing the waves of the Riemann problem between two states.
- `riemann_data`: returns left and right physical fluxes and signal speeds together in `RiemannData`.
  The default implementation validates inputs and calls `normal_flux` and `wave_bounds`.
  `IdealGasEuler` overrides it to compute each state's pressure and sound speed once and share them between the flux and wave speed calculations.
- `reflect`: the reflected state at a slip wall.
  The default implementation returns an error.

`RiemannData` is an immutable value with public fields `left_flux`, `right_flux`, and `wave_bounds`; `Rusanov` and `Hlle` construct a flux from this value and the left and right states.
For a custom equation, its implementer must ensure that the signal speeds enclose the actual waves and that the admissible set is convex.

A numerical flux is a type implementing `NumericalFlux<R, E, D, N>`, whose `evaluate(equation, left, right, normal)` returns `RiemannReport`.
`normal` must be a finite unit vector satisfying $|\,|n|^2 - 1\,| \le 128\,\varepsilon$; other normals and inadmissible states are rejected with `InvalidInput`.
For `RiemannReport`, `flux()` returns flux per unit face measure from left to right, `maximum_speed()` returns $\max(|S_L|, |S_R|)$, and `used_hlle_fallback()` returns whether HLLC switched to HLLE.
A custom numerical flux constructs its report with `RiemannReport::new(flux, maximum_speed, used_hlle_fallback)`.
Because `maximum_speed` is used to compute the CFL limit, supply a speed that bounds the waves.

The three built-in equations are as follows.

- `LinearAdvection { velocity }`: linear advection with finite velocity `velocity`.
  All finite values are admissible.
- `Burgers { direction }`: the Burgers equation with flux $(d\cdot n)\,u^2/2$.
  `direction` need only be finite, not a unit vector.
  The signal speeds are the smaller and larger of $(d\cdot n)u_L$ and $(d\cdot n)u_R$.
- `IdealGasEuler`: a calorically perfect ideal gas.
  The state is `[ρ, ρv_0, …, ρv_{D-1}, E]` with $N = D+2$.
  `new(gamma)` requires finite $\gamma > 1$, and `with_minima(density, pressure)` sets nonnegative minima (0 by default).
  Admissible states have density and pressure strictly above these minima; exact vacuum is not handled.
  `conserved(rho, velocity, pressure)` constructs conserved quantities from primitive variables, `pressure(u)` computes pressure from conserved quantities, and `gamma()` returns the ratio of specific heats.
  `reflect` reverses normal momentum $m\cdot n$ and returns $m - 2(m\cdot n)n$.

The equation and numerical flux combination is determined by types; combinations without an implementation do not compile.

| Numerical flux | `LinearAdvection` | `Burgers` | `IdealGasEuler` | Wave speeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Godunov` | Exact | Exact (including sonic points) | None | $\max(\lvert S_L\rvert, \lvert S_R\rvert)$ |
| `Rusanov` | Available | Available | Available | Equation's `wave_bounds` |
| `Hlle` | None | None | Available | Einfeldt–Roe and endpoint eigenvalues |
| `Hllc` | None | None | Available (including HLLE fallback) | Same as `Hlle` |

## Comparing fluxes at one interface

The next cell places primitive states $(\rho, u, p)$ on either side of one interface and compares the `Rusanov`, `Hlle`, and `Hllc` fluxes with the exact Godunov flux $F(w(0))$.
The inputs are a pair of left and right states and a common velocity $\Delta v$ added to both sides.
The three pairs are the initial interface of Sod's shock tube, a stationary contact discontinuity (only $\rho$ jumps between 1 and 0.125, with $u = 0$ and $p = 1$), and Toro's double-rarefaction problem ($\rho = 1$, $p = 0.4$, $u = \mp 2$).
$\Delta v$ moves the wave fan relative to the interface, changing the upwind direction and which wave contains the interface.
All quantities are dimensionless, with $\gamma = 1.4$.
The table's “Difference from exact value” is the maximum absolute difference over the three components.

```rust

//| ref: pde-conservation-interface-fluxes
//| title: "Compare Rusanov, HLLE, HLLC, and exact Godunov fluxes at one interface"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [pde-conservation-exact-euler]
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-pde, scioxide-rootfind]
//| exports: [left, right, shift]
//| inputs:
//|   pair: {"type":"select","label":"Left and right state pair","options":["Initial Sod interface","Stationary contact discontinuity","Toro double rarefaction"],"value":"Initial Sod interface"}
//|   shift: {"type":"range","label":"Common velocity addition Δv [-]","description":"Add the same value to both velocities to move the wave fan relative to the interface.","min":-3,"max":3,"step":0.25,"value":0}

//|   - {"id":"contact","label":"Stationary contact discontinuity","description":"HLLC returns the exact flux, while an artificial viscosity term remains in the Rusanov and HLLE mass fluxes.","inputs":{"pair":"Stationary contact discontinuity"}}

    ("HLLE", Hlle.evaluate(&gas, &ul, &ur, normal)),
    ("HLLC", Hllc.evaluate(&gas, &ul, &ur, normal)),

let mut rows = Vec::new();
for (name, report) in reports {
    let report = report.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let flux = report.flux();
    let difference = (0..3)
        .map(|i| NumericScalar::abs(flux[i] - reference[i]))
        .fold(0.0, f64::max);
    let fallback = if report.used_hlle_fallback() {
        "Yes"
    } else {
        "No"
    };
    rows.push((
        name.to_string(),
        flux[0],
        flux[1],
        flux[2],
        Some(difference),

        Some(fallback.to_string()),
    ));
}
rows.push((
    "Exact Godunov".to_string(),
    reference[0],
    reference[1],
    reference[2],
    None,
    None,
    None,
));
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Numerical flux" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Mass flux F_ρ [-]", "type": "number", "format": digits6 },
```

At the default Sod interface, the mass component of the exact Godunov flux is 0.39539.
The interface $\xi = 0$ lies between the tail of the left rarefaction ($\xi = -0.0703$) and the contact discontinuity ($\xi = 0.927$), so the flux is the physical flux of the left intermediate state.
All three approximate fluxes differ from this value by about 0.18.
For this pair, the wave speeds in `IdealGasEuler` are symmetric, $S_L = -S_R = -c_L = -1.1832$, so the HLLE formula equals the Rusanov formula.
Einfeldt's wave speeds alone would give $S_R = \tilde v + \tilde c = 1.1519$, but including the endpoint value $v_L + c_L = 1.1832$ sets $S_R$ to that value.
The HLLC difference (0.1789) is also almost the same as HLLE's (0.1771).
The exact interface state is the left intermediate state and is unrelated to contact treatment, so adding a contact wave does not reduce the difference.

For a stationary contact discontinuity, the HLLC flux equals the exact flux $(0, 1, 0)$.
Rusanov and HLLE retain $\tfrac12 a(\rho_L - \rho_R) = 1.464$ in their mass flux, diffusing the density jump.
For the double-rarefaction pair, the HLLC contact pressure becomes negative and the HLLE fallback column reads “Yes.”
The approximate momentum flux is then $-1.097$, differing even in sign from the exact flux ($p^* = 0.0019$).
The HLL average state is an integral average over the fan, so density and pressure remain positive, but the interface flux value itself can be far from the exact solution.
At $\Delta v = 2$, both states move supersonically to the right; HLLE and HLLC return the physical flux of the upwind left state, matching the exact value.
Rusanov continues to add artificial viscosity with $a = 3.18$, giving a difference of 1.47.

## Summary

The finite-volume method for conservation laws expresses changes in cell averages as sums of numerical face fluxes; interior fluxes cancel, preserving totals discretely.
For scalar equations, `Godunov` returns the flux of the exact Riemann solution, while `Rusanov` applies to any `ConservationLaw` but adds diffusion proportional to the maximum wave speed.
For the Euler equations, `Hlle` is an HLL flux preserving positive density and pressure, and `Hllc` treats contact discontinuities exactly; `Hllc` switches to HLLE when an intermediate state is inadmissible and records this in `used_hlle_fallback`.
The single-interface comparison supports choosing `Hllc` when sharp contacts matter, or `Hlle` when robustness near vacuum takes priority.
Custom equations can be solved with the built-in `Rusanov` and spatial plans by implementing `ConservationLaw` and ensuring bounding signal speeds and a convex admissible set.
