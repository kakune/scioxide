A polynomial passing through all values at distinct nodes is uniquely determined.
Sensitivity to sample errors and approximation error between samples must nevertheless be assessed separately.
Barycentric and Newton forms evaluate without monomial expansion; node choice determines conditioning of the interpolation problem itself.
This page collects barycentric derivations, interpolation remainders, Lebesgue constants, and the Runge phenomenon, serving as a reference for the [Runge example](../../examples/runge-nodes/) and [scioxide-interp interpolators](../../../scioxide-interp/algorithms/global-polynomial-and-rational/).
Floating-point barycentric rounding and node-family weights are covered in [Barycentric rounding error and weights for node families](../barycentric-rounding-and-weights/).

## Uniqueness and representations of the interpolating polynomial

Given $n+1$ distinct nodes $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n$ and samples $y_0, y_1, \dots, y_n$, a unique polynomial $p \in \mathcal{P}_n$ of degree at most $n$ satisfies $p(x_i) = y_i$ ($i = 0, 1, \dots, n$).
The Lagrange form below constructs existence.

Uniqueness follows as below.
If two polynomials $p, q \in \mathcal{P}_n$ satisfy the same interpolation conditions, their difference $r = p - q$ has degree at most $n$ yet $n+1$ distinct zeros $x_i$.
A nonzero polynomial has at most its degree in roots, so $r = 0$, hence $p = q$.

### Barycentric Lagrange interpolation formula

Lagrange form is $p(x) = \sum_{j=0}^n y_j \ell_j(x)$ with $\ell_j(x) = \prod_{k \ne j} \frac{x - x_k}{x_j - x_k}$.
Direct single-point evaluation costs $O(n^2)$ operations, rebuilding the same products at each point.

Define nodal polynomial $\omega(x) = \prod_{k=0}^n (x - x_k)$ and barycentric weights $w_j$ as follows.

$$
w_j = \frac{1}{\prod_{k \ne j} (x_j - x_k)} = \frac{1}{\omega'(x_j)}
$$

These weights rewrite the Lagrange basis as $\ell_j(x) = \omega(x) \frac{w_j}{x - x_j}$.
Substitution gives the first barycentric formula (modified Lagrange formula).

$$
p(x) = \omega(x)\sum_{j=0}^n \frac{w_j}{x - x_j}\, y_j
$$

Interpolation of the constant $1$ gives $1$ itself, so $\sum_{j=0}^n \ell_j(x) = 1$, or $1=\omega(x)\sum_j w_j/(x-x_j)$.
Dividing the first form by this identity eliminates $\omega(x)$, giving the second barycentric formula.

$$
p(x) = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^n \dfrac{w_j}{x - x_j} y_j}{\sum_{j=0}^n \dfrac{w_j}{x - x_j}}
$$

After computing weights once in $O(n^2)$, either form evaluates arbitrary points in $O(n)$.
Common scaling of weights leaves the second form unchanged, allowing a free choice of weight scale.
The first form multiplies by $\omega(x)$, making $\omega$ prone to overflow or underflow at high degree.
Adding a node $x_{n+1}$ updates weights in $O(n)$ by dividing each existing weight by $x_j - x_{n+1}$ and forming one new weight.
SciOxide does not provide this update; rebuild interpolation after adding nodes.

SciOxide's `ApproxPolynomial::interpolate_barycentric` uses the second form.
Nodes and samples may be real or complex, in any node order.
Weights are computed once in $O(n^2)$, normalized to maximum absolute value 1, and stored.
No API accepts user-supplied weights, so the result is always the polynomial interpolating the nodes.
Empty nodes give `InvalidInput` (`Empty`), length mismatch `DimensionMismatch`, NaN or infinity `NonFinite`, duplicate nodes `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`), and normalized weights rounded to 0 `NumericalInstability` (`Underflow`).
`interpolate_barycentric_with_options` attaches `PolynomialApproximationPolicy` to the same interpolation; policy thresholds overflowing at the coefficient scale also give `NumericalInstability` (`Overflow`).
Borrow stored nodes and weights with `basis().nodes()` and `basis().weights()`; `basis().coefficients()` returns samples.
Use `to_barycentric(nodes)` to convert a monomial polynomial to barycentric form at supplied nodes.

Evaluation costs $O(n)$ per point without allocation.
[Barycentric rounding error](oxref:poly-barycentric-rounding) explains rounding, evaluation techniques, and condition estimates returned by `evaluate_with_derivative`.

### Newton interpolation and divided differences

Newton form uses successively nested nodal basis $1, (x-x_0), (x-x_0)(x-x_1), \dots$ and stores divided differences.

$$
p(x) = f[x_0] + f[x_0, x_1](x - x_0) + f[x_0, x_1, x_2](x - x_0)(x - x_1) + \dots
$$

Starting from $f[x_i] = f(x_i)$, the recurrence

$$
f[x_i, \dots, x_{i+k}] = \frac{f[x_{i+1}, \dots, x_{i+k}] - f[x_i, \dots, x_{i+k-1}]}{x_{i+k} - x_i}
$$

forms a table in $O(n^2)$.
If $f$ is $k$ times continuously differentiable, a point $\xi$ satisfying $f[x_0,\dots,x_k] = f^{(k)}(\xi)/k!$ lies in the smallest interval containing the nodes.
As nodes coincide, divided differences become derivatives, giving Hermite interpolation matching values and slopes.

Appending a node leaves existing divided differences unchanged; adding one table row raises degree by one.
Nested-product evaluation $p(x)=f[x_0]+(x-x_0)(f[x_0,x_1]+(x-x_1)(\cdots))$ costs $O(n)$.
Divided differences divide by nearby node differences, so rounding amplification depends on node order and spacing.
Leja ordering (choose next the point maximizing the product of distances from previously selected nodes) is known to reduce amplification (Reichel, 1990).

`ApproxPolynomial::interpolate_newton` computes real-node, real-sample divided differences in $O(n^2)$ and returns a Newton-form polynomial.
Nodes remain in supplied order without sorting.
Intermediate divided-difference overflow or underflow uses extended exponent range; only final coefficients must fit the scalar range.
Exact trailing zero coefficients and their nodes are removed, so collinear samples give a linear polynomial.
Input errors match `interpolate_barycentric`; final coefficient overflow or a nonzero coefficient underflowing to 0 gives `NumericalInstability`.
Use `interpolate_newton_with_options` to attach a policy.
Complex samples are unsupported; use `interpolate_barycentric`.

Newton condition estimates take the larger of 1 and the absolute nested-product envelope $\sum_k |c_k|\prod_{i<k}|x-x_i|$ divided by $|p(x)|$ ($c_k$ are divided differences).
Divided differences depend on node order, so reordering changes estimates even for the same polynomial.
`ApproxPolynomial` is immutable; adding nodes requires a new polynomial.
Use `to_newton(nodes)` to convert monomials to Newton form.

## Interpolation error and analysis of the Runge phenomenon

If $f$ is $n+1$ times continuously differentiable on $[a, b]$ and nodes and evaluation point $x$ lie there, interpolation error has the remainder below.

$$
f(x) - p_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!} \, \omega(x),
\qquad \omega(x)=\prod_{j=0}^n (x - x_j)
$$

$\xi$ lies inside the smallest interval containing $x$ and all nodes and depends on $x$.
The derivation repeatedly applies Rolle's theorem.
When $x$ is not a node, $g(t) = f(t) - p_n(t) - \bigl(f(x)-p_n(x)\bigr)\omega(t)/\omega(x)$ vanishes at the nodes and $x$, totaling $n+2$ points.
Applying Rolle $n+1$ times makes $g^{(n+1)}$ vanish at an interior point $\xi$ of their smallest enclosing interval.
Substitute $p_n^{(n+1)}=0$ and $\omega^{(n+1)}=(n+1)!$ to obtain the formula above.
In divided differences, the same error is $f(x)-p_n(x) = f[x_0,\dots,x_n,x]\,\omega(x)$.
Error is the product of the magnitude of a higher derivative and $|\omega(x)|$; the latter depends solely on node placement.

### Lebesgue constants and equally spaced nodes

The uniform norm of interpolation operator $\mathcal{I}_n: C[a, b] \to \mathcal{P}_n$ equals the Lebesgue constant

$$
\Lambda_n = \max_{x \in [a, b]} \lambda_n(x),\qquad \lambda_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n |\ell_j(x)|
$$


$\lambda_n$ is the Lebesgue function and equals 1 at nodes.
Relative to best polynomial approximation (polynomial $p_n^*$ minimizing uniform error) with error $\|f - p_n^*\|_\infty$, interpolation error is bounded as follows.

$$
\|f - p_n\|_\infty \le (1 + \Lambda_n) \|f - p_n^*\|_\infty
$$

$\Lambda_n$ also amplifies sample errors: perturbing each sample by at most absolute $\eta$ changes interpolated values by at most $\Lambda_n\eta$ over the interval.

The following results describe growth of $\Lambda_n$ (Trefethen, ATAP, Theorem 15.2).
Every node placement satisfies $\Lambda_n \ge \frac{2}{\pi}\ln(n+1) + 0.5212$; logarithmic growth is unavoidable.
For equally spaced nodes $x_j = -1 + 2j/n$, $\Lambda_n > 2^{n-2}/n^2$, and the Lebesgue constant grows exponentially according to the asymptotic expression below.

$$
\Lambda_n \sim \frac{2^{n+1}}{e \, n \ln n} \quad (n \to \infty)
$$

Runge function $f(x) = 1/(1 + 25x^2)$ is infinitely differentiable on the real axis but has complex poles at $z = \pm \mathrm{i}/5$.
Their proximity to the interval causes equally spaced interpolation to diverge with degree where $|x|$ exceeds approximately $0.7267$.
At nodes the interpolation conditions still hold; error appears as between-node oscillations, largest near endpoints.

### Lebesgue constants of Chebyshev nodes

Among choices of $n+1$ nodes, the zeros $x_j=\cos\bigl((2j+1)\pi/(2n+2)\bigr)$ of $T_{n+1}$ minimize the monic nodal polynomial maximum $\max_{[-1,1]}|\omega(x)|$.
These are Chebyshev first-kind points (Chebyshev root nodes).
Then $\omega = 2^{-n}T_{n+1}$, with maximum $2^{-n}$.
Endpoint-including $x_k = \cos(k\pi/n)$ ($k = 0, 1, \dots, n$) are extrema of $T_n$, called Chebyshev second-kind points (Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes).
Second-kind points do not minimize $\max|\omega|$, but their Lebesgue constants grow at the same logarithmic rate and satisfy $\Lambda_n \le \frac{2}{\pi}\ln(n+1) + 1$.
Both have the following asymptotic form.

$$
\Lambda_n^{\text{first kind}} \sim \frac{2}{\pi} \ln (n+1) + 0.9625\dots,\qquad
\Lambda_n^{\text{second kind}} \sim \frac{2}{\pi} \ln n + 0.9625\dots \quad (n \to \infty)
$$

Its constant is $\frac{2}{\pi}(\gamma+\ln\frac{8}{\pi})$, where $\gamma$ is Euler's constant.
Only a constant separates this from lower bound $\frac{2}{\pi}\ln(n+1)+0.5212$, making Chebyshev growth order optimal.

Convergence rate depends on analyticity.
If $f$ is analytic inside ellipse $E_\rho$ enclosing $[-1,1]$ (foci $\pm1$, semimajor plus semiminor axes $\rho>1$) and $|f|\le M$, second-kind Chebyshev interpolation satisfies $\|f-p_n\|_\infty \le 4M\rho^{-n}/(\rho-1)$ (ATAP, Theorem 8.2).
For Runge's function, the ellipse through the poles $\pm\mathrm{i}/5$ gives the upper limit $\rho = 1/5 + \sqrt{1 + 1/25} \approx 1.22$; error decreases roughly as $\rho^{-n}$.

Chebyshev weights have closed forms; `backend::barycentric::chebyshev_points` constructs nodes and weights together.
[Barycentric weights for node families](oxref:poly-node-family-weights) gives formulas and comparison with general weight computation.

## Comparing errors for equally spaced and Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes

The next cell interpolates Runge function $f(x) = 1/(1 + 25x^2)$ at the same degree $n$ using equally spaced and Chebyshev–Lobatto (second-kind) nodes, measuring error on a 401-point grid.
Construct $n+1$ Lobatto nodes with `Extrema` of `chebyshev_points`.
Use `ApproxPolynomial::interpolate_barycentric` for interpolation and `backend::polynomial_basis::barycentric_weights` for weights; evaluate the Lebesgue function $\lambda_n(x)$ on the grid from those same weights.
The grid maximum is a lower estimate of the true $\Lambda_n$.
For independent references, compute asymptotic formulas $2^{n+1}/(en\ln n)$ and $\frac{2}{\pi}\ln n + 0.9625$ with f64 `powi` and `ln` and compare table ratios.
Zero errors at nodes cannot be plotted logarithmically; omit them from error series and count them in the table.
The error and Lebesgue plots share the horizontal axis with linked zoom.

```rust
//| id: poly-chebyshev-runge
//| ref: poly-chebyshev-runge
//| title: "Runge errors and Lebesgue constants for equally spaced and Chebyshev nodes"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-poly]
//| inputs:
//|   degree: {"type":"range","label":"Interpolation degree n","description":"Change node count n+1.","min":4,"max":30,"step":2,"value":14}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"degree-30","label":"n = 30","description":"Equally spaced error exceeds 10³ while Chebyshev–Lobatto error falls to 10⁻³.","inputs":{"degree":30}}
//|   - {"id":"degree-4","label":"n = 4","description":"At low degree, node choice barely affects error.","inputs":{"degree":4}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":7.19201,"rtol":1e-5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":0.053466,"rtol":1e-4}}
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/1/2","approx":{"value":2.63829,"rtol":1e-5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/1/4","range":{"min":0.99,"max":1.0}}
//|   - case: degree-30
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"min":1e3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/1/1","range":{"max":3e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/0/4","range":{"min":0.8,"max":0.9}}
//|   - case: degree-4
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":0.43836,"rtol":1e-4}}
//|       - {"artifact":"runge","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":0.45995,"rtol":1e-4}}
use scioxide_poly::ApproxPolynomial;
use scioxide_poly::backend::barycentric::{ChebyshevPoints, chebyshev_points};
use scioxide_poly::backend::polynomial_basis::barycentric_weights;

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let fixed3 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 3 });
let n = degree.round() as usize;
let nf = n as f64;
let runge = |x: f64| 1.0 / (1.0 + 25.0 * x * x);
let equal: Vec<f64> = (0..=n).map(|i| -1.0 + 2.0 * i as f64 / nf).collect();
// n + 1 ascending Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes cos(kπ/n). Compute weights from the general formula below.
let (lobatto, _) =
    chebyshev_points::<f64>(n + 1, ChebyshevPoints::Extrema).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
// Independent reference: asymptotic Lebesgue constants (f64 powi and ln)
let asymptotes = [
    2f64.powi(n as i32 + 1) / (std::f64::consts::E * nf * nf.ln()),
    2.0 / std::f64::consts::PI * nf.ln() + 0.9625,
];
let grid: Vec<f64> = (0..=400).map(|i| -1.0 + 2.0 * i as f64 / 400.0).collect();
let (mut rows, mut errors, mut lebesgue_curves) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new(), Vec::new());
for ((name, nodes), asymptote) in [("Equally spaced", &equal), ("Chebyshev–Lobatto", &lobatto)]
    .into_iter()
    .zip(asymptotes)
{
    // Barycentric interpolation (SciOxide computes weights)
    let values: Vec<f64> = nodes.iter().map(|&x| runge(x)).collect();
    let p = ApproxPolynomial::interpolate_barycentric(nodes.clone(), values)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let w = barycentric_weights(nodes).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let (mut max_error, mut lebesgue, mut omitted) = (0.0_f64, 1.0_f64, 0_usize);
    let (mut error_curve, mut lebesgue_curve) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new());
    for &x in &grid {
        let error = (p.evaluate(x).map_err(|e| e.to_string())? - runge(x)).abs();
        max_error = max_error.max(error);
        // Omit zero-error points at nodes from the logarithmic plot
        if error > 0.0 {
            error_curve.push([x, error]);
        } else {
            omitted += 1;
        }
        // λ(x) = Σ|w_j/(x − x_j)| / |Σ w_j/(x − x_j)|. At nodes, λ = 1.
        let lambda = if nodes.contains(&x) {
            1.0
        } else {
            let (num, den) = nodes.iter().zip(&w).fold((0.0, 0.0), |(a, b), (&xj, &wj)| {
                (a + (wj / (x - xj)).abs(), b + wj / (x - xj))
            });
            num / den.abs()
        };
        lebesgue = lebesgue.max(lambda);
        lebesgue_curve.push([x, lambda]);
    }
    rows.push((
        name,
        max_error,
        lebesgue,
        asymptote,
        lebesgue / asymptote,
        omitted,
    ));
    errors.push(serde_json::json!({ "name": name, "points": error_curve }));
    lebesgue_curves.push(serde_json::json!({ "name": name, "points": lebesgue_curve }));
}
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Nodes" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Maximum error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Grid maximum λ_n [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Asymptotic Λ_n [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Grid maximum / asymptotic formula [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed3 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Zero-error points omitted [points]", "type": "integer" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "runge" })
);
for (series, label) in [
    (errors, "Absolute interpolation error [-]"),
    (lebesgue_curves, "Lebesgue function λ_n(x) [-]"),
] {
    emit_line_chart!(
        &series,
        options = &serde_json::json!({
            "link": { "group": "runge-x" },
            "xLabel": "Position x [-]",
            "yLabel": label,
            "yType": "log"
        })
    );
}
```

### Reading numerical results

At default $n = 14$, grid maxima of the Lebesgue function are $2.8 \times 10^{2}$ for equally spaced nodes (asymptotic $3.3 \times 10^{2}$) and $2.638$ for Chebyshev–Lobatto (asymptotic $2.643$).
At $n = 30$, these become $6.6 \times 10^{6}$ (asymptotic $7.7 \times 10^{6}$) and $3.122$ (asymptotic $3.128$), confirming exponential equally spaced growth and logarithmic Chebyshev growth.
A 100,000-point grid gives Lobatto $n=30$ value $3.127$, showing the 401-point grid slightly underestimates the maximum.
Equally spaced grid-to-asymptotic ratios are 0.87 at $n=14$ and 0.85 at $n=30$.
The asymptotic formula captures exponential growth but overestimates values by about 15% in this $n$ range; the ratios are not approaching 1.
Equally spaced $\lambda_n$ rises sharply between nodes near endpoints; Chebyshev–Lobatto has peaks of nearly equal height across the interval.

Interpolation error follows this behavior.
At $n=14$, equally spaced maximum error reaches $7.2$ near endpoints, while Chebyshev–Lobatto gives $0.053$, a ratio around 135.
Large equally spaced errors occur over $|x|\gtrsim0.73$, matching positions of large Lebesgue functions.
At $n = 30$, equally spaced error grows to $2.4 \times 10^{3}$ while Chebyshev error decreases to $2.4 \times 10^{-3}$.
From $n=14$ to $30$, Chebyshev error decreases by about a factor of 22, close to the $\rho^{16}\approx24$ predicted from $\rho \approx 1.22$ above.
At $n = 4$, maxima 0.44 (equally spaced) and 0.46 (Chebyshev) differ little; node choice matters at high degree.

## Summary

Interpolation uniqueness and approximation accuracy are distinct; assess error through remainder terms $f^{(n+1)}$ and $\omega$ and the Lebesgue constant.
Barycentric evaluation costs $O(n)$ per point after weights are prepared once in $O(n^2)$; `interpolate_barycentric` builds this interpolation for real or complex values.
Equally spaced Lebesgue constants grow exponentially with degree; for high-degree interpolation, construct Chebyshev nodes with `chebyshev_points` for only logarithmic growth.
Newton form suits node addition and divided-difference reuse, but accuracy depends on node order; choose it before passing to `interpolate_newton`.
With only equally spaced samples, consider [least squares](../least-squares-and-vandermonde/) or piecewise interpolation rather than raising degree.
