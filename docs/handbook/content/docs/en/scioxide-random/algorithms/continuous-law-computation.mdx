For the families defined in [Classical continuous-distribution families](../continuous-law-families/), this page explains how quantiles and samples are computed and how cancellation in tail probabilities is avoided.
The quantile method determines computational cost and accuracy; the sampling method determines per-sample cost and what sample inspection can check.
The final cell samples five families, compares the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance with its null distribution, and compares confidence intervals for sample means and variances with closed-form values.

## Quantile and sampling methods

There are three ways to compute quantiles.

- Closed form: solve $F(x) = p$ using elementary functions or standard normal quantiles.
  For example, `Burr12` uses $Q(p) = \bigl((1 - p)^{-1/d} - 1\bigr)^{1/c}$, `Moyal` uses $Q(p) = -\ln\bigl(2\,\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(p)^2\bigr)$, and `FatigueLife` uses $Q(p) = (w + \sqrt{w^2 + 1})^2$ with $w = c\,\Phi^{-1}(p)/2$.
- Inverse special functions: the upper side of `Argus` uses the inverse of $P(3/2, \cdot)$, while `DoubleGamma` and `Pearson3` ($\lvert\gamma\rvert \ge 0.002$) use gamma-distribution inversion.
- Numerical inversion: quantiles without closed forms use bisection or safeguarded Newton iteration with a residual equal to the smaller log-tail minus the target log-probability ($\ln F(x) - \ln p$ for $p \le 1/2$ and $\ln S(x) - \ln(1 - p)$ for $p > 1/2$).

Bisection brackets using support endpoints (expanding from $\pm 1$ for infinite endpoints) and then shrinks the interval, with the work in `LawOptions` serving as a shared limit for iterations and evaluations.
The log-probability residual tolerance is 1/8 of the relative tolerance in `LawOptions`; a log difference $\delta$ corresponds to probability relative error $e^{\delta} - 1 \approx \delta$.
`Cosine` and `GeneralizedExponential` compute every quantile with this bisection method.
Some families use numerical solving only in part of the domain.
`Alpha` solves the lower side with closed form $x = 1/\bigl(a - \Phi^{-1}(p\,\Phi(a))\bigr)$ and the upper side with Newton iteration in $\ln x$ (at most 16 iterations), initialized by a closed form (or leading term $1/x \approx q\,\Phi(a)/\varphi(a)$ when cancellation occurs).
`Argus` solves points left of the median (lower-tail probability at most 1/2) using safeguarded Newton iteration in $\ln x$, stopping at log-probability residual $8\varepsilon(1 + \lvert\ln p\rvert)$.
This stopping criterion is fixed, independent of the tolerance in `LawOptions`; only the work limit is taken from `LawOptions`.
For $0 < \lvert\gamma\rvert < 0.002$, `Pearson3` uses the same Newton method initialized by a Cornish–Fisher expansion.
`PowerNormal` switches to bisection only when $\bar\Phi(x) = p^{1/c}$ underflows.
Quantiles of `ReciprocalInverseGaussian` also require numerical inversion because inverse Gaussian quantiles use numerical inversion.

`TukeyLambda` reverses the situation: its quantile has a closed form but its CDF does not.
Its CDF is obtained by up to 128 bisections of $\ln p$ in $[\ln(\text{smallest normal number}) - 60, \ln(1/2)]$, retaining relative accuracy in tail probabilities.

Sampling methods are selected independently of quantile methods.

| Method | Families |
|---|---|
| Inverse-transform sampling $Q(U)$ with uniform $U$ on the open interval $(0, 1)$ | `Alpha`, `Anglit`, `Argus`, `Burr12`, `Cosine`, `ExponentiatedWeibull`, `GeneralizedExponential`, `GeneralizedHalfLogistic`, `GeneralizedLogistic`, `HyperbolicSecant`, `InverseWeibull`, `Mielke`, `Moyal`, `PowerNormal`, `TukeyLambda` |
| Transforming a normal variate $Z \mapsto (w + \sqrt{w^2 + 1})^2$, $w = cZ/2$ | `FatigueLife` |
| Transforming gamma variates | `DoubleGamma` (sign $\pm$ and a gamma variate), `Pearson3` ($(G - \beta^2)/\beta$ for $\lvert\gamma\rvert \ge 0.002$; inverse-CDF sampling using the `f64` CDF for smaller $\lvert\gamma\rvert$) |
| Power of an exponential variate $\pm E^{1/c}$ | `DoubleWeibull` |
| Reciprocal of an inverse Gaussian variate (Michael–Schucany–Haas method) | `ReciprocalInverseGaussian` |

Among inverse-transform families, `Cosine` and `GeneralizedExponential` perform numerical inversion by bisection for every sample, requiring more evaluations per sample than closed-form families.
`Argus` also iterates for each sample, using Newton iteration for $U \le 1/2$ and iterations for the inverse of $P(3/2, \cdot)$ for $U > 1/2$.
`Alpha` performs at most 16 Newton iterations for samples with $U > 1/2$.

## Avoiding cancellation in the tails

These families use log-tails $\ln F(x)$ and $\ln S(x)$ as fundamental quantities, avoiding subtraction $1 - (\text{smaller tail})$ for probabilities close to 1.
The smaller side is computed first; the complement evaluates $\ln(1 - e^{y})$ by switching formulas according to the size of $y$ (`log1mexp`).
The exception is the upper side of `Alpha`: for $x \le 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, $S(x)$ is computed from the difference between $\ln\Phi(a)$ and $\ln\Phi(a - 1/x)$, so relative accuracy decreases as $x$ grows.
For $a = 2$ and $x = 10^7$, the relative error was about $1.4 \times 10^{-9}$.
The specific treatment for each family is as follows.

- `Burr12` evaluates $\ln S(x) = -d\ln(1 + e^{c\ln x})$ with softplus, avoiding overflow of $x^c$.
- `InverseWeibull` uses $\ln S(x) \approx \ln t$ in the upper tail when $t = x^{-c}$ is smaller than $\varepsilon$.
- `Moyal` uses $\ln \operatorname{erfc}(t) = \ln \operatorname{erfcx}(t) - t^2$ on the lower side and $\ln S(x) \approx -x/2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2/\pi)$ far in the upper tail.
- Since $F(-\pi + t) = (t - \sin t)/(2\pi)$ in `Cosine` suffers cancellation for small $t$, it uses Taylor expansion $t^3/6 - t^5/120$ for $t < 0.01$.
- Far in the lower tail, `HyperbolicSecant` sets $\arctan z \approx z$ and returns $\ln F(x) = \ln(2/\pi) + \pi x/2$ without forming $e^{\pi x/2}$.
- For $x > 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, `Alpha` writes $\Phi(a) - \Phi(a - h) = \varphi(a)\,h\,B$ with $h = 1/x$, computing $S(x)$ by Taylor expansion through $\ln B = ah/2 + (a^2/24 - 1/6)h^2$ to avoid subtraction $\Phi(a) - \Phi(a - 1/x)$.
  The omitted term is $O(h^3(1 + a^4))$; since $h < \sqrt{\varepsilon}$, the relative error is less than $\varepsilon$ for $\lvert a\rvert \le 100$.
- For $\lvert\gamma\rvert < 0.002$, `Pearson3` avoids a centered gamma representation to avoid subtracting large constants $\beta^2$.
  It computes tails using a second-order Edgeworth expansion in the range $\lvert\gamma\rvert(1 + \lvert x \rvert)^3 \le 10^{-3}$, an endpoint Laplace expansion for $\lvert x \rvert > 128$, and quadrature of the centered density otherwise.
- The normalizing constant $P(3/2, \chi^2/2)$ of `Argus` decreases like $\chi^3$ as $\chi^2 \to 0$, so it switches to the limiting closed form for $\chi^2/2 < \varepsilon$.

## Numerical considerations for the basic families

The following numerical considerations apply to the 13 families in [Other standard-form families](oxref:random-families-other-standard).

- `Uniform` retains the endpoints directly and uses convex combinations for samples and quantiles even when the width $\text{high} - \text{low}$ overflows.
  Quantiles and samples for $p < 1$ are below $\text{high}$; only the quantile at $p = 1$ returns $\text{high}$.
- `IrwinHall` represents the density and CDF on each unit interval in a Bernstein basis with nonnegative coefficients, avoiding cancellation in the alternating sum $\sum_k (-1)^k\binom{n}{k}(x - k)^{n-1}$.
  Preparation requires $O(n^3)$ computation and a table of size $O(n^2)$; if the table exceeds `LawOptions::max_bytes`, it gives `ResourceLimit`.
  Preparation consumes about $n^3/3$ work units, so with the default 65,536 units, constructing $n \ge 57$ gives `ConvergenceFailure`; increase the limit with `with_options` for large $n$.
  Each sample uses $n$ uniforms, with these requests counted against `sample_word_budget`.
- Normal-variate requests for samples of `Rice` also count against `sample_word_budget`.
- `InverseGaussian` directly forms the upper-tail probability $S(x) = \bar\Phi(a) - e^{2/\mu}\Phi(b)$ ($a = (x/\mu - 1)/\sqrt{x}$, $b = -(x/\mu + 1)/\sqrt{x}$) from the difference between the logarithms of its two terms, including $e^{2/\mu}$ within the logarithm to avoid overflow for small $\mu$.
- `GeneralizedNormal` and `GeneralizedGamma` generate samples from logarithmic gamma variates, rounding only once at the end.
  If the result rounds to 0 or becomes nonfinite, they return `NumericalInstability` with `Underflow` or `Overflow`.
- `GaussHypergeometric` obtains the normalizing constant and moments by quadrature split at $x = 1/2$, and each tail by one quadrature from the nearer endpoint to $x$.
  Both remove endpoint singularities by a change of variable.
- `RelativisticBreitWigner` integrates tails directly with a positive integrand, using leading term $\propto x^{-3}$ in the upper tail when $x$ exceeds $10^6$ times $\sqrt{(\rho\sqrt{1 + \rho^2} + \rho^2)/2}$.
- `Bradford` rewrites the upper-tail probability as $\ln\bigl(1 + c(1 - x)/(1 + cx)\bigr)/\ln(1 + c)$ to avoid subtraction as $x \to 1$.

## Comparing CDFs and moments with samples

The sampler and CDF are implemented separately, so an error in one appears as a disagreement between them.
For $n$ samples sorted as $x_{(1)} \le \dots \le x_{(n)}$, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between empirical CDF $F_n$ and theoretical CDF $F$ is

$$
D_n = \sup_x \lvert F_n(x) - F(x) \rvert = \max_{1 \le i \le n} \max\Bigl( \tfrac{i}{n} - F(x_{(i)}),\; F(x_{(i)}) - \tfrac{i - 1}{n} \Bigr)
$$


When $F$ is continuous and samples follow $F$, the distribution of $\sqrt{n}\,D_n$ approaches the Kolmogorov distribution $K$ as $n \to \infty$; its upper 5% critical value is about 1.358.
This page reports the asymptotic upper-tail probability $P(K > \sqrt{n}\,D_n)$ as an approximate $p$-value; its difference from the exact finite-sample $p$-value decreases at order $n^{-1/2}$.
The exact finite-sample distribution is available through `KolmogorovSmirnovTwoSided` in [Test-statistic laws and the finite-sample KS distribution](../test-statistic-laws/).

Even for a correct sampler, the $p$-value is a random variable approximately uniform on $(0, 1)$, ranging from 0 to 1 as the seed changes.
The probability of $p < 0.05$ on one run is 5%, so when a small $p$-value occurs, change the seed to check whether it recurs.

For moments, estimate the standard error of sample mean $\bar{x}$ by $s/\sqrt{n}$ and of sample variance $s^2$ by $\sqrt{(m_4 - s^4)/n}$ (where $m_4$ is the sample fourth central moment), reporting intervals of $\pm 1.96$ standard errors and $z = (\text{estimate} - \text{closed form})/\text{standard error}$.
A variance interval requires the fourth moment to exist, so the `Burr12` parameters are chosen to satisfy $cd = 6 > 4$.

The following cell generates $n$ samples from the selected family with seed `seed` and reports $D_n$, $\sqrt{n}\,D_n$, the approximate $p$-value, and the upper 5% Kolmogorov critical value in the first table.
The second table gives sample means and variances, their 95% confidence intervals, and $z$ for differences from closed-form values.
Closed-form values are independent checks computed from published formulas in the cell; only the gamma function for `Burr12` uses `scioxide_special::gamma`.

With the defaults (`Burr12`, $c = 3$, $d = 2$, $n = 2\,000$, seed 42), $D_n = 0.01348$ and $\sqrt{n}\,D_n = 0.603$, with approximate $p$-value 0.860.
For the closed-form mean $\Gamma(4/3)\Gamma(5/3) = 0.80613$, the sample mean is 0.80866 ($z = 0.29$); for the closed-form variance 0.15628, the sample variance is 0.14771 ($z = -1.19$), and both closed-form values lie within the respective 95% confidence intervals.

Selecting `Cosine` or `Moyal` leaves $D_n$ unchanged at 0.01348.
This is because inverse-transform sampling gives $F(x_{(i)}) = F(Q(U_{(i)})) = U_{(i)}$, making $D_n$ exactly the KS distance of the uniforms from the same seed.
The difference in $D_n$ between `Cosine` and `Burr12` is $4.7 \times 10^{-11}$, indicating the accuracy with which numerical inversion of `Cosine` satisfies $F(Q(u)) = u$.
Thus, for inverse-transform families, the KS distance checks only that $F$ and $Q$ are inverses and misses errors shared by $F$ and $Q$.
The moment $z$ comparisons against published closed forms detect such errors.

`FatigueLife` and `Pearson3` ($\lvert\gamma\rvert \ge 0.002$) generate samples by transforming normal or gamma variates, without passing through the inverse CDF.
Here the KS distance compares two independent implementations, the sampler and CDF.
For `FatigueLife` ($c = 0.5$), $D_n = 0.00868$ ($p = 0.998$), and mean 1.12379 differs from the closed-form 1.125 by $z = -0.10$.
For `Pearson3` (skewness 1), $D_n = 0.0225$ ($p = 0.263$), with mean $z = 1.70$; changing to $n = 5\,000$ gives $D_n = 0.00619$ ($p = 0.991$), with mean $z = -0.12$.

For each quantity, the probability that a single run's $z$ lies outside $\pm 1.96$ is approximately 5%.
For `Burr12` with seed 1, the mean has $z = -1.72$ and the KS test gives $p = 0.166$, both within seed-induced fluctuations.

## Summary

Quantiles use closed forms, inverse special functions, or numerical inversion; numerical inversion uses bisection bounded by the work in `LawOptions` or Newton iteration with fixed stopping criteria.
`Cosine`, `GeneralizedExponential`, `Argus`, `GaussHypergeometric`, and `RelativisticBreitWigner` iterate on every sample, and `Alpha` iterates for samples with $U > 1/2$, making large samples more expensive than for closed-form families.
Tail probabilities compute the smaller side in logarithmic form first, retaining relative accuracy even in complements of probabilities near 1, except on the upper side of `Alpha`.
For inverse-transform families, KS distance checks only the consistency of $F$ and $Q$, so also compare with closed-form moments.
For families using transformations, KS distance and the $p$-value from its null distribution independently compare the sampler and CDF.
