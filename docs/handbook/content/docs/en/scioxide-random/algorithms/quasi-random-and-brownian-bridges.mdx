Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) approximates integrals using deterministic sequences filling the unit hypercube evenly instead of independent uniform random numbers.
Independent-point errors decrease as $O(N^{-1/2})$; small discrepancy, measuring sequence uniformity, proportionally reduces the error bound for bounded-variation integrands.
This page explains QMC sequence construction in `scioxide-random` and discrepancy decay with point count.
Sequence positions, randomization, and error estimation are covered in [QMC sequence position, randomization, and error estimation](../qmc-positions-and-randomization/); normal-coordinate assignment to Brownian paths is in [Constructing paths with a Brownian bridge](../brownian-bridge-construction/).

## Discrepancy and the Koksma–Hlawka inequality

Star discrepancy of point set $P_N = \{\mathbf{u}_0, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{N-1}\} \subset [0, 1)^d$ is the supremum of the difference between the fraction of points in origin-anchored box $[\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{a})$ and its volume.

$$
D_N^*(P_N) = \sup_{\mathbf{a} \in [0, 1]^d} \left| \frac{\#\{n : \mathbf{u}_n \in [\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{a})\}}{N} - \prod_{j=1}^d a_j \right|
$$

For $f$ with bounded Hardy–Krause variation $V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f) < \infty$ (equal to total variation $\int_0^1 |f'(x)|\,dx$ for $d = 1$), the Koksma–Hlawka inequality is

$$
\left| \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(\mathbf{u}_n) - \int_{[0,1]^d} f \right| \le V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)\, D_N^*(P_N)
$$


This is an upper bound, not an estimate of actual error.
Few functions have computable $V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)$, and the bound often greatly exceeds actual error.
Low-discrepancy sequences satisfy $D_N^* = O((\log N)^d / N)$.
However, for large $d$ and finite $N$ the logarithmic factor dominates; improvement over independent points is not assured unless the function's effective dimension is low.
Since exact maximization for $D_N^*$ is difficult in high dimensions, `discrepancy` computes mean-square $L^2$ discrepancies in closed form ([Discrepancy measures](oxref:random-sampling-discrepancy)).

## Halton sequences

Write integer $n \ge 0$ in prime base $b$ as $n = \sum_{i \ge 0} a_i b^i$ ($0 \le a_i < b$); its radical inverse reflects digits across the radix point:

$$
\phi_b(n) = \sum_{i \ge 0} a_i\, b^{-(i+1)}
$$


The van der Corput sequence for $b = 2$ is $\phi_2(1), \phi_2(2), \phi_2(3), \dots = 1/2, 1/4, 3/4, 1/8, \dots$, with each new point entering a largest gap between existing points.
A $d$-dimensional Halton sequence uses distinct primes $p_1, \dots, p_d$ as $\mathbf{x}_n = (\phi_{p_1}(n), \dots, \phi_{p_d}(n))$.

`QmcPlan::halton(d)` uses the built-in first 100 primes $2, 3, \dots, 541$ ($1 \le d \le 100$); `halton_with_bases` accepts user primes and checks nonemptiness, primality, and uniqueness.
Radical inverses compute numerator and denominator as integers and convert to floating point only for the final division.
Digit division is exact using precomputed integer reciprocals; base $b = 2$ uses bit reversal.
Batch generation of successive points updates integer numerators through digit carries, avoiding the accumulating error of floating-point recurrences.
One point costs $O(d \log n)$; with only an index as state, moving to any index takes $O(1)$.

For coordinates with large bases, $\phi_{p_j}(n) = n/p_j$ while $n < p_j$.
While both bases $p_j, p_k$ of two coordinates exceed $n$, points $(n/p_j, n/p_k)$ lie on one line through the origin.
For example, the two-dimensional projection of the final two coordinates of $d = 100$ (bases 523 and 541) has its first 523 points on a single line.

`QmcScramble::DigitPermutation(seed)` breaks these stripes by creating one seeded permutation of digits $1, \dots, b-1$ per base and applying it at every digit position.
Digit 0 is fixed, leaving infinitely many leading zeros unchanged and the radical inverse a finite sum.
`with_permutations` accepts user permutations, checking one per base, length equal to the base, bijectivity, and mapping 0 to 0.
`QmcPlan::reverse_halton(d)` uses a fixed permutation replacing nonzero digit $a$ with $b - a$ (Vandewoestyne–Cools), accepting no further randomization.
These digit permutations change point configurations but do not make points uniformly distributed.

## Sobol sequences and Gray codes

Sobol sequences are binary digital sequences.
For each coordinate $j$, choose generator matrix $C_j$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, multiply the vector of binary digits of $n$ by $C_j$, and read the result as a binary fraction.
Column $i$ of $C_j$ is the binary expansion of direction number $v_i = m_i / 2^i$.

For each coordinate, direction numbers are constructed from primitive polynomial $x^c + a_1 x^{c-1} + \dots + a_{c-1} x + 1$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and odd initial values $m_1, \dots, m_c$ ($m_i < 2^i$) through recurrence

$$
m_i = 2 a_1 m_{i-1} \oplus 2^2 a_2 m_{i-2} \oplus \dots \oplus 2^{c-1} a_{c-1} m_{i-c+1} \oplus 2^c m_{i-c} \oplus m_{i-c}
$$

where $\oplus$ is bitwise exclusive OR.
Since $m_i$ is odd, digit $2^{-i}$ of $v_i$ is 1; the generator matrix is upper triangular with diagonal 1 and is invertible.

Uniformity of digital sequences is expressed by net properties.
A set of $2^m$ points is a $(t, m, d)$-net if every elementary interval $\prod_j [a_j 2^{-k_j}, (a_j + 1) 2^{-k_j})$ of volume $2^{t-m}$ contains exactly $2^t$ points.
In a Sobol sequence, every consecutive block of $2^m$ points beginning at an index divisible by $2^m$ is a $(t, m, d)$-net for every $m$ satisfying $m \ge t$.
Smaller $t$ means uniform filling of finer boxes; $t$ depends on dimension and direction-number choices.

Each coordinate of point $n$ is the XOR of direction numbers for set bits in the Gray code $g(n) = n \oplus \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ of $n$.
$g(n)$ and $g(n+1)$ differ only at position $c$ of the least significant set bit of $n+1$, so advancing from $n$ to $n+1$ requires one XOR $\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n \oplus \mathbf{v}_c$ (Antonov–Saleev).
Gray codes map a block of $2^m$ indices starting at a multiple of $2^m$ bijectively to another block in natural order.
Thus Gray ordering preserves the block-net property, and the first $2^m$ points form the same set as in natural order.

`QmcPlan::sobol(d)` supports 21,201 dimensions through Joe–Kuo's D(6) table, using direction numbers chosen for good two-dimensional projections.
Leading coordinates have better projections, so place important variables first.
The first coordinate is the van der Corput sequence with identity generator matrix.
`sobol_with_direction_numbers(d, source)` takes `DirectionNumberSet` and uses the `SobolDirection` sequence of `Custom` from the second coordinate onward.
`SobolDirection::new(c, a, m)` checks that degree $c$ is in 1–32, the encoded polynomial is primitive, and there are $c$ initial values, all odd and satisfying $m_i < 2^i$; violations return `InvalidInput`.

Direction numbers are left-aligned 64-bit integers, point indices use `u64`, and the sequence contains $2^{64}$ points.
Output is formed from the high bits of 64-bit words, with 53 significant bits for `f64` and 24 for `f32`.
The first unrandomized Sobol point is the origin.
Generating a point from its predecessor costs $O(d)$; moving to an arbitrary index costs $O(d \cdot \mathrm{popcount}(g(n)))$.

`QmcPlan::niederreiter_base2(d)` uses Niederreiter's base-2 construction in 1–12 dimensions (Bratley–Fox–Niederreiter), generating points in Gray order like Sobol.
Coordinate $j$ uses the $j$th monic irreducible polynomial $x, x+1, x^2+x+1, x^3+x+1, \dots$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, with the construction's freely chosen Laurent-series coefficients set to 1.

## Discrepancy decay with point count

The following cell compares $L^2$ star discrepancy (`DiscrepancyMethod::L2Star`, root mean square) in $d$ dimensions for unrandomized Sobol, Sobol randomized by `DigitalShift`, Halton, and independent uniform points, from $N = 2^4$ through $2^k$.
Inputs are dimension $d$, exponent $k$ setting the point limit, and the shift and independent-point seed.
For independent uniform points, expected squared $L^2$ star discrepancy is $(2^{-d} - 3^{-d})/N$; its square root is the dashed independent reference.
Generate each method's points once to the maximum count, measuring the leading $N$ points.
The cost is $O(N^2 d)$, so this cell runs by button.

```rust
//| id: random-qmc-discrepancy-decay
//| ref: random-qmc-discrepancy-decay
//| title: "Compare Sobol, Halton, and independent-point discrepancy across point counts"
//| run: button
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-random]
//| inputs:
//|   dimension: {"type":"range","label":"Dimension d [-]","min":2,"max":8,"step":1,"value":4}
//|   maximum_exponent: {"type":"range","label":"Maximum point-count exponent k (N = 2ᵏ) [-]","min":6,"max":11,"step":1,"value":10}
//|   seed: {"type":"range","label":"Random seed (shifts and independent points) [-]","min":1,"max":1000,"step":1,"value":17}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"eight-dimensions","label":"d = 8, k = 9","description":"In higher dimensions, the Sobol versus independent-point difference is small even at N = 512.","inputs":{"dimension":8,"maximum_exponent":9}}
//|   - {"id":"two-dimensions","label":"d = 2, seed 99","description":"In two dimensions Sobol discrepancy is more than an order of magnitude below independent points.","inputs":{"dimension":2,"seed":99}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/6/0","range":{"min":1024,"max":1024}}
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/6/1","range":{"min":5e-4,"max":3e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/6/4","range":{"min":3.5e-3,"max":1.4e-2}}
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/6/5","range":{"min":6.99e-3,"max":7.0e-3}}
//|   - case: eight-dimensions
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/5/0","range":{"min":512,"max":512}}
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/5/4","range":{"min":1e-4,"max":5e-3}}
//|   - case: two-dimensions
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/6/2","range":{"min":1e-4,"max":2e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"decay","path":"/rows/6/4","range":{"min":4e-3,"max":2.5e-2}}
use scioxide_core::RealScalar;
use scioxide_random::{
    DiscrepancyMethod, QmcPlan, QmcScramble, QuasiRng, Rng, SeedableRng, SplitMix64, discrepancy,
};

let sci4 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 4 });
let d = dimension.round() as usize;
let seed = seed.round() as u64;
let n_max = 1_usize << maximum_exponent.round() as u32;
// Generate each method's points once to the maximum count and measure discrepancy on its first N points.
let sobol = QmcPlan::sobol(d).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let shifted = QmcPlan::sobol(d)
    .and_then(|plan| plan.with_scramble(QmcScramble::DigitalShift(seed)))
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let halton = QmcPlan::halton(d).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut sets = Vec::new();
for plan in [&sobol, &shifted, &halton] {
    let mut cursor = plan.sequence::<f64>().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    sets.push(cursor.points(n_max).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?);
}
let mut rng = SplitMix64::from_seed(seed);
let mut independent = vec![0.0_f64; n_max * d];
for x in &mut independent {
    *x = rng.next_float().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
}
sets.push(independent);
let mut curves: Vec<Vec<[f64; 2]>> = vec![Vec::new(); 4];
let (mut theory, mut rows) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new());
let mut n = 16;
while n <= n_max {
    let mut values = [0.0; 4];
    for ((value, curve), points) in values.iter_mut().zip(&mut curves).zip(&sets) {
        *value = discrepancy(&points[..n * d], d, DiscrepancyMethod::L2Star)
            .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        curve.push([n as f64, *value]);
    }
    // Independent check: independent uniform point root mean square √((2^-d − 3^-d)/N) (analytical).
    let moment = RealScalar::powi(0.5, d as i32) - RealScalar::powi(1.0 / 3.0, d as i32);
    let expected = RealScalar::sqrt(moment / n as f64);
    theory.push([n as f64, expected]);
    rows.push((n, values[0], values[1], values[2], values[3], expected));
    n *= 2;
}
let names = ["Sobol", "Sobol（DigitalShift）", "Halton", "Independent uniform points"];
let mut series: Vec<_> = names
    .iter()
    .zip(curves)
    .map(|(name, points)| {
        serde_json::json!({
            "name": name,
            "points": points,
            "style": { "showSymbol": true, "symbol": "circle" }
        })
    })
    .collect();
series.push(serde_json::json!({
    "name": "Independent-point theory √((2⁻ᵈ − 3⁻ᵈ)/N)",
    "points": theory,
    "style": { "lineType": "dashed" }
}));
emit_line_chart!(
    &series,
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Point count N [points]",
        "yLabel": "L² star discrepancy [-]",
        "xType": "log",
        "yType": "log"
    })
);
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Point count N [points]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Sobol [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Sobol（DigitalShift）[-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Halton [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Independent uniform points [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Independent-point theory [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci4 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "decay" })
);
```

Independent uniform points follow dashed slope $-1/2$, while Sobol and Halton curves fall faster.
With the defaults ($d = 4$, $k = 10$, seed 17), unrandomized Sobol decreases from $5.536 \times 10^{-2}$ at $N = 16$ to $1.409 \times 10^{-3}$ at $N = 1024$ with slope about $-0.88$.
Over the same range, independent uniform points decrease from $3.989 \times 10^{-2}$ to $7.094 \times 10^{-3}$; the value at $N = 1024$ is close to theoretical $6.998 \times 10^{-3}$.
At $N = 1024$, randomized Sobol ($1.323 \times 10^{-3}$) is about 1/5.4 of independent points, and Halton ($1.926 \times 10^{-3}$) about 1/3.7.
Independent-point values are one realization and fluctuate around theory.

For $d = 8$ and $k = 9$, Sobol at $N = 512$ is $2.262 \times 10^{-3}$ and independent points $2.609 \times 10^{-3}$, a small difference.
At $N = 16$, unrandomized Sobol gives $6.149 \times 10^{-2}$, larger than independent points ($1.472 \times 10^{-2}$); the bias of short origin-starting prefixes becomes apparent in high dimensions.
For the same $N = 16$, Sobol randomized by `DigitalShift` gives $1.277 \times 10^{-2}$, with the shift removing origin bias.
For $d = 2$ and seed 99, Sobol at $N = 1024$ is $8.679 \times 10^{-4}$ versus independent points $1.018 \times 10^{-2}$, differing by more than an order of magnitude.

## Summary

The Koksma–Hlawka inequality bounds QMC error through discrepancy but does not estimate actual error, so error assessment requires randomization and replicates.
Halton uses prime-base radical inverses; `halton(d)` supports 100 dimensions, but coordinates with large bases align their first points, so break stripes with digit permutations or reverse Halton.
Sobol supports 21,201 dimensions with Joe–Kuo direction numbers and forms nets in blocks of $2^m$ points, making powers of 2 suitable point counts.
In low dimensions, discrepancy falls faster with point count than for independent points; in high dimensions with few points the difference is small, and unrandomized Sobol prefixes can be more biased than independent points.
