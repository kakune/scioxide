Results of calculations using random numbers fluctuate when the random seed changes.
A scatter plot that looks uniform or a mean matching intuition therefore does not establish that a sampler is correct.
The examples for `scioxide-random` compare statistics from generated samples and sequences with independently known closed forms, analytical solutions, exhaustive calculations, or other algorithms, interpreting differences in units of standard error or confidence intervals.

## Reading results

Every example exposes the random seed as an input control.
Interpret the difference between a statistic and its theoretical value, divided by the standard error shown in the table, as $z$.
Under a normal approximation, $|z| > 2$ has probability about 4.6%, so a table with many rows can naturally have several values with magnitude above 2.
A 95% interval misses the true value about 5% of the time as the seed changes; judge trends across seeds rather than one seed's result.
Errors estimated from QMC replicates are statistical estimates, not certified bounds.
Discrepancies and point counts returned by Latin hypercube or Poisson disk reports measure design quality rather than bounding integration error.
Numerical values in the text use the default inputs at the commit described by this handbook and can change when sampling algorithms change ([Scope of reproducibility](oxref:random-algorithms-reproducibility)).

## Summary

Before using results of sampler-based calculations, compare them with closed forms or another algorithm and interpret differences through standard errors or confidence intervals.
The first 10 examples establish how to interpret samples from laws, weighted sampling, quasi-random sequences, Brownian bridges, and space-filling designs.
The remaining 16 apply that same interpretation to concrete models from Buffon's needle to Wishart matrices.
Before assigning random seeds in parallel computations, use the seed-collision example to examine random-seed collision probabilities and the guarantees of stream splitting.
Before passing parameters for extreme-value or directional data, use the GEV and von Mises examples to check their parameter conventions: GEV shape sign and azimuth orientation.
