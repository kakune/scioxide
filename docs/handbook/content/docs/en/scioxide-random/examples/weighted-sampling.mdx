Demand simulations, particle-filter resampling, and stochastic action selection choose categories with probabilities proportional to nonnegative weights $w_0, \dots, w_{K-1}$.
This example repeatedly draws indices from the same weights with `WeightedSampler`, using multinomial and binomial theory to examine how observed proportions approach probabilities and how uncertain rare-category proportions are.
Alias-table construction is also explained in [Weighted indices and the alias method](oxref:random-discrete-alias-method).

## From weights to probabilities

The probability of category $i$ is

$$
p_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j} w_j}
$$

Multiplying all weights by the same positive constant leaves it unchanged.
`WeightedSampler::new(&weights)` divides weights by the largest positive weight, normalizes them using compensated summation (Neumaier's method), and constructs a Walker–Vose alias table.
Division by the maximum avoids sum overflow even for weights such as `f32::MAX`.
Weights need not sum to 1.

Weights must be finite and nonnegative.
An empty array, negative weights, or no positive weights gives `InvalidInput`; NaN or infinite weights give `NonFinite`.
Zero-weight categories are never drawn.
Table construction uses $O(K)$ time and storage; each draw takes expected $O(1)$ time with no allocation.

## Why the alias method reproduces category probabilities

An alias table stores one retention probability $F_i \in [0, 1]$ and one alias $A_i$ in each of $K$ columns.
A draw selects column $i$ uniformly with probability $1/K$ and returns $i$ if uniform value $u$ is below $F_i$, or $A_i$ otherwise.
The probability of returning category $i$ is therefore

$$
\Pr(\text{returned value} = i) = \frac{1}{K}\Bigl(F_i + \sum_{j:\,A_j = i} (1 - F_j)\Bigr)
$$


Vose's construction fills scaled masses $q_i = K p_i$ (with mean exactly 1) by pairing columns with $q_i < 1$ and $q_i \ge 1$.
For an underfilled column $j$, set $F_j = q_j$, assign its remaining $1 - q_j$ to surplus column $i$ as an alias, and subtract $1 - q_j$ from $q_i$.
If the remaining $q_i$ falls below 1, column $i$ moves to the underfilled group.
Each pair finalizes one column, so construction finishes in $O(K)$.
Each height-one column box contains mass from only two categories, making the sum above equal to $q_i/K = p_i$.
The remaining columns have mass exactly 1 apart from rounding error, so set $F_i = 1$.
For zero-weight columns, discard residual rounding mass, set retention probability to 0, and point the alias to a positive-weight category.

There are four draw forms: `sample_index(rng)` returning an index, `sample(rng, &values)` returning a borrow from an array of values, `sample_n(rng, n)` returning $n$ indices in an owned `Vec`, and `sample_n_into(rng, policy, output, workspace)` writing to caller-provided output.
Each draw uses one unbiased integer draw `uniform_index` to select a column ([Lemire rejection](oxref:random-sampling-lemire) removes modulo bias) and one `next_float` to compare against the retention probability.

## Multinomial fluctuations

Counts $(C_0, \dots, C_{K-1})$ from $N$ independent draws follow the multinomial distribution $\mathrm{Multinomial}(N, \mathbf{p})$, and each $C_i$ follows the binomial distribution $\mathrm{Binomial}(N, p_i)$.
$C_i$ sums $N$ independent 0/1 trials, each with variance $p_i(1 - p_i)$, so the standard error of observed proportion $\hat{p}_i = C_i/N$ is

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\hat{p}_i) = \sqrt{\frac{p_i(1-p_i)}{N}}
$$

Halving it requires quadrupling $N$.

For a low-probability category $r$, even a seemingly small absolute error $|\hat{p}_r - p_r|$ can accompany a large relative uncertainty,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{SE}(\hat{p}_r)}{p_r} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - p_r}{N p_r}} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{N p_r}}
$$


Even with expected count $N p_r = 1$, the probability of a zero count is $(1 - p_r)^N \approx e^{-1} \approx 0.368$.
When expected counts are small, the normal-approximation $z$ does not apply; interpret count $c$ directly using binomial probabilities $\Pr(C_r \le c)$ and $\Pr(C_r \ge c)$ for both tails.

Examine overall goodness of fit with Pearson's statistic

$$
X^2 = \sum_{i=0}^{K-1} \frac{(C_i - N p_i)^2}{N p_i}
$$


Each term has expectation $\mathrm{Var}(C_i)/(N p_i) = 1 - p_i$, so $\mathrm{E}[X^2] = \sum_i (1 - p_i) = K - 1$ holds independently of $N$.
If all $N p_i$ are at least about 5, $X^2$ is approximately chi-squared with $K - 1$ degrees of freedom.
The count sum is fixed at $N$, reducing the number of independent terms to $K - 1$.

## Variety weights and draw counts

Set order weights of five product varieties to $[40, 25, 20, 10, w_r]$ and vary rare-variety weight $w_r$, draw count $N$, and the random seed.
The following cell plots each variety's standardized difference $z_i = (\hat{p}_i - p_i)/\mathrm{SE}(\hat{p}_i)$ as a bar and compares it with lines at $\pm 2$.
The second plot compares maximum absolute error $\max_i |\hat{p}_i - p_i|$ over the first $n$ draws with the standard error $\max_i \sqrt{p_i(1-p_i)/n}$ of the most variable variety (dashed), on logarithmic axes.
Compute probabilities $p_i$ by direct weight normalization in the cell without using the alias table.

The first table gives each variety's probability, count, observed proportion, standard error, and $z_i$.
The second compares $X^2$ with the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles and upper-tail probability of a chi-squared distribution with 4 degrees of freedom, computed with `ChiSquared::quantile` and `Survival::sf`.
The third compares rare-variety count $c$ with $\Pr(C_r \le c)$ and $\Pr(C_r \ge c)$ computed from `cdf(c)` and `sf(c - 1)` of `Binomial::new(N, p_r)`.

Each variety's $z_i$ bar generally lies within the $\pm 2$ lines.
Each individual bar has about a 4.6% chance of falling outside $\pm 2$, so one of five slightly exceeding the lines is not unusual.
Incorrect alias-table construction would make the same variety's bar repeatedly deviate in the same direction across seeds.
The maximum-absolute-error line fluctuates downward along the dashed $n^{-1/2}$ slope.
If it levels off orders of magnitude above the dashed line, the probabilities themselves are incorrect.

With the default inputs ($w_r = 5$, $N = 2048$, seed 29), the rare-variety probability is $0.05$, count is 82 against expectation $102.4$, $z = -2.07$, and relative uncertainty is $0.096$.
The binomial lower-tail probability is $\Pr(C_r \le 82) = 0.019$, agreeing with the normal-approximation $\Phi(-2.07) \approx 0.019$.
This single variety is slightly more than 2 standard errors away, but $X^2 = 6.59$ across all five varieties lies between the 4-degree-of-freedom 2.5% quantile $0.48$ and 97.5% quantile $11.14$, with upper-tail probability $0.159$.
With seed 1, the rare-variety count is 118, with $z = 1.58$ and $X^2 = 3.54$.

At $w_r = 0.5$, $p_r \approx 0.0052$; even with $N = 2048$, expected count is $10.7$ and relative uncertainty is $0.305$.
Reducing further to $N = 256$ gives expected count about $1.3$, violating the $X^2$ chi-squared approximation requirement of expected counts at least about 5.
The resulting count of 1 has $\Pr(C_r \le 1) = 0.612$ and $\Pr(C_r \ge 1) = 0.739$ and lies in neither binomial tail.

## Reading results

Interpret differences in observed proportions as $z_i$ using that variety's standard error $\sqrt{p_i(1-p_i)/N}$ as the unit.
Rare varieties have large relative uncertainty even for small absolute differences, so do not infer their probabilities from proportions until expected count $N p_r$ is sufficiently large.
For small expected counts, interpret counts using binomial probabilities for both tails.
Even a correct sampler gives $X^2$ outside the 95% interval for about 5% of seeds; interpret its distribution across seeds rather than one seed's value.
For many draws from the same weights, construct the table once and repeat `sample_index` or `sample_n_into`.

## Summary

`WeightedSampler` constructs an alias table in $O(K)$ once by normalizing weights divided by their maximum, then uses one unbiased integer draw and one floating-point value per draw.
Each alias-table column holds mass from only two categories; uniformly choosing columns gives category probabilities exactly $p_i$.
Observed proportions fluctuate around probabilities with width $\sqrt{p_i(1-p_i)/N}$, while maximum absolute error decreases on the order of $N^{-1/2}$.
Rare-variety proportions fluctuate widely in relative terms while expected count $N p_r$ is small, so interpret counts through binomial tail probabilities.
Assess overall fit by comparing Pearson's $X^2$ (expectation $K - 1$) with chi-squared quantiles.
