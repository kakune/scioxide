The amplitude and phase of an oscillatory signal are not uniquely determined by a single sequence of real samples.
There are infinitely many pairs $(A, \phi)$ satisfying $x(t) = A(t)\cos\phi(t)$.
The analytic signal constructs a complex signal $z = x + i\,\mathcal{H}[x]$ with negative frequencies removed and defines its magnitude and argument as amplitude and phase.
This page derives the weights for a discrete analytic signal and gives conditions for interpreting its envelope and instantaneous frequency.
It then covers two multidimensional extensions (single-orthant analytic signals and Riesz transforms) and vector strength, which measures synchronization of event times to a period.

The continuous-time Hilbert transform and the construction of an analytic signal from `rfft` and `ifft` are derived in [Analytic signals and the discrete Hilbert transform](../../../scioxide-fft/algorithms/analytic-signal/) in the fft chapter.
Building on that result, this page explains the assumptions and reliability of the functions in `scioxide-signal`.

## Discrete analytic signals

Treat a real sequence $x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}$ of length $N$ as one period, with DFT $X_k = \sum_n x_n e^{-2\pi i kn/N}$.
For a real sequence, $X_{N-k} = \overline{X_k}$, so indices $k$ and $N-k$ carry the same information.
Interpret indices as signed frequencies in $(-N/2, N/2]$, with $0 < k < N/2$ positive and $N/2 < k < N$ negative.

The continuous Hilbert transform has frequency response $-i\,\mathrm{sgn}(f)$.
In the discrete case, the DC bin $k = 0$ and the Nyquist bin $k = N/2$ for even lengths are self-paired.
At these bins, $X_k$ is real, so multiplying by $-i$ makes it imaginary and the inverse transform ceases to be real.
To retain a Hilbert transform mapping real signals to real signals, the response at these two bins must be zero.
The discrete Hilbert transform response is therefore

$$
H_k = \begin{cases} -i & 0 < k < N/2 \\ 0 & k = 0,\ \text{or } N \text{ is even and } k = N/2 \\ +i & N/2 < k < N \end{cases}
$$

and the DFT of the analytic signal $z = x + i\,\mathcal{H}[x]$ is $Z_k = (1 + iH_k) X_k = w_k X_k$.
The weights are

$$
w_k = \begin{cases} 1 & k = 0,\ \text{or } N \text{ is even and } k = N/2 \\ 2 & 0 < k < N/2 \\ 0 & N/2 < k < N \end{cases}
$$


Odd lengths have no Nyquist bin, so all $1 \le k \le (N-1)/2$ are doubled.
Because $\mathcal{H}[x]$ is real, $\operatorname{Re} z_n = x_n$ holds.

`hilbert(input)` returns this $z$, and `envelope(input)` returns $\lvert z_n \rvert$.
Internally, a real FFT computes the nonnegative-frequency half of $X_k$, positive frequencies are multiplied by $-i$, DC and Nyquist are set to zero, and a real inverse FFT gives $\mathcal{H}[x]$.
The real part copies the input directly, so $\operatorname{Re} z_n$ equals $x_n$ bit for bit.
The cost is $O(N \log N)$.
When the maximum input magnitude is large or small, the input is rescaled by a power of 2 before transformation, preventing finite results from overflowing in intermediate steps or underflowing prematurely.
Error has the same form as FFT error, proportional to $\varepsilon$, the input norm, and $\log N$ ([Working-precision error](../)).
Components orders of magnitude smaller than the largest component lose relative accuracy.

`complex_envelope_into(input, output, cx)` only writes complex sample magnitudes to the beginning of the output; it does not perform a Hilbert transform.
Use it to obtain an envelope from a `hilbert` result or a demodulated baseband signal while reusing output storage.
Output shorter than input gives `DimensionMismatch`; non-finite samples give `NonFinite`.

### Periodicity assumption

The DFT treats a record as one period, so a record whose endpoint values or slopes do not match has a jump in its periodic extension.
The jump's spectrum spans all bands; the weights doubling positive frequencies and removing negative frequencies produce time-domain oscillations near the endpoints.
These disturb the envelope and phase near both ends.
`hilbert` does not accept a zero-padding length.
Apply a window that smoothly reduces both ends to zero, or provide a record with margins before and after the region of interest and discard the results in those margins.

## Instantaneous phase and frequency

Write the analytic signal in polar form $z_n = A_n e^{i\phi_n}$ and call $A_n = \lvert z_n \rvert$ the instantaneous amplitude (envelope) and $\phi_n = \arg z_n$ the instantaneous phase.
Instantaneous frequency is the time derivative of phase, $f(t) = \phi'(t)/(2\pi)$.
For discrete samples, use the phase difference between adjacent samples:

$$
\hat f_{n+1/2} = \frac{f_s}{2\pi}\arg\left(z_{n+1}\overline{z_n}\right)
$$

Here, $f_s$ is the sample rate.
Taking the argument of the product avoids phase unwrapping (removing $2\pi$ jumps) and is correct within $\lvert f \rvert < f_s/2$.
For phase quadratic in time (a linear chirp), this difference equals the instantaneous frequency at midpoint $t_{n+1/2}$ up to rounding error.
When $A_n$ is small, rounding error and noise dominate the argument, so restrict the interpretation of instantaneous frequency by envelope magnitude.

## Conditions for interpreting the envelope

$\lvert z \rvert$ does not always represent amplitude.
Bedrosian's theorem states that the Hilbert transform passes through a low-frequency factor only when the two signals have separated spectra.

> If the low-frequency signal $a(t)$ has spectrum contained in $\lvert f \rvert < B$ and the high-frequency signal $c(t)$ has spectrum contained in $\lvert f \rvert > B$, then $\mathcal{H}[a\,c] = a\,\mathcal{H}[c]$.

If $a \ge 0$ and $c = \cos\phi$ satisfies this condition, then $z = a\,(\cos\phi + i\sin\phi)$, giving $\lvert z \rvert = a$ and $\arg z = \phi$.
Interpreting the envelope and instantaneous frequency assumes that the bandwidth of the envelope $a$ is narrow relative to the carrier frequency.
An example violating the condition (amplitude modulation with carrier frequency below modulation frequency) is covered in [Recovering an envelope with the Hilbert transform](../../examples/hilbert-envelope/).

The following cell creates a linear chirp with Gaussian envelope $a(t) = \exp(-(t - t_0)^2/(2w^2))$:

$$
x(t) = a(t)\cos\left(2\pi\left(f_0 (t - t_0) + \tfrac{c}{2}(t - t_0)^2\right)\right)
$$

It uses $N = 1000$ samples (1 s) at $f_s = 1000$ Hz and applies `hilbert`, `envelope`, and `complex_envelope_into`.
Its center is $t_0 = 0.5$ s and center frequency $f_0 = 100$ Hz, with theoretical instantaneous frequency $f(t) = f_0 + c\,(t - t_0)$.
Inputs are the frequency change rate $c$ [Hz/s] and envelope width $w$ [s].
Errors are measured over $a(t) \ge 0.1$, and the table also shows endpoint amplitude $a(0)$.
Signal generation and theoretical values are reference computations using inherent f64 methods, independent of the SciOxide computation.

In every case, error is governed by endpoint handling and the ratio of envelope bandwidth to instantaneous frequency.

## Single-orthant analytic signals

For multidimensional signals, negative frequency is not uniquely defined.
Two-dimensional frequencies $(k_1, k_2)$ have four quadrants; a real signal's spectrum carries the same information in two quadrants symmetric about the origin.
Hahn's single-orthant analytic signal multiplies the DFT by the product of one-dimensional weights for the selected axes:

$$
W_{k_1, \dots, k_d} = \prod_{j} w^{(j)}_{k_j}
$$

It then takes the inverse transform ($w^{(j)}$ is the preceding section's weight for the length of axis $j$).
The resulting spectrum remains only in the orthant where frequencies on all selected axes are nonnegative.

Selecting just one axis applies the preceding one-dimensional analytic signal to each row (or column), with real part equal to the input.
With two or more selected axes, the real part generally differs from the input.
For a two-dimensional plane wave $\cos(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})$, only one of the components $e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}$ and $e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}$ receives the weight $w_{k_1} w_{k_2}$.
When $k_1, k_2 > 0$, the weight is $2 \cdot 2 = 4$, giving $z = 2e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}$ and $\lvert z \rvert = 2$.
If both components lie in discarded quadrants, as when $k_1 > 0 > k_2$, then $z = 0$.
The single-orthant analytic signal depends on orientation and is unsuitable for local amplitudes of patterns with varying orientations.
For orientation-independent amplitude, use the monogenic signal in the next section.

`AnalyticSignalNdPlan::new(shape, axes, options)` prepares FFT plans for array shape `shape` and selected axes `axes`.
Unselected axes are treated as independent batches.
`analytic(input)` allocates and returns a row-major complex array; `analytic_into(input, output, ws, cx)` and `envelope_into(input, output, ws, cx)` use workspace allocated by `workspace(())` to write to caller-provided outputs, which may be strided views.
Plan preparation allocates, but execution with sufficient workspace does not.
`build(shape, axes, options, cx)` prepares FFT plans subject to cancellation and the deadline of execution context `cx`.
Empty or duplicate axes, or a selected axis of length zero, give `InvalidInput`; an axis index at least the array rank gives `OutOfBounds`.
`hilbert` and `envelope` are one-shot forms that create a one-dimensional plan and execute it once; prepare a reusable plan for repeated records of equal length.
The cost is $O(M \log M)$ for $M$ elements.

## Riesz transforms and monogenic signals

The Riesz transform extends the Hilbert transform to multiple dimensions in an orientation-independent form.
For $d$ selected axes, there are $d$ components, with component $j$ having frequency response

$$
\widehat{R_j f}(\boldsymbol{\omega}) = -i\,\frac{\omega_j}{\lvert\boldsymbol{\omega}\rvert}\,\hat f(\boldsymbol{\omega}),
\qquad \omega_j = \frac{k_j}{n_j h_j},
\qquad \lvert\boldsymbol{\omega}\rvert^2 = \sum_i \omega_i^2
$$


Here, $k_j$ is the signed frequency index on axis $j$ ($(-n_j/2, n_j/2]$), $n_j$ is the axis length, and $h_j$ is the sample spacing.
$\omega_j$ has units reciprocal to sample spacing; unequal spacings change orientation even for the same indices.
Pass physical spacings as `spacing` to handle isotropic patterns with the correct orientation.
In one dimension, $\omega_j / \lvert\omega\rvert = \mathrm{sgn}(k)$, so the Riesz transform equals the periodic Hilbert transform above.

The response of component $j$ is set to zero at bins with $k_j = 0$ and, for even $n_j$, $k_j = n_j/2$.
As with the one-dimensional Nyquist bin, multiplying self-paired bins by a purely imaginary value would prevent real input from producing real components.
DC with $k = 0$ on all axes contributes to no component.

The monogenic signal is the tuple $(f, R_1 f, \dots, R_d f)$ containing input $f$ and its Riesz components.
In two dimensions, it gives local amplitude $A = \sqrt{f^2 + (R_1 f)^2 + (R_2 f)^2}$, local phase $\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(\lvert Rf \rvert, f)$, and local orientation $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(R_2 f, R_1 f)$.
For a plane wave $f = \cos(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})$, $R_j f = (k_j/\lvert\boldsymbol{k}\rvert)\sin(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})$, so local amplitude is 1 everywhere and local orientation follows the wave vector.
The sign of $\sin(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})$ flips $\theta$ by $180°$, so use the following doubled-angle mean to summarize orientation over the field:

$$
\bar\theta = \tfrac12\operatorname{atan2}\left(\textstyle\sum 2 R_1 f\,R_2 f,\ \sum \left((R_1 f)^2 - (R_2 f)^2\right)\right)
$$

This gives orientation modulo $180°$.

`RieszPlan::new(shape, axes, spacing, options)` computes and retains component multipliers once during preparation ($dM$ real values for $d$ axes and $M$ elements).
The Riesz output shape is `output_shape()` (a leading component axis of length $d$, then the input shape); the monogenic shape is `monogenic_shape()` (leading length $d + 1$); both are row-major.
Components follow the order in `axes`; the monogenic signal places the input first.
`riesz(input)` and `riesz_into` accept real and complex inputs; real input produces real components.
`monogenic(input)` and `monogenic_into` accept only real input.
One execution performs one forward FFT and one inverse FFT per component, costing $O((d + 1) M \log M)$.
The one-shot calls `riesz_nd(input, axes, spacing)` and `monogenic_nd(input, axes, spacing)` create a plan and execute it once.
A `spacing` length differing from `axes` gives `DimensionMismatch`, nonpositive spacings give `InvalidInput`, and non-finite spacings give `NonFinite`.

The following cell uses `RieszPlan` to compute the monogenic signal of a plane wave $f = \cos(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})$ on a periodic $32 \times 32$ grid and compares local amplitude, Riesz components, and local orientation with analytic values.
Inputs are the number of periods $m_x$ and $m_y$ per grid side and the sample spacing $h_y$ [m] in direction $y$; spacing in direction $x$ is fixed at 1 m.
The physical wave vector is $(k_x, k_y) = (m_x/(n_x h_x), m_y/(n_y h_y))$.
Also apply a two-axis `AnalyticSignalNdPlan` to the same plane wave and compare $\lvert z \rvert$ with the preceding prediction (one of $2$, $1$, or $0$).
Also check that the one-shot `monogenic_nd` result matches the plan result.

```rust
//| ref: signal-monogenic-plane-wave
//| title: "Monogenic and single-orthant analytic signals of a plane wave"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-signal]
//| inputs:
//|   wave_x: {"type":"range","label":"Periods in x direction m_x [-]","description":"Number of periods per grid side.","min":0,"max":6,"step":1,"value":3}
//|   wave_y: {"type":"range","label":"Periods in y direction m_y [-]","description":"Negative values point the wave vector into the fourth quadrant.","min":-6,"max":6,"step":1,"value":2}

//|   - {"id":"fourth-quadrant","label":"m_y = −2 (fourth quadrant)","description":"The monogenic signal still returns amplitude 1, but the single-orthant analytic signal becomes zero.","inputs":{"wave_y":-2}}

//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"min":-1e-13,"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/1/3","range":{"min":-1e-13,"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/2/1","approx":{"value":33.69,"atol":0.01}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/2/3","range":{"min":-1e-10,"max":1e-10}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/3/1","approx":{"value":2.0,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/5/3","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/6/3","equals":0}
//|   - case: fourth-quadrant
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"min":-1e-13,"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/2/1","approx":{"value":146.31,"atol":0.01}}
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/4/1","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|   - case: fine-y-spacing
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"monogenic","path":"/rows/2/1","approx":{"value":53.13,"atol":0.01}}
use scioxide_core::{NdView, StridedLayout};
use scioxide_signal::{AnalyticSignalNdPlan, RieszPlan, SignalExecutionOptions, monogenic_nd};

let (ny, nx) = (32_usize, 32_usize);
let (mx, my, hy) = (wave_x.round(), wave_y.round(), spacing_y);
if mx == 0.0 && my == 0.0 {
    return Err("A constant field has zero Riesz components. Set at least one period count to a nonzero value".to_owned());
}
// Plane wave in row-major [y][x] order. Physical wave vector (m_x/(n_x h_x), m_y/(n_y h_y)).
let phase = |p: usize| {
    let (i, j) = ((p / nx) as f64, (p % nx) as f64);
    std::f64::consts::TAU * (mx * j / nx as f64 + my * i / ny as f64)
};

let data: Vec<f64> = (0..size).map(|p| phase(p).cos()).collect();
let (shape, strides) = ([ny, nx], [nx, 1]);

    &data,
    StridedLayout::new(&shape, &strides).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?,
)
.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

// Axis 0 is y and axis 1 is x. The monogenic signal order is [f, R_y f, R_x f].
let options = SignalExecutionOptions::new();
let plan = RieszPlan::new(&shape, &[0, 1], &[hy, 1.0], options).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let out = plan.monogenic(view).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let one_shot = monogenic_nd(view, &[0, 1], &[hy, 1.0]).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let orthant = AnalyticSignalNdPlan::new(&shape, &[0, 1], options)
    .and_then(|plan| plan.analytic(view))
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

let (ky, kx) = (my / (ny as f64 * hy), mx / nx as f64);
let norm = ky.hypot(kx);
let (mut a_min, mut a_max, mut component_gap) = (f64::INFINITY, 0.0_f64, 0.0_f64);
let (mut z_min, mut z_max, mut plan_gap) = (f64::INFINITY, 0.0_f64, 0.0_f64);
let (mut cross, mut difference) = (0.0, 0.0);
for p in 0..size {
    let (f, ry, rx) = (out[p], out[size + p], out[2 * size + p]);
    let amplitude = (f * f + ry * ry + rx * rx).sqrt();
    (a_min, a_max) = (a_min.min(amplitude), a_max.max(amplitude));
    let s = phase(p).sin();
    component_gap = component_gap.max((ry - ky / norm * s).abs().max((rx - kx / norm * s).abs()));
    // Sum doubled angles to cancel the 180° reversal due to the sign of sin.
    cross += 2.0 * rx * ry;
    difference += rx * rx - ry * ry;
    (z_min, z_max) = (z_min.min(orthant[p].norm()), z_max.max(orthant[p].norm()));
}
for (a, b) in out.iter().zip(&one_shot) {
    plan_gap = plan_gap.max((a - b).abs());
}
let degrees = |angle: f64| angle.to_degrees().rem_euclid(180.0);
let orientation = degrees(0.5 * cross.atan2(difference));
let expected_orientation = degrees(ky.atan2(kx));
// Single-orthant weight: product of one-dimensional weights w(m). Half the coefficient of the retained component gives |z|.
let w = |m: f64| {
    if m > 0.0 {
        2.0_f64
    } else if m == 0.0 {
        1.0
    } else {
        0.0
    }
};
let expected_z = (w(mx) * w(my)).max(w(-mx) * w(-my)) / 2.0;

let fixed6 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 6 });
let sci2 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Quantity" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Computed value", "type": "number", "format": fixed6, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Analytic value", "type": "number", "format": fixed6, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Difference", "type": "number", "format": sci2 }
]);
let row =
    |name: &str, value: f64, exact: f64| (name.to_owned(), Some(value), Some(exact), value - exact);
let rows = vec![
    row("Minimum local amplitude A [-]", a_min, 1.0),
    row("Maximum local amplitude A [-]", a_max, 1.0),
    row("Local orientation θ̄ [degrees]", orientation, expected_orientation),
    row("Minimum single-orthant |z| [-]", z_min, expected_z),
    row("Maximum single-orthant |z| [-]", z_max, expected_z),
    (
        "Maximum difference between R_x f, R_y f and analytic values [-]".to_owned(),
        None,
        None,
        component_gap,
    ),
    (
        "Maximum difference between monogenic_nd and plan [-]".to_owned(),
        None,
        None,
        plan_gap,
    ),
];
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "monogenic" })
);
```

With defaults $(m_x, m_y) = (3, 2)$ and $h_y = 1$ m, local amplitude ranges from $1 - 1.3\times10^{-15}$ to $1 + 8.9\times10^{-16}$, and the maximum difference between Riesz components and analytic values is $1.8\times10^{-15}$.
Local orientation is $33.69°$, agreeing with wave-vector orientation $\operatorname{atan2}(k_y, k_x)$ within $5\times10^{-14}$ degrees.
The single-orthant analytic signal returns $\lvert z \rvert = 2$ within $2.7\times10^{-15}$, and its real part is twice the input.
The difference between the plan and `monogenic_nd` results is zero.

With $m_y = -2$, the wave vector points into the fourth quadrant and local orientation is $146.31°$.
The monogenic local amplitude remains 1, but the single-orthant $\lvert z \rvert$ is at most $2.4\times10^{-15}$, eliminating the plane wave.
For $m_x = 0$ (a wave varying only in direction $y$), the $x$-axis weight is the DC weight 1, giving $\lvert z \rvert = 1$.
At $h_y = 0.5$ m, physical wave number $k_y$ doubles and local orientation changes from $33.69°$ to $53.13°$.
Even for the same grid indices, omitting sample spacings therefore gives the wrong orientation.
A plane wave with a noninteger period count does not match the grid period, making its periodic extension discontinuous, so this agreement no longer holds.

## Vector strength

One may wish to measure how strongly event times, such as neural spikes, heartbeats, or earthquakes, synchronize with a stimulus of period $T$.
Convert event times $t_1, \dots, t_N$ to phases $\theta_k = 2\pi (t_k \bmod T)/T$ using remainders modulo the period; the mean of unit-circle points $e^{i\theta_k}$ is the mean resultant vector:

$$
R = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} e^{i\theta_k}
$$


Vector strength is $\lvert R \rvert \in [0, 1]$: it is 1 when all events have the same phase and near 0 when phases are uniformly spread over the cycle.
The mean phase is $\arg R \in [-\pi, \pi]$, with phase 0 at times $t \equiv 0 \pmod T$.

Pass event times and periods in the same time units; strength is dimensionless and phase is in radians.
When scanning frequencies instead of periods, form $T = 1/f$ in the same units as the times ($T = 1000/f$ ms for times in ms).
Remainders are taken in period units before conversion to angles, so rounding error in large timestamps remains as phase error $2\pi\,\delta t/T$.
For example, the double-precision spacing $\delta t \approx 1.2\times10^{-7}$ s near $t \approx 10^9$ s corresponds to $3.7\times10^{-4}$ rad for phase at $T = 2$ ms.
Subtract a reference, such as the record start time, before passing the times.

For Gaussian phase scatter (time jitter with standard deviation $\sigma$), the wrapped normal distribution gives $\mathrm{E}\,\lvert R\rvert \approx \rho = \exp(-(2\pi\sigma/T)^2/2)$.
For large $N$, the standard error of $\lvert R \rvert$ can be estimated from the component variance along the mean direction as $\mathrm{SE} \approx (1 - \rho^2)/\sqrt{2N}$.
To test the null hypothesis of uniformly scattered phases, use the Rayleigh statistic $Z = N\lvert R \rvert^2$; for large $N$, the approximate $p$ value is $e^{-Z}$.
For small $N$, use a formula with correction terms.

`vector_strength(events, period)` returns $R$ for one period as a `VectorStrength`; `strength()` gives $\lvert R \rvert$, and `phase()` gives $\arg R$.
Phase is undefined when $R$ is exactly zero, so `phase()` returns `None`.
`vector_strength_into(events, periods, output, cx)` computes multiple periods together and writes the result for `periods[j]` to `output[j]`.
The cost is $O(NP)$ for $N$ events and $P$ periods, using compensated summation.
Events need not be sorted.
Empty events give `InvalidInput` (`Empty`), nonpositive periods give `InvalidInput` (`OutOfRange`), non-finite times or periods give `NonFinite`, and output shorter than the number of periods gives `DimensionMismatch`.
`vector_strength_into` checks results for all periods before writing output, so errors leave output unchanged.
`VectorStrength::default()` has strength 0 and phase `None` and is used to initialize output storage.

The following cell examines vector strength using synthetic data modeling an auditory nerve fiber firing in synchrony with a 500 Hz pure tone.
The period is $T = 2$ ms; one spike occurs per cycle, centered at phase $\pi/2$ (0.5 ms after cycle start), with Gaussian jitter of standard deviation $\sigma$.
Times are in ms.
Inputs are jitter $\sigma$ [ms], cycle count $N$, and random seed.
Use `vector_strength` to compute strength and phase at $T = 2$ ms alongside theoretical $\rho$, standard error, and the Rayleigh statistic; use `vector_strength_into` to scan 201 frequencies from 490 to 510 Hz.

```rust
//| id: signal-vector-strength
//| ref: signal-vector-strength
//| title: "Vector strength of spike times synchronized to a 500 Hz pure tone"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-random, scioxide-signal]
//| inputs:
//|   jitter: {"type":"range","label":"Spike time jitter σ [ms]","description":"Gaussian standard deviation.","min":0.05,"max":1,"step":0.05,"value":0.2}
//|   cycles: {"type":"range","label":"Cycle count N [-]","description":"Number of spikes (one per cycle).","min":50,"max":800,"step":50,"value":200}
//|   seed: {"type":"range","label":"Random seed","min":1,"max":100,"step":1,"value":7}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"weak-locking","label":"σ = 1 ms (nearly uniform)","description":"Theoretical ρ is 0.0072, and the Rayleigh statistic does not indicate synchronization.","inputs":{"jitter":1}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"strength","path":"/rows/0/2","approx":{"value":0.8209,"atol":1e-4}}
//|       - {"artifact":"strength","path":"/rows/0/4","range":{"min":-3,"max":3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"strength","path":"/rows/1/1","approx":{"value":1.5708,"atol":0.05}}
//|       - {"artifact":"scan","path":"/spec/series/0/points/100/1","range":{"min":0.7}}
//|   - case: weak-locking
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"strength","path":"/rows/0/2","approx":{"value":0.00719,"atol":1e-4}}
//|       - {"artifact":"strength","path":"/rows/0/4","equals":null}
//|       - {"artifact":"rayleigh","path":"/rows/0/2","range":{"min":0.05}}
use scioxide_core::ExecutionContext;
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Normal, SeedableRng, SplitMix64};
use scioxide_signal::{VectorStrength, vector_strength, vector_strength_into};

// Period T = 2 ms (500 Hz). Spikes are centered 0.5 ms after each cycle starts (phase π/2).
let period = 2.0_f64;
let n = cycles.round() as usize;
let mut rng = SplitMix64::from_seed(seed as u64);
let noise = Normal::new(0.0, jitter).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut events = Vec::with_capacity(n);
for k in 0..n {
    let delay = noise.sample(&mut rng).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    events.push(k as f64 * period + 0.5 + delay);
}

let locked = vector_strength(&events, period).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let strength = locked.strength();
// Wrapped-normal theoretical value and standard error estimated from the component variance along the mean direction.
let rho = (-0.5 * (std::f64::consts::TAU * jitter / period).powi(2)).exp();
let standard_error = (1.0 - rho * rho) / (2.0 * n as f64).sqrt();
let rayleigh = n as f64 * strength * strength;
// Use the normal approximation only when ρ√N is sufficiently large. Near uniformity, use the Rayleigh statistic.
let z_score = (rho * (n as f64).sqrt() >= 3.0).then(|| (strength - rho) / standard_error);

// Scan 490–510 Hz in 0.1 Hz steps. Pass periods in ms, matching the times.
let frequencies: Vec<f64> = (0..=200).map(|i| 490.0 + 0.1 * i as f64).collect();
let periods: Vec<f64> = frequencies.iter().map(|f| 1000.0 / f).collect();
let mut scan = vec![VectorStrength::default(); periods.len()];
vector_strength_into(&events, &periods, &mut scan, ExecutionContext::sequential())
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let points: Vec<[f64; 2]> = frequencies
    .iter()
    .zip(&scan)
    .map(|(&f, result)| [f, result.strength()])
    .collect();

let fixed4 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 4 });
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Quantity" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Computed value", "type": "number", "format": fixed4, "nullText": "Undefined" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Theoretical value", "type": "number", "format": fixed4, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Standard error", "type": "number", "format": fixed4, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "(Computed − theoretical)/SE", "type": "number", "format": fixed4, "nullText": "—" }
]);
let rows = vec![
    (
        "Strength |R| [-]".to_owned(),
        Some(strength),
        Some(rho),
        z_score.map(|_| standard_error),
        z_score,
    ),
    (
        "Mean phase arg R [rad]".to_owned(),
        locked.phase(),
        Some(std::f64::consts::FRAC_PI_2),
        None,
        None,
    ),
];
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "strength" })
);

    { "key": "0", "label": "Event count N [-]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Rayleigh statistic Z [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed4 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Approximate p value e^(−Z) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    rayleigh_columns,
    vec![(n, rayleigh, (-rayleigh).exp())],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "rayleigh" })
);
emit_chart!(
    serde_json::json!({
        "kind": "line",
        "dataZoom": true,
        "tooltip": true,
        "series": [{ "name": "Vector strength |R|", "points": points }],
        "xLabel": "Scanned frequency [Hz]",
        "yLabel": "Vector strength [-]",
        "xMin": 490.0,
        "xMax": 510.0,
        "yMin": 0.0,
        "yMax": 1.0,
        "annotations": [{ "kind": "line", "axis": "x", "value": 500.0, "label": "Stimulus 500 Hz" }]
    }),
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "scan" })
);
```

With defaults $\sigma = 0.2$ ms, $N = 200$, and seed 7, strength is $0.8142$; its difference from theoretical $\rho = 0.8209$ is $-0.41$ times the standard error $0.0163$.
Mean phase is $1.594$ rad, close to the central firing phase $\pi/2 \approx 1.571$ rad.
The Rayleigh statistic is $Z = 132.6$ and the approximate $p$ value is $2.6\times10^{-58}$, rejecting the hypothesis of uniformly scattered phases.
At $N = 800$, strength is $0.8188$ and standard error $0.0082$; the difference is $-0.25$ standard errors, and estimation fluctuations decrease in proportion to $1/\sqrt{N}$.

The scan peaks at $0.814$ at 500 Hz and falls to $0.607$ and $0.616$ at 499 and 501 Hz.
For record duration $D = NT = 0.4$ s, shifting frequency by $\Delta f$ rotates phase by $2\pi\Delta f\,t$ over the record, so strength falls approximately as $\rho\,\lvert\mathrm{sinc}(\pi\Delta f D)\rvert$.
At $\Delta f = 1$ Hz, $0.821 \times 0.757 \approx 0.62$, matching the chart.
Period resolution is determined by reciprocal record duration $1/D$; increasing the event count without increasing duration does not refine it.

At $\sigma = 1$ ms, $\rho = 0.0072$ and phases are nearly uniform.
Strength is $0.030$, $Z = 0.185$, and the approximate $p$ value is $0.83$, so synchronization is not detected.
Even for uniform phases, $\lvert R \rvert$ is nonzero, with a positive mean of about $\sqrt{\pi/(4N)} \approx 0.063$.
When $\rho\sqrt{N}$ is this small, the normal-approximation standard error is unusable; the table shows “—” for the standard error and ratio, and the Rayleigh statistic is used for assessment.

## Summary

`hilbert` and `envelope` treat a record as one period and construct an analytic signal using weights that retain DC and the even-length Nyquist bin and double positive frequencies.
The real part matches the input bit for bit; the envelope and instantaneous frequency can be interpreted when envelope bandwidth is narrower than carrier frequency and record endpoints join smoothly.
In multiple dimensions, the single-orthant analytic signal (`AnalyticSignalNdPlan`) depends on orientation; use the monogenic signal from `RieszPlan` with physical sample spacings for orientation-independent local amplitude and local orientation.
Measure synchronization of event times to a period using strength and phase from `vector_strength`, and compare with chance using the Rayleigh statistic and a standard error estimate.

See [Recovering an envelope with the Hilbert transform](../../examples/hilbert-envelope/) for envelope errors when Bedrosian's condition fails.
Compare tracking time-varying frequencies through windowed Fourier transforms in [Short-time Fourier transforms](../stft/).
