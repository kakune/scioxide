Many circuits, machines, and chemical reactors can be approximated by linear ordinary differential equations near equilibrium.
When coefficients are independent of time, the system is called linear time-invariant (LTI).
An LTI input-output relationship can be represented by a transfer function, impulse response, or state-space model.
This page explains conversions, discretization, time and frequency response computation, and state-feedback pole placement for the state-space models central to `scioxide-signal`.

LTI is a modeling assumption and does not automatically extend to nonlinear or time-varying systems.
For nonlinear systems, linearize around an equilibrium before using these methods.

## State-space models

Continuous and discrete state-space models have the following forms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Continuous:}\quad & \dot x(t) = A x(t) + B u(t), & y(t) &= C x(t) + D u(t), \\
\text{Discrete:}\quad & x[k+1] = A x[k] + B u[k], & y[k] &= C x[k] + D u[k].
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, $x$ is a state of length $n$, $u$ an input of length $m$, and $y$ an output of length $p$.
Matrices $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ have shapes $n \times n$, $n \times m$, $p \times n$, and $p \times m$; $D$ is the direct-feedthrough term from input to output.
The caller chooses the continuous system's time unit; entries of $A$ have reciprocal-time units.

`StateSpace::new(time, a, b, c, d)` checks shapes and finiteness of all four matrices, then retains copies.
Matrices are row-major and may be real or complex.
Any of $n$, $m$, and $p$ may be zero.
Incompatible shapes give `DimensionMismatch`; NaN or infinite entries or sample periods give `NonFinite`; a nonpositive discrete sample period gives `InvalidInput`.

`LtiTime` selects the time domain.
`LtiTime::Continuous` denotes a continuous system and `LtiTime::Discrete { sample_period }` a discrete system with sample period $T$; `sample_period()` returns `None` for continuous systems.
`view()` returns a `StateSpaceView` without copying matrices; `StateSpaceView::to_owned` restores an owned model.
Use a borrowed view when passing a model to functions that only read dimensions (`states`, `inputs`, `outputs`) or matrices.

Model poles are the eigenvalues of $A$; `poles()` computes them in $O(n^3)$ through balancing, Hessenberg reduction, and shifted QR iteration.
For real models, complex poles are returned as exact conjugate pairs.
`controllability_matrix()` forms $[B, AB, \dots, A^{n-1}B]$, and `observability_matrix()` forms $[C; CA; \dots; CA^{n-1}]$.
`controllability(policy)` and `observability(policy)` return numerical ranks by counting singular values exceeding the `RankPolicy` threshold.
Rank $n$ means the pair is controllable (observable).

## Conversion to transfer functions and zero-pole representations

The transfer function from input $j$ to output $i$ is

$$
H_{ij}(\lambda) = c_i (\lambda I - A)^{-1} b_j + d_{ij} = \frac{N(\lambda)}{\det(\lambda I - A)}
$$


Here, $b_j$ is column $j$ of $B$, $c_i$ is row $i$ of $C$, and $d_{ij}$ is an entry of $D$; $\lambda$ is $s$ for continuous systems and $z$ for discrete systems.

`to_transfer(input, output)` takes input then output as arguments and converts this path to a `TransferFunction`.
The denominator is expanded from the eigenvalues of $A$; the numerator is obtained from eigenvalues of $A - b_j c_i$ using the matrix determinant lemma $\det(\lambda I - A + b c) = \det(\lambda I - A) + c\,\mathrm{adj}(\lambda I - A)\,b$.
Common factors are not canceled, so uncontrollable or unobservable modes remain in both numerator and denominator.
The cost is $O(n^3)$ with $O(n^2)$ memory.
`to_zpk(input, output)` finds roots of that numerator and denominator with `TransferFunction::to_zpk` and returns a `PoleZeroGain`.
Use `to_zpk_scoped` to find roots within an outer solver's limits.

High-degree polynomial coefficients are ill-conditioned functions of their roots.
For high-order models, compute responses directly from state space with `frequency_response` below, avoiding transfer-function conversion.

For the reverse conversion, `TransferFunction::to_state_space(time)` creates a single-input, single-output controllable canonical form.
For normalized denominator $1, a_1, \dots, a_n$, $A$ is the companion matrix with first row $-a_1, \dots, -a_n$ and ones on the subdiagonal, with $B = e_1$ and $D = b_0$.
`PoleZeroGain::to_state_space(time)` groups poles and zeros into sections of order at most two, converts each to controllable canonical form, and connects them in series.
Avoiding high-degree polynomial expansion avoids coefficient ill-conditioning, but the matrices are complex.
Both conversions require `LtiTime::Continuous` for analog representations and `LtiTime::Discrete` for digital ones; a mismatch gives `InvalidInput`.

## Discretizing continuous systems

To use a continuous system in digital control or simulation, map it to a discrete system with sample period $h$.
`discretize(h, method)` accepts only continuous systems and returns a new model with `LtiTime::Discrete { sample_period: h }`.
Select the method with `DiscretizationMethod`.

### Zero-order hold and the matrix exponential

Zero-order hold (ZOH) assumes input is held constant over each sampling interval.
If $u$ is constant on $[kh, (k+1)h)$, the state equation has the solution

$$
x[k+1] = e^{hA} x[k] + \left( \int_0^h e^{sA}\,ds \right) B\,u[k]
$$

which is exact under this assumption.
Thus, $A_d = e^{hA}$, $B_d = \int_0^h e^{sA}\,ds\,B$, $C_d = C$, and $D_d = D$.

Writing $B_d$ as $A^{-1}(A_d - I)B$ fails when $A$ is singular, as in systems containing integrators.
Instead, `DiscretizationMethod::ZeroOrderHold` computes one exponential of a block matrix of order $n + m$:

$$
\exp\left( h \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \right) = \begin{bmatrix} A_d & B_d \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}
$$

It then reads the upper-left and upper-right blocks.
The matrix exponential uses Padé approximation and scaling and squaring in `scioxide-linalg` ([Matrix function algorithms](oxref:linalg-matrix-function-methods)).

### Generalized bilinear transform

`DiscretizationMethod::GeneralizedBilinear { alpha }` is a generalized bilinear transform with weight $\alpha \in [0, 1]$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_d &= (I - \alpha h A)^{-1} \bigl( I + (1 - \alpha) h A \bigr), &
B_d &= (I - \alpha h A)^{-1} h B, \\
C_d &= C (I - \alpha h A)^{-1}, &
D_d &= D + \alpha C B_d.
\end{aligned}
$$

One LU factorization of $I - \alpha h A$ yields all four matrices.
This model's transfer function equals the continuous $H_c(s)$ under the substitution

$$
s = \frac{z - 1}{h \bigl( \alpha z + 1 - \alpha \bigr)}
$$


$\alpha = 0$ gives forward Euler, $\alpha = 1/2$ the Tustin transform (bilinear transform, trapezoidal rule), and $\alpha = 1$ backward Euler.
These are the same maps as the ODE $\theta$ method; eigenvalue $\lambda$ maps to $z = (1 + (1 - \alpha) h\lambda) / (1 - \alpha h \lambda)$.

For $\alpha \ge 1/2$, the left half-plane maps into the unit disk, so stable continuous systems remain stable after discretization at any step size.
For $\alpha = 0$, $|1 + h\lambda| < 1$ is required, placing a strict upper bound on $h$ for lightly damped oscillatory systems.
Stability functions and absolute stability regions are explained in [ODE error and stability](../../../scioxide-ode/algorithms/error-order-and-stability/).

An $\alpha$ outside $[0, 1]$ or an already discrete model gives `InvalidInput`; singular $I - \alpha h A$ gives `Singular`; overflow in the matrix exponential or discretized matrices gives `NumericalInstability`.
Both methods require $O(n^3)$ operations when $m, p \le n$.

### Discretization error and stability

The following block defines the model shared by two cells on this page.
It represents a damped oscillator $\ddot x + 2\zeta \dot x + x = u$ in dimensionless time with state $(x, \dot x)$ and two outputs, position and velocity.
Natural angular frequency is 1, and poles are $-\zeta \pm i\omega_d$ ($\omega_d = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$).
`step_position` and `ramp_position` give closed-form positions from zero initial state for a unit step $u = 1$ and ramp $u = t$ when $0 < \zeta < 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{\mathrm{step}}(t) &= 1 - e^{-\zeta t} \left( \cos \omega_d t + \frac{\zeta}{\omega_d} \sin \omega_d t \right), \\
x_{\mathrm{ramp}}(t) &= t - 2\zeta + e^{-\zeta t} \left( 2\zeta \cos \omega_d t + \frac{2\zeta^2 - 1}{\omega_d} \sin \omega_d t \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

These serve as reference values for checks.

```rust
//| id: signal-lti-oscillator-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Damped-oscillator state-space model and closed-form step response"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-linalg, scioxide-signal]
use scioxide_core::SciOxideError;
use scioxide_linalg::Matrix;
use scioxide_signal::{LtiTime, StateSpace};

/// Continuous system x'' + 2ζx' + x = u with state (x, x'). Outputs are position x and velocity x', in that order.
pub(crate) fn oscillator(damping_ratio: f64) -> Result<StateSpace<f64>, SciOxideError> {
    let a = Matrix::from_vec(2, 2, vec![0.0, 1.0, -1.0, -2.0 * damping_ratio])?;
    let b = Matrix::from_vec(2, 1, vec![0.0, 1.0])?;
    let c = Matrix::identity(2)?;
    let d = Matrix::zeros(2, 1)?;
    StateSpace::new(LtiTime::Continuous, a.view(), b.view(), c.view(), d.view())
}

/// Position x(t) for a unit step u = 1 from zero initial state (0 < ζ < 1). Reference using standard f64 functions.
pub(crate) fn step_position(damping_ratio: f64, time: f64) -> f64 {
    let damped = (1.0 - damping_ratio * damping_ratio).sqrt();
    let envelope = (-damping_ratio * time).exp();
    1.0 - envelope * ((damped * time).cos() + damping_ratio / damped * (damped * time).sin())
}

/// Position x(t) for a ramp u = t from zero initial state (0 < ζ < 1). Reference using standard f64 functions.
pub(crate) fn ramp_position(damping_ratio: f64, time: f64) -> f64 {
    let damped = (1.0 - damping_ratio * damping_ratio).sqrt();
    let envelope = (-damping_ratio * time).exp();
    let sine = (2.0 * damping_ratio * damping_ratio - 1.0) / damped;
    let homogeneous = 2.0 * damping_ratio * (damped * time).cos() + sine * (damped * time).sin();
    time - 2.0 * damping_ratio + envelope * homogeneous
}
```

With defaults ($\zeta = 0.1$, $h = 0.4$, step input), zero-order hold error is at most $2.4 \times 10^{-15}$ for every step size, at rounding-error level.
The step is constant on each interval, exactly satisfying the zero-order hold assumption and giving exact values at sample points.
Forward Euler has spectral radius 1.039, exceeding 1, and its plotted response diverges while oscillating.
Eigenvalue $\lambda$ maps to $1 + h\lambda$; since $|1 + h\lambda|^2 = 1 - 2h\zeta + h^2$, stability requires $h < 2\zeta$.
The Tustin transform has observed order 0.961 rather than the trapezoidal rule's second order.
The bilinear transform linearly interpolates input samples, treating the jump at $t = 0$ as a ramp over $[-h, 0]$ and leaving an $O(h)$ error.
Backward Euler has observed order 0.766; steps of $h/4 = 0.1$ have not yet reached the asymptotic first-order regime.

The relationships reverse in the “Ramp input” case.
Input varies within intervals, violating zero-order hold assumptions and reducing its observed order to 1.000.
The input is linear, making Tustin interpolation exact and giving observed order 1.999.
Discretization error depends on assumptions about input between samples as well as method order.

In case “h = 0.1”, $h < 2\zeta$ holds and forward Euler has spectral radius 0.995, making it stable.
In case “ζ = 0.9”, stronger damping gives forward Euler spectral radius 0.663 even at step 0.4.
Backward Euler and Tustin remain stable at every step size, but backward Euler adds damping to the oscillation.
Stability and high accuracy are separate properties.

## Time responses

`impulse_response(times)`, `step_response(times)`, and `simulate_zoh(times, inputs, initial_state)` sample responses on a time grid and return `TimeResponse`.
The grid `times` must be nonempty, start at zero, and be equally spaced with step $h$ = `times[1]`.
Each sample may differ from the rounded product $kh$ by up to $64\varepsilon(|t_k| + h)$, admitting grids formed by `k as f64 * h` or `k as f64 * T / (K - 1)`.
For discrete systems, $h$ must match sample period $T$ within $64\varepsilon(1 + T)$; a mismatch gives `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`).

For continuous systems, preparation computes the zero-order hold transition $(A_d, B_d)$ for step $h$ once, then advances $x[k+1] = A_d x[k] + B_d u[k]$.
For input constant over each interval, sample values are exact up to rounding; accuracy depends on system conditioning and accumulated rounding over steps.
The cost per response channel is $O(K(n + p)(n + m))$, where $K$ is the time count.

Impulse and step responses have one channel per input.
Channel $j$ is the response with only input $j$ driven.
A continuous impulse response samples only its ordinary-function part $C e^{At} B$, excluding the feedthrough delta function $D\delta(t)$.
The discrete impulse response is $y[0] = D$, and $y[k] = C A^{k-1} B$ for $k \ge 1$.
Step responses start from zero state and include $D$ at every sample.
`simulate_zoh` computes one channel from input in `[sample][input]` order and an initial state; the first state-history row is the initial state.

In `TimeResponse`, `values()` follows `[sample][output][channel]` order and `states()` follows `[sample][state][channel]` order.
For repeated computations on the same grid, create `TimeResponsePlan::new(model, times)` and allocate workspace `TimeResponseWorkspace` once using `workspace()`.
`impulse_response_into`, `step_response_into`, and `simulate_into` write output and state histories into caller-provided storage without allocating during execution.
Query lengths with methods such as `output_len()` ($Kpm$), `states_len()` ($Knm$), and `simulation_output_len()` ($Kp$); incorrect lengths give `DimensionMismatch`.
The three APIs returning owned results create a plan and execute with a sequential context.

## Frequency responses and frequency units

A frequency response evaluates $H(\lambda) = C(\lambda I - A)^{-1} B + D$ on a frequency grid.
Units follow the crate-wide conventions.
Continuous systems accept angular frequency $\omega$ [rad/time unit] and evaluate $\lambda = i\omega$.
Discrete systems accept the ratio $f$ to Nyquist frequency and evaluate $\lambda = e^{i\pi f}$ without using the sample period.
$f = 1$ denotes Nyquist frequency; conversion to angular frequency gives $\omega = \pi f / T$.
This matches filter evaluations such as `TransferFunction::response`, giving equal values for different representations of the same system.

`FrequencyResponsePlan::new(model)` uses Householder reflections for upper Hessenberg reduction $A = Q H Q^{\mathsf H}$, retaining $H$, $Q^{\mathsf H} B$, and $C Q$ ($O(n^3)$).
At each frequency, it solves $(\lambda I - H) X = Q^{\mathsf H} B$ by Gaussian elimination with row pivoting and forms $C Q X + D$ at cost $O(n^2 m)$.
Avoiding the characteristic polynomial avoids coefficient ill-conditioning even for high-order models.
However, as $\lambda$ approaches a pole, $\lambda I - A$ becomes ill-conditioned and accuracy decreases.
A frequency where $\lambda I - A$ is exactly singular gives `Singular`.

`workspace_requirements(K)` returns sizes for $K$ frequencies as `FrequencyResponseRequirements`; `output_len()` gives output length $Kpm$.
Allocate workspace `FrequencyResponseWorkspace` with `plan.workspace(K)` and grow it for longer sweeps with `try_reserve`.
`frequency_response_into(frequencies, output, workspace, cx)` requires `output` of length exactly $Kpm$ and writes in `[frequency][output][input]` order.
The value for frequency $k$, output $i$, and input $j$ has index $(kp + i)m + j$.
The owned-result API `frequency_response` creates a plan, performs one sweep, and returns copies of frequencies and values in `FrequencyResponse`.

```rust
let h = sample_period;
// Discrete frequencies are ratios f to Nyquist frequency (0 < f < 1).
let ratios: Vec<f64> = (1..400).map(|k| k as f64 / 400.0).collect();
let analog: Vec<f64> = ratios.iter().map(|f| PI * f / h).collect();
// Prewarped angular frequency Ω = (2/h) tan(πf/2). Reference using standard f64 functions.
let warped: Vec<f64> = ratios
    .iter()
    .map(|f| 2.0 / h * (PI * f / 2.0).tan())
    .collect();

// Create the continuous plan and workspace once and reuse them for both sweeps.
let plan = FrequencyResponsePlan::new(&model).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let requirements = plan
    .workspace_requirements(ratios.len())
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut workspace = plan.workspace(ratios.len()).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let zero = Complex::new(0.0, 0.0);
let mut at_analog = vec![zero; requirements.output_len()];
let mut at_warped = vec![zero; requirements.output_len()];
let cx = ExecutionContext::sequential();
plan.frequency_response_into(&analog, &mut at_analog, &mut workspace, cx)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
plan.frequency_response_into(&warped, &mut at_warped, &mut workspace, cx)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

let tustin = model
    .discretize(h, DiscretizationMethod::GeneralizedBilinear { alpha: 0.5 })
    .and_then(|discrete| discrete.frequency_response(&ratios))
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let zoh = model
    .discretize(h, DiscretizationMethod::ZeroOrderHold)
    .and_then(|discrete| discrete.frequency_response(&ratios))
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

// There are p = 2 outputs (position, velocity) and m = 1 input, so frequency k, output i is at index 2k + i.
let warp_gap = tustin
    .values()
    .iter()
    .zip(&at_warped)
    .map(|(d, c)| (*d - *c).norm())
    .fold(0.0, f64::max);
let order_gap = analog
    .iter()
    .enumerate()
    .map(|(k, &w)| (at_analog[2 * k + 1] - Complex::new(0.0, w) * at_analog[2 * k]).norm())
    .fold(0.0, f64::max);
let rows = vec![
    ("max |H_d(e^{iπf}) − H_c(iΩ)| (Tustin, both outputs)", warp_gap),
    ("max |H_velocity − iω H_position| (continuous)", order_gap),
];
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Check" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Value [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "checks" })
);

let magnitude = |values: &[Complex<f64>], k: usize| values[2 * k].norm();
let curve = |values: &[Complex<f64>]| -> Vec<[f64; 2]> {
    analog
        .iter()
        .enumerate()
        .map(|(k, &w)| [w, magnitude(values, k)])
        .collect()
};
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![
        serde_json::json!({
            "name": "Continuous H_c(iω)",
            "points": curve(&at_analog),
            "style": { "lineType": "dashed" }
        }),
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Tustin transform", "points": curve(tustin.values()) }),
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Zero-order hold", "points": curve(zoh.values()) }),
    ],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Angular frequency ω = πf/h [rad/time unit]",
        "yLabel": "Position magnitude |H| [-]",
        "xType": "log",
        "yType": "log",
        "annotations": [{
            "kind": "line",
            "axis": "x",
            "value": PI / h,
            "label": "Nyquist frequency π/h",
            "style": { "lineType": "dotted" }
        }]
    })
);
```

With defaults ($\zeta = 0.1$, $h = 0.5$), the difference between Tustin and prewarped continuous responses is $1.7 \times 10^{-14}$, confirming substitution equivalence to within rounding error.
The velocity-to-position check also differs by $8.9 \times 10^{-16}$, confirming output order `[frequency][output][input]`.
The three curves overlap at low frequencies.
Tustin resonance shifts to lower $\omega$ through the angular-frequency compression $\Omega = (2/h)\tan(\pi f/2)$, and its magnitude tends to zero at Nyquist frequency.
The zero-order hold response includes aliasing with period $2\pi/h$ and departs from the continuous system near Nyquist frequency.
In case “h = 2”, Nyquist frequency $\pi/2$ approaches resonance (angular frequency 1), increasing the differences between discretizations.
To match a specific frequency with Tustin, begin with an analog design prewarped at that frequency ([IIR filter design](../iir-design/)).

## Pole placement

Adding state feedback $u = -Kx$ gives the closed-loop matrix $A - BK$.
Pole placement finds a gain $K$ ($m \times n$) making the eigenvalues of $A - BK$ equal the $n$ requested poles $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$.
If the pair $(A, B)$ is controllable, arbitrary poles can be placed (conjugate pairs for real systems).

`place_poles(requested)` computes $K$ as follows.
Models without states or inputs give `InvalidInput` (`Empty`); a pole count other than $n$ gives `DimensionMismatch`.

1. For real systems, verify a conjugate partner within relative distance $32\varepsilon$ for every nonreal pole, then make each pair exactly conjugate.
   A nonreal pole without a partner gives `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`).
2. Test numerical controllability rank with `RankPolicy`; deficient rank gives `NumericalInstability`.
   Requesting the same pole more than $\operatorname{rank}(B)$ times gives `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`).
3. If $\operatorname{rank}(B) = n$, form a block diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ with the requested eigenvalues and solve $BK = A - \Lambda$ using the SVD of $B$.
4. Otherwise, choose vectors $(v_j, w_j)$ from the null space of $[A - \lambda_j I, -B]$ for each pole $\lambda_j$ and set $K = W V^{-1}$.
   Then $(A - BK) v_j = \lambda_j v_j$ holds.
   Reselect null-space columns until $V$ is numerically nonsingular.
5. Independently compute eigenvalues of $A - BK$ and compare them with the requested poles.

The comparison residual `maximum_residual` is the maximum $|\mu - \lambda| / (1 + |\lambda|)$ when requested poles $\lambda$ are matched in order to the nearest unmatched achieved poles $\mu$.
If the residual is within tolerance (default $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$), termination status `PolePlacementTermination` is `Converged`; otherwise, it is `ResidualToleranceNotMet`.
The standard `place_poles` form turns the latter into `ConvergenceFailure`.
The options form `place_poles_with_options(requested, options)` selects rank policy (`with_rank_policy`) and tolerance (`with_residual_tolerance`) through `PolePlacementOptions` and returns a `PolePlacementReport` even when tolerance is not met.
Use `place_poles_scoped` to compute within an outer solver's limits.

Use the gain only when the report's `is_success()` is true.
Even when false, `gain()` and `achieved_poles()` contain finite values, and `trust()` returns `DiagnosticOnly`.
Achieved pole accuracy depends on eigenvector matrix $V$ conditioning; `transfer_condition()` returns its estimate (`None` when $\operatorname{rank}(B) = n$).
`controllability_condition()` and `input_condition()` estimate conditioning of the controllability matrix and $B$.

The following cell places the poles of a chain of $n$ integrators $x_1' = x_2, \dots, x_{n-1}' = x_n, x_n' = u$ at $-1, -2, \dots, -n$.
This system is in controllable canonical form, so $A - BK$ has characteristic polynomial $s^n + K_n s^{n-1} + \dots + K_1$.
The exact gain therefore consists of coefficients of $\prod_{j=1}^{n}(s + j)$; the cell independently checks these by expansion in integer arithmetic.
Requested poles are equally spaced as in Wilkinson's polynomial, so increasing $n$ rapidly worsens conditioning of $V$ (a Vandermonde-type matrix).
Changing the tolerance exponent changes the termination assessment for the same gain.

```rust
//| id: signal-lti-pole-placement
//| ref: signal-lti-pole-placement
//| title: "Pole placement for an integrator chain and closed-form gains"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-linalg, scioxide-signal]
//| inputs:
//|   state_count: {"type":"range","label":"State count n [-]","description":"Number of integrators. Requested poles are −1, …, −n.","min":2,"max":12,"step":1,"value":6}
//|   tolerance_exponent: {"type":"range","label":"Residual tolerance exponent q (10^q)","description":"PolePlacementOptions tolerance.","min":-14,"max":-4,"step":1,"value":-8}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"long-chain","label":"n = 12","description":"Conditioning of V worsens, and the residual exceeds the default tolerance.","inputs":{"state_count":12}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"gains","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"max":1e-9}}
//|       - {"path":"/outputs/1/state","equals":"satisfied"}
//|   - case: long-chain
//|     expect:
//|       - {"path":"/outputs/1/state","equals":"partial"}
use scioxide_core::Complex;
use scioxide_linalg::Matrix;
use scioxide_signal::{LtiTime, PolePlacementOptions, StateSpace};

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let n = state_count.round() as usize;
// A is an integrator chain with ones on the superdiagonal, B = e_n, C = e_1ᵀ, D = 0.
let mut a = Matrix::zeros(n, n).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
for row in 0..n - 1 {
    a.as_mut_slice()[row * n + row + 1] = 1.0;
}
let mut b = Matrix::zeros(n, 1).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
b.as_mut_slice()[n - 1] = 1.0;
let mut c = Matrix::zeros(1, n).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
c.as_mut_slice()[0] = 1.0;
let d = Matrix::zeros(1, 1).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let model = StateSpace::new(LtiTime::Continuous, a.view(), b.view(), c.view(), d.view())
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

let requested: Vec<Complex<f64>> = (1..=n).map(|j| Complex::new(-(j as f64), 0.0)).collect();
let options =
    PolePlacementOptions::new().with_residual_tolerance(10.0_f64.powf(tolerance_exponent));
let report = model
    .place_poles_with_options(&requested, options)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

// Independent check: expand ∏(s + j) with integer coefficients. coefficients[k] is the coefficient of s^k.
let mut coefficients = vec![1_i64];
for j in 1..=n as i64 {
    let mut next = vec![0_i64; coefficients.len() + 1];
    for (k, &value) in coefficients.iter().enumerate() {
        next[k] += j * value;
        next[k + 1] += value;
    }
    coefficients = next;
}
let gain = report.gain().as_slice();
let gain_error = (0..n)
    .map(|k| (gain[k] - coefficients[k] as f64).abs() / coefficients[k] as f64)
    .fold(0.0, f64::max);
let condition = report
    .transfer_condition()
    .map(|estimate| 1.0 / estimate.reciprocal().value());

let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "K_1 = n! [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "1", "label": "K_n = n(n+1)/2 [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Condition number estimate for V [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": "None" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Maximum relative gain error [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Maximum pole residual [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    vec![(
        gain[0],
        gain[n - 1],
        condition,
        gain_error,
        report.maximum_residual()
    )],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "gains" })
);

let outcome = if report.is_success() {
    Outcome::satisfied("Achieved poles are within tolerance of requested poles")
} else {
    Outcome::partial("Achieved poles do not meet tolerance")
        .explanation("A gain is returned, but inspect achieved poles and conditioning of V before using it.")
};
emit_outcome!(
    outcome
        .status(format!("{:?}", report.termination()))
        .diagnostic("Maximum pole residual", report.maximum_residual(), None)
        .diagnostic("Maximum relative gain error", gain_error, None)
);
```

With defaults ($n = 6$, tolerance $10^{-8}$), maximum relative gain error is $1.4 \times 10^{-13}$ and maximum pole residual $4.6 \times 10^{-12}$; termination is `Converged`.
The condition estimate for $V$ is $1.9 \times 10^{5}$.
In case “n = 12”, the condition estimate grows to $2.1 \times 10^{14}$ and pole residual $1.7 \times 10^{-4}$ exceeds tolerance, giving `ResidualToleranceNotMet`.
Relative gain error is then $3.7 \times 10^{-9}$; although the gain itself is close to the closed form, closed-loop poles differ substantially.
For high-degree polynomials with equally spaced poles, small relative coefficient errors can move roots substantially.
Even at default $n = 6$, setting tolerance exponent to $-14$ makes residual $4.6 \times 10^{-12}$ exceed tolerance and changes the assessment to `ResidualToleranceNotMet`, without changing gain or poles.
The assessment is a conclusion relative to tolerance; read placement accuracy itself from residuals and conditioning.

## Summary

Validate and retain state-space models with `StateSpace::new` and distinguish continuous and discrete systems through `LtiTime`.
For discretization, choose zero-order hold when input can be assumed constant per interval or Tustin to preserve the frequency-axis mapping, and observe the forward Euler stability condition $|1 + h\lambda| < 1$.
Frequency responses use angular frequencies for continuous systems and ratios to Nyquist frequency for discrete systems; output follows `[frequency][output][input]` order.
Continuous impulse responses exclude the delta function $D\delta(t)$, so handle feedthrough separately when present.
For pole placement, inspect termination, achieved pole residuals, and the condition number of $V$ as well as the gain.
