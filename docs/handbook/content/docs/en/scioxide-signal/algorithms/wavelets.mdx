The Fourier transform separates a signal into frequencies, but its coefficients do not retain when a component appeared.
Local changes such as steps and spikes spread across coefficients at all frequencies.
The discrete wavelet transform (DWT) repeatedly splits a signal with low-pass and high-pass filters and downsamples each output by two, assigning coefficients to both time positions and scales (frequency bands).
This page explains the single-level computation and perfect reconstruction conditions, built-in filter families, multilevel coefficient ordering, endpoints of finite records, and extension to two dimensions.
The denoising application is covered in [Wavelet denoising](../../examples/wavelet-denoising/).

## Two-channel filter banks

Using a low-pass analysis filter $h$ and a high-pass analysis filter $g$ of length $L$, form approximation coefficients $a$ and detail coefficients $d$ from signal $x$ as follows:

$$
a[j] = \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} h[k]\,\tilde x[2j + k - o],
\qquad
d[j] = \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} g[k]\,\tilde x[2j + k - o]
$$


Here, $\tilde x$ extends the signal outside the record according to a boundary rule, and $o$ is the filter phase offset (described below).
The coefficient index $j$ advances every two samples, so $a$ and $d$ each have about half the original length.
This is equivalent to filtering and then retaining every other sample (downsampling).

Synthesis uses synthesis filters $\tilde h$ and $\tilde g$ to assemble the original samples from the downsampled coefficients:

$$
x[n] = \sum_j \left(\tilde h[n + o - 2j]\,a[j] + \tilde g[n + o - 2j]\,d[j]\right)
$$


Downsampling introduces aliasing, but if the following two conditions in the z-transform hold,

$$
\tilde H(z) H(z) + \tilde G(z) G(z) = 2 z^{-\ell},
\qquad
\tilde H(z) H(-z) + \tilde G(z) G(-z) = 0
$$

aliasing from the two bands cancels, and synthesis reproduces the input with only a delay $\ell$ (the terms replacing $z$ with $-z$ are the aliasing components).
These are called the perfect reconstruction conditions.
All built-in filters are designed to satisfy them.

An orthogonal (orthonormal) filter pair uses the same filters for synthesis and analysis, and the family of translated filters forms an orthonormal basis.
The high-pass filter is then determined by $g[k] = (-1)^k h[L - 1 - k]$, with $\sum_k h[k] = \sqrt2$ and $\sum_k h[k]^2 = 1$.
Because the transform is an orthogonal matrix, periodic extension and an even-length record give equal sums of squares for the coefficients and signal (Parseval's relation).
A biorthogonal filter pair uses different analysis and synthesis filters, with mutually dual analysis and synthesis bases.
It combines symmetric filters (linear phase) with compact support, but the transform is not an orthogonal matrix, so the coefficient sum of squares does not equal signal energy.
Do not compare orthogonal and biorthogonal coefficients under the same energy convention.

## Built-in families

`WaveletFamily` selects a built-in filter pair.
Every family is normalized so that the low-pass analysis filter coefficients sum to $\sqrt2$.

| Variant | Family | Accepted values | Properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Haar` | Haar | None | Two-tap orthogonal family. Represents piecewise constant signals sparsely |
| `Daubechies(L)` | Daubechies | Even lengths $L = 4, 6, \dots, 20$ | Orthogonal, with $L/2$ vanishing moments; detail coefficients for polynomials of degree at most $L/2 - 1$ are zero away from boundaries |
| `BiorthogonalSpline(c)` | Cohen–Daubechies–Feauveau splines | $c = 100 i + j$: 103, 105, 202, 204, 206, 208, 301, 303, 305, 307, 309 | Biorthogonal; $i$ and $j$ denote the orders of the two filters in Cohen–Daubechies–Feauveau notation, and the filters are symmetric (linear phase) |

`Daubechies(2)` is unavailable; select `Haar` for the two-tap orthogonal family.
Lengths or codes absent from the table give `InvalidInput`.

`WaveletFilters::new(family)` returns the family's analysis and synthesis filters rounded to working precision.
`analysis_low()`, `analysis_high()`, `synthesis_low()`, and `synthesis_high()` contain coefficients in increasing sample-index order; synthesis filters use the same phase as analysis filters.
Coefficients come from double-precision tables, so types such as `f32` receive rounded table values.
Biorthogonal tables are padded with zeros to a common even length so analysis and synthesis lengths agree.
Filters are real; complex samples use the same filter on their real and imaginary parts.

## Multilevel decomposition and coefficient packing

Repeatedly decompose the approximation $a^{(1)}$ from one level using the same filters.
A decomposition of $J$ levels returns the final approximation $a^{(J)}$ and details $d^{(J)}, d^{(J-1)}, \dots, d^{(1)}$.
At sample rate $f_s$, details at level $l$ roughly cover the band $[f_s/2^{l+1}, f_s/2^{l}]$.
Because each level halves the length, a $J$-level decomposition of a record of length $n$ costs $O(nL)$, independent of the number of levels.

`WaveletPlan::new(family, n, levels, options)` prepares a decomposition of `levels` levels for a record of length $n$.
Zero levels give the identity map.
Coefficients are returned packed into one array, with the final approximation first and details following from coarsest to finest level.

```text
[ a^(J) | d^(J) | d^(J−1) | … | d^(1) ]
```

`levels()` returns `WaveletLevel` values from finest to coarsest, describing each level's shape.
`input_len()` is the length of the signal decomposed at that level; `band_len()` is the length of each approximation and detail band; `detail_offset()` is the detail's starting offset in the packed array; and `first()` is the first coefficient index $j$ in the band.
`coefficient_len()` is the total packed coefficient count, and `approximation_len()` is the final approximation length.
Each level adds $2\,\mathrm{band\_len} - \mathrm{input\_len}$ coefficients to the total; with `Zero` or `Reflect` boundaries and no phase shift, the increase is $L - 2$ per level (plus one more for odd input length).

Decomposing too deeply makes the final approximation shorter than the filter, so the decomposition mainly reflects boundary extensions.
`wavelet_max_level(n, family)` returns the maximum useful depth

$$
J_{\max} = \max\left(0,\ \left\lfloor \log_2 \left\lfloor \frac{n}{L - 1} \right\rfloor \right\rfloor\right)
$$

where $L$ is the analysis filter length, or the common even length for biorthogonal filters.
Plans with `Zero` or `Reflect` boundaries reject deeper decompositions with `InvalidInput`.
Periodic boundaries accept up to $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ levels.

## Boundary extension and filter phase

At the ends of a finite record, filters refer to samples outside the record.
The `SignalBoundary` passed to `WaveletOptions::with_boundary` determines these samples (default `Periodic`).

- `Periodic` repeats the record with period $n$.
  An odd-length record repeats its last sample once to become even before periodization, giving $\lceil n/2 \rceil$ coefficients in each band.
- `Zero` uses zero outside the record.
- `Reflect` reflects without repeating endpoint samples ($x[-1] = x[1]$, $x[n] = x[n-2]$).
  Bands under `Zero` and `Reflect` contain coefficients at every position where the filter support overlaps the record, so `first()` may be negative and the total coefficient count exceeds $n$.

Periodic boundaries retain $n$ coefficients for even lengths, and orthogonal families give an orthogonal transform matrix.
However, unequal endpoint values produce a jump in the periodic extension and large fine-level detail coefficients.
`Reflect` gives a continuous extension at the endpoints, tending to keep nearby detail coefficients small.

`WaveletPhase` determines how coefficient index $j$ corresponds to filter position.
`Uncentered` (the default) uses $o = 0$, so tap $k$ multiplies sample $2j + k$.
`Centered` uses $o = \lfloor L/2 \rfloor$, placing the filter center at sample $2j$ and aligning coefficient positions with signal features.
Analysis and synthesis use the same phase; both phases allow perfect reconstruction.

The following cell decomposes and synthesizes a signal $x_i = \sin(4\pi i/n) + 0.5\,[\,i \ge \lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor\,]$ of length $n$, consisting of a smooth sinusoid plus a step, and computes the maximum difference from the original and the ratio of coefficient and signal sums of squares.
Inputs are the family, boundary, record length $n$, and level count.
When the requested count exceeds the limit (`wavelet_max_level` for `Zero` and `Reflect`, or $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ for `Periodic`), the plan uses the limit and displays the actual level count in the table.
Decomposition uses the standard `analyze` form; synthesis uses the caller-provided-output API `synthesize_into` with workspace from `workspace(1)`.
This checks a round trip without removing coefficients, distinct from experiments in compression or denoising.
The chart plots packed coefficients in index order and marks band boundaries with vertical lines.

```rust
let n = length.round() as usize;
let family = match family_name.as_str() {
    "Haar" => WaveletFamily::Haar,
    "Daubechies(4)" => WaveletFamily::Daubechies(4),
    "Daubechies(8)" => WaveletFamily::Daubechies(8),
    _ => WaveletFamily::BiorthogonalSpline(303),
};
let boundary = match boundary_name.as_str() {
    "Periodic" => SignalBoundary::Periodic,
    "Zero" => SignalBoundary::Zero,
    _ => SignalBoundary::Reflect,
};
// Level limit: maximum useful depth for Zero and Reflect, ⌈log₂ n⌉ for Periodic.

} else {
```

With defaults `Daubechies(8)`, `Reflect`, $n = 63$, and 3 levels, `wavelet_max_level` is $\lfloor\log_2\lfloor 63/7\rfloor\rfloor = 3$, so decomposition uses the requested 3 levels.
The 63 samples become 84 coefficients, with the first 14 forming the final approximation.
In the level table, band lengths are 35, 21, and 14, and `first()` is $-3$.
Each level adds $2 \cdot 35 - 63 = 7$ coefficients ($L - 2 = 6$ plus one for odd length), adding 21 over 3 levels.
The maximum reconstruction error is $6.7\times10^{-16}$, and the sum-of-squares ratio is $1.41$.
`Reflect` extension creates nonzero samples outside the record, so even an orthogonal family does not preserve the sum of squares.
In the finest detail band (indices 49–83), coefficients are at most $10^{-3}$ except for indices 70–72 at the step position $i = 42$ (maximum 0.24) and a few near the record ends.
The four vanishing moments of Daubechies(8) nearly cancel the smooth sinusoid, concentrating detail coefficients at the step and endpoints.

With `Periodic` and $n = 64$, there are 64 coefficients and 8 final approximation coefficients; the sum-of-squares ratio is $1 - 4\times10^{-16}$, satisfying Parseval's relation to within rounding error.
With the same `Periodic` but $n = 63$, the last sample is repeated to make 64 samples before transformation, giving ratio $1.0031$.
With `Zero` boundaries, the coefficient count grows to 84, but zero values outside the record mean orthogonal coefficients carry the signal's full energy, giving ratio $1 + 4\times10^{-16}$.
With `BiorthogonalSpline(303)`, `Periodic`, and $n = 64$, reconstruction error is $3.3\times10^{-16}$ and perfect reconstruction holds, but the sum-of-squares ratio is $1.69$.
At $n = 32$, `wavelet_max_level` falls to 2, so a request for 3 levels creates a 2-level plan.

## Two-dimensional transforms

Two-dimensional arrays such as images use a separable transform, applying one-dimensional transforms along both rows and columns.
`Wavelet2dPlan::new(family, shape, levels, layout, options)` prepares the transform of a row-major matrix of shape `shape = [rows, columns]`.
`levels[0]` is the level count for axis 0 (the row direction, transforming along each column); `levels[1]` is the count for axis 1 (along each row), each with the same limit as a one-dimensional plan.
`Wavelet2dLayout` selects how levels are combined.

- `Pyramid` (Mallat's pyramid) transforms both axes once per level and decomposes only the low-low block at the next level.
  Both axes must have equal level counts; otherwise, it returns `InvalidInput` (`Inconsistent`).
  Coefficients contain the final low-low block first, followed from coarsest to finest level by each level's three detail blocks (with detail levels `[0, l]`, `[l, 0]`, and `[l, l]`).
- `Tensor` (tensor product) applies the complete multilevel one-dimensional transform to each axis.
  Level counts may differ by axis; coefficients enumerate every pair of one-dimensional bands, with axis 0 outermost.

`subbands()` returns blocks of packed coefficients as `WaveletSubband` values in storage order.
`offset()` is the block's starting offset, `shape()` its shape `[rows, columns]`, and `detail_levels()` its detail levels for axes 0 and 1 (1 is finest, and 0 denotes low-pass on that axis).
Each block is stored contiguously in row-major order.
`coefficient_len()` returns the total coefficient count, `input_shape()` the matrix shape, and `layout()` the selected layout.
The result agrees bit for bit with successively applying one-dimensional transforms to rows and columns and is independent of execution policy.

## Transforms along an array axis

`WaveletPlan::analyze_axis_into(input, axis, output, ws, cx)` and `synthesize_axis_into` transform all lanes (one-dimensional sequences parallel to an axis) along one axis of a multidimensional array using the same plan.
The output shape replaces that axis's input length with `coefficient_len()` ($n$ for synthesis); input and output may be strided views.
Allocate workspace with `workspace(lanes)`, passing the product of the other axis lengths as `lanes`.
Lanes can be processed in parallel under the execution policy from `WaveletOptions::with_execution_policy` (up to 32 workers); output is written only after every lane succeeds.
An axis index at least the array rank gives `InvalidInput`; an axis length different from $n$ or an incompatible output shape gives `DimensionMismatch`.

The following cell decomposes a $32 \times 32$ image (a rectangle of value 1 plus a one-period cosine $0.25\cos(2\pi j/32)$ along columns) in two dimensions with periodic boundaries.
Inputs are the family, layout, and level count for both axes.
The table shows each block's offset, shape, detail levels, and energy fraction.
Checks compute synthesis error, the ratio of coefficient and image sums of squares, and, for `Tensor` layout, the difference from applying `analyze_axis_into` along axis 1 and then axis 0.
With periodic boundaries and power-of-two side lengths, the blocks exactly fill a grid of the original size.
The chart arranges coefficients in that layout (final approximation at upper left) and uses signed colors for values divided by each block's maximum absolute value.

```rust
//| id: signal-wavelet-2d
//| ref: signal-wavelet-2d
//| title: "Two-dimensional wavelet decomposition of an image containing a rectangle"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-signal]
//| inputs:
//|   family_name: {"type":"select","label":"Family","options":["Haar","Daubechies(4)"],"value":"Haar"}
//|   layout_name: {"type":"select","label":"Layout","options":["Pyramid","Tensor"],"value":"Pyramid"}
//|   levels: {"type":"range","label":"Levels per axis [-]","min":1,"max":3,"step":1,"value":2}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"tensor","label":"Tensor, 2 levels","description":"There are 9 blocks, matching the composition of axis transforms with zero difference.","inputs":{"layout_name":"Tensor"}}
//|   - {"id":"daubechies","label":"Daubechies(4), Pyramid","description":"Detail coefficients at the rectangle edges spread to neighboring coefficients, changing the fine-level detail energy fractions.","inputs":{"family_name":"Daubechies(4)"}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"bands","path":"/rows/6/1","equals":16}
//|       - {"artifact":"bands","path":"/rows/6/4","equals":1}
//|       - {"artifact":"checks","path":"/rows/0/0","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"checks","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":1.0,"atol":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"checks","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":null}
//|   - case: tensor
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"bands","path":"/rows/8/0","equals":768}
//|       - {"artifact":"checks","path":"/rows/0/2","equals":0}
//|   - case: daubechies
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"checks","path":"/rows/0/0","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"checks","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":1.0,"atol":1e-13}}
use scioxide_core::{ExecutionContext, NdView, NdViewMut, StridedLayout};
use scioxide_signal::{Wavelet2dLayout, Wavelet2dPlan, WaveletFamily, WaveletOptions, WaveletPlan};

let side = 32_usize;
let depth = levels.round() as usize;
let family = if family_name == "Haar" {
    WaveletFamily::Haar
} else {
    WaveletFamily::Daubechies(4)
};
let tensor = layout_name == "Tensor";
let layout = if tensor {
    Wavelet2dLayout::Tensor
} else {
    Wavelet2dLayout::Pyramid
};
// Row-major [i][j]. Add a cosine along columns to a rectangle of value 1 in rows 8–19 and columns 10–23.
let image: Vec<f64> = (0..side * side)
    .map(|p| {
        let (i, j) = (p / side, p % side);
        let inside = (8..20).contains(&i) && (10..24).contains(&j);
        let wave = 0.25 * (std::f64::consts::TAU * j as f64 / side as f64).cos();
        if inside { 1.0 + wave } else { wave }
    })
    .collect();
let options = WaveletOptions::new();
let plan = Wavelet2dPlan::new(family, [side, side], [depth, depth], layout, options)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let coefficients = plan.analyze(&image).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let restored = plan.synthesize(&coefficients).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

// Arrange blocks on the original grid. On an axis with detail level l ≥ 1, a block starts at a position equal to its length.
let total: f64 = coefficients.iter().map(|c| c * c).sum();
let (mut mosaic, mut shaded) = (vec![0.0; side * side], vec![0.0; side * side]);
let mut bands = Vec::new();
for band in plan.subbands() {
    let ([rows, cols], [l0, l1]) = (band.shape(), band.detail_levels());
    let block = &coefficients[band.offset()..band.offset() + rows * cols];
    let peak = block.iter().fold(0.0_f64, |acc, c| acc.max(c.abs()));
    let (r0, c0) = (
        if l0 == 0 { 0 } else { rows },
        if l1 == 0 { 0 } else { cols },
    );
    for (k, &value) in block.iter().enumerate() {
        let target = (r0 + k / cols) * side + c0 + k % cols;
        mosaic[target] = value;
        shaded[target] = if peak > 0.0 { value / peak } else { 0.0 };
    }
    let energy: f64 = block.iter().map(|c| c * c).sum();
    bands.push((band.offset(), rows, cols, l0, l1, energy / total));
}

// Check 1: synthesis error and sum-of-squares ratio (1 for orthogonal families with periodic boundaries).
let error = restored
    .iter()
    .zip(&image)
    .fold(0.0_f64, |acc, (y, x)| acc.max((y - x).abs()));
let ratio = total / image.iter().map(|x| x * x).sum::<f64>();
// Check 2: matrix from multilevel one-dimensional transforms along axis 1 and then axis 0 (compare with Tensor layout).
let axis_plan = WaveletPlan::<f64>::new(family, side, depth, options).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let (square, strides) = ([side, side], [side, 1]);
// Periodic boundaries and power-of-two side lengths make the coefficient matrix row-major with shape side × side as well.
let grid = StridedLayout::new(&square, &strides).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut rows_done = vec![0.0; side * side];
let mut composed = vec![0.0; side * side];
```

## Error and API selection

Each level's coefficients are sums of products at working precision; each component of a $m$-term sum has absolute error at most $\gamma(2m + 8)\,M + (2m + 8)\,\eta$ ($\gamma(k) = ku/(1 - ku)$ for unit roundoff $u$, where $M$ is the sum of absolute products and $\eta$ is the smallest positive subnormal).
Errors propagate between levels through the absolute filter coefficients.
For orthogonal families, the transform is an orthogonal matrix, so synthesis does not amplify error; round-trip error remains around $10^{-15}$ times the signal magnitude, as in the earlier cell.
Biorthogonal families amplify errors according to synthesis filter magnitudes, but built-in families give errors of the same order.

`analyze` and `synthesize` are convenient one-shot forms that allocate output and workspace on each call.
For repeated transforms of equal-length records, prepare a plan and `workspace(1)`, then pass output storage to `analyze_into` and `synthesize_into` for allocation-free execution.
One workspace serves both decomposition and synthesis.
Input length other than $n$, output length other than `coefficient_len()`, or insufficient workspace gives `DimensionMismatch`; non-finite samples give `NonFinite`; overflowing coefficients give `NumericalInstability`.
Results are computed in workspace and then copied to output, leaving output unchanged on error.
`WaveletPlan` accepts real and complex (`NumericScalar`) samples, as does `Wavelet2dPlan`.

## Summary

The discrete wavelet transform repeatedly applies low-pass and high-pass filters and downsampling; under perfect reconstruction conditions, its coefficients reproduce the original signal to within rounding error.
Orthogonal families (`Haar`, `Daubechies`) preserve coefficient sums of squares with periodic boundaries and even lengths; biorthogonal families (`BiorthogonalSpline`) have symmetric filters but do not preserve sums of squares.
Use `wavelet_max_level` as a guide for depth and read packed coefficient positions with `levels()` and `detail_offset()`, or `subbands()` in two dimensions.
Select endpoint handling with `SignalBoundary` and allow for more coefficients than record samples when allocating storage for `Zero` and `Reflect`.

See [Wavelet denoising](../../examples/wavelet-denoising/) for shrinking coefficients to remove noise and comparing families.
Compare filter design from the perspective of frequency response in [FIR design and minimum phase](../fir-design-and-minimum-phase/).
