Observed data such as acoustic waveforms, vibrations, bioelectric potentials, and photometric measurements enter a computer as sequences sampled along a time or spatial axis.
Digital signal processing is a system of computations for extracting the desired components from these sequences, suppressing noise, and estimating and correcting the response of transmission channels and measurement systems.

`scioxide-signal` uses prepared transforms from `scioxide-fft` to provide the following operations.

- Linear, circular, and multidimensional convolution and correlation, and streaming convolution of long records
- FIR and IIR filter design and application through second-order sections (SOS), and forward–backward zero-phase filtering
- Rational-ratio polyphase and Fourier resampling
- Window functions and DPSS, periodograms, Welch estimation, cross-spectra and coherence, and the Lomb–Scargle method for unevenly sampled data
- Short-time Fourier transforms (STFT) and reconstruction with dual windows
- One-dimensional and multidimensional analytic signals, Riesz transforms and monogenic signals, and conversion to minimum phase
- Discrete wavelet transforms (one and two dimensions)
- Savitzky–Golay, Whittaker–Henderson, and spline smoothing, and detrending
- Median, Hampel, and local Wiener filters, peak and cross-scale ridge detection, phase concentration, and test waveform generation
- Continuous-time and discrete-time linear time-invariant (LTI) state-space models

Samples are real or complex `f32` or `f64` values, and every feature has the same API with both the default `std` and `no_std + alloc` configurations.

## A first computation

Extract a 10 Hz component with a low-pass filter from a record containing a 10 Hz sine wave plus 150 Hz noise.
The sampling rate is $f_s = 1000$ Hz, the record lasts 1 second (1000 samples), and the input is

$$
x(t) = \sin(2\pi \cdot 10\, t) + 0.5 \sin(2\pi \cdot 150\, t)
$$


The filter is a Butterworth low-pass filter with cutoff frequency $f_c$, designed with `design_iir` and converted to a cascade of second-order sections with `to_sos()`.
`design_iir` accepts band edges as ratios to the Nyquist frequency $f_s/2$, so convert $f_c$ [Hz] to $f_c / (f_s/2)$ before passing it.

The magnitude response of an order-$N$ Butterworth filter constructed by the bilinear transform has a closed form.
With digital angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f / f_s$, it is

$$
\lvert H(e^{i\omega}) \rvert = \left[ 1 + \left( \frac{\tan(\omega/2)}{\tan(\omega_c/2)} \right)^{2N} \right]^{-1/2}
$$

(see [IIR filter design](algorithms/iir-design/) for the derivation).
Causal filtering with `filter` multiplies the amplitude by $\lvert H \rvert$ and shifts the phase by $\arg H$ (a delay in the low-pass passband).
Forward–backward filtering with `forward_backward` passes the signal through the same filter in both directions, canceling the phase delay and multiplying the amplitude by $\lvert H \rvert^2$.

The next cell compares the designed filter's frequency response `response` with the closed-form magnitude, and compares the outputs of the two filtering methods with ideal sine waves.
It compares the causal output with $\lvert H \rvert \sin(2\pi f t + \arg H)$ over the last 0.5 seconds, after the transient has decayed, and the zero-phase output with $\lvert H \rvert^2 \sin(2\pi f t)$ over the middle 0.5 seconds, excluding the endpoint transients.
The inputs are the filter order and cutoff frequency.

```rust
//| id: signal-first-lowpass
//| ref: signal-first-lowpass
//| title: "Extract a 10 Hz component with a Butterworth low-pass filter"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-signal]
//| inputs:
//|   order: {"type":"range","label":"Filter order N","description":"Increasing the order steepens the transition band and increases the group delay.","min":2,"max":8,"step":1,"value":4}
//|   cutoff: {"type":"range","label":"Cutoff frequency f_c [Hz]","description":"The frequency at which the magnitude is 1/√2.","min":20,"max":100,"step":5,"value":40}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"order-8","label":"Order 8, f_c = 40 Hz","description":"The remaining 150 Hz component decreases further, and the group delay at 10 Hz increases.","inputs":{"order":8}}
//|   - {"id":"low-cutoff","label":"Order 4, f_c = 20 Hz","description":"The 10 Hz component approaches the cutoff frequency, and the passband magnitude falls below 1.","inputs":{"cutoff":20}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"gains","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"gains","path":"/rows/1/3","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"outputs","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"max":1e-3}}
//|       - {"artifact":"outputs","path":"/rows/1/1","range":{"max":1e-3}}
//|   - case: order-8
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"gains","path":"/rows/1/3","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|   - case: low-cutoff
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"gains","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"max":1e-13}}
use scioxide_signal::{IirBand, IirDomain, IirFamily, design_iir};

let rate = 1000.0_f64;
let n = 1000_usize;
let (tone, noise) = (10.0_f64, 150.0_f64);
let order = order.round() as usize;
let nyquist = rate / 2.0;

// Design: pass band edges as ratios to the Nyquist frequency
let sos = design_iir(
    order,
    IirFamily::Butterworth,
    IirBand::Lowpass(cutoff / nyquist),
    IirDomain::Digital,
)
.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?
.to_sos()
.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

let tau = std::f64::consts::TAU;
let times: Vec<f64> = (0..n).map(|i| i as f64 / rate).collect();
let input: Vec<f64> = times
    .iter()
    .map(|&t| (tau * tone * t).sin() + 0.5 * (tau * noise * t).sin())
    .collect();
let causal = sos.filter(&input).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let zero_phase = sos.forward_backward(&input).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

// Independent check: closed-form magnitude of a bilinear-transformed Butterworth filter
let warp = |f: f64| (std::f64::consts::PI * f / rate).tan();
let closed_form = |f: f64| (1.0 + (warp(f) / warp(cutoff)).powi(2 * order as i32)).powf(-0.5);
let mut gains = Vec::new();
let mut response = Vec::new();
for f in [tone, noise] {
    let h = sos.response(f / nyquist).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let delay = sos.group_delay(f / nyquist).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let magnitude = h.re.hypot(h.im);
    gains.push((
        f,
        magnitude,
        closed_form(f),
        (magnitude - closed_form(f)).abs(),
        1000.0 * delay / rate,
    ));
    response.push((magnitude, h.im.atan2(h.re)));
}

// Ideal output: |H| sin(2πft + arg H) for causal filtering, |H|² sin(2πft) for zero-phase filtering
let ideal = |t: f64, causal_form: bool| {
    [(tone, 1.0), (noise, 0.5)]
        .iter()
        .zip(&response)
        .map(|(&(f, a), &(g, phase))| {
            if causal_form {
                a * g * (tau * f * t + phase).sin()
            } else {
                a * g * g * (tau * f * t).sin()
            }
        })
        .sum::<f64>()
};
let max_gap = |output: &[f64], range: std::ops::Range<usize>, causal_form: bool| {
    range
        .map(|i| (output[i] - ideal(times[i], causal_form)).abs())
        .fold(0.0_f64, f64::max)
};
let outputs = vec![
    ("Causal filter (0.5–1 s)", max_gap(&causal, 500..n, true)),
    (
        "Zero-phase forward_backward (0.25–0.75 s)",
        max_gap(&zero_phase, 250..750, false),
    ),
];

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let fixed4 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 4 });
let fixed2 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 2 });
let gain_columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Frequency [Hz]", "type": "number" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "|H| from response [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed4 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Closed-form |H| [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed4 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Difference [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Group delay [ms]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    gain_columns,
    gains,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "gains" })
);
let output_columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Output (comparison interval)", "type": "string" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Maximum difference from the ideal sine wave [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    output_columns,
    outputs,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "outputs" })
);

let series = |name: &str, values: &[f64]| {
    let points: Vec<[f64; 2]> = times.iter().zip(values).map(|(&t, &y)| [t, y]).collect();
    serde_json::json!({ "name": name, "points": points })
};
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![
        series("Input x", &input),
        series("Causal filter", &causal),
        series("Zero-phase forward_backward", &zero_phase),
    ],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Time t [s]",
        "yLabel": "Amplitude [-]",
        "yMin": -1.6,
        "yMax": 1.6
    })
);
```

With the default order 4 and $f_c = 40$ Hz, the magnitude from `response` is $0.99999$ at 10 Hz and $3.78 \times 10^{-3}$ at 150 Hz, differing from the closed form by at most $2.2 \times 10^{-15}$.
The 150 Hz component decreases to about $1/265$ in the causal output and, because of $\lvert H \rvert^2$, to about $1/70000$ in the zero-phase output.
The causal output agrees with the ideal sine wave to within $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ over the last 0.5 seconds, and the 10 Hz component lags the input by the 10.6 ms group delay.
The zero-phase output agrees to within $4.6 \times 10^{-12}$ over the middle 0.5 seconds, and its peaks occur at the same times as the input's 10 Hz peaks in the plot.

Increasing the order to 8 reduces the 150 Hz magnitude to $1.43 \times 10^{-5}$, but increases the group delay at 10 Hz to 20.8 ms; the zero-phase output's transients also persist longer, giving a difference of $2.8 \times 10^{-7}$ in the middle.
Lowering the cutoff frequency to 20 Hz brings 10 Hz closer to the transition band and reduces its magnitude to $0.998$.
For every input, the designed magnitude differs from the closed form by at most $5 \times 10^{-15}$.

## Reading the results

This computation includes three conventions that recur throughout the chapter.

- Frequency units: digital filter design, `response`, and `group_delay` accept ratios to the Nyquist frequency.
  Welch estimation and STFT use cycles per unit time, while the Lomb–Scargle method and continuous-time systems use angular frequency, so check the units for each API ([frequency units](oxref:signal-frequency-units)).
- Delay and phase: a causal filter changes the phase at each frequency; in this low-pass filter, the 10 Hz component is delayed by the group delay.
  Use forward–backward filtering to preserve waveform timing, but its magnitude is squared, and it is a batch operation that requires the entire record.
- Endpoint transients: for finite records, the filter's initial state and the extension beyond the record determine the output near its endpoints.
  Even if the middle agrees with the ideal value, treat the few samples at each endpoint separately ([extension beyond the record](oxref:signal-boundaries)).

For a one-off computation, begin with a one-shot function or a standard representation (`design_iir`, `filter`, or `forward_backward`), as in this example.
When repeating the same operation on many records or chunks, move to a form in which the caller retains a plan or method object (such as `IirFilter`) and workspace ([workspace and execution contexts](oxref:signal-workspaces)).

## Summary

`scioxide-signal` handles uniformly sampled convolution, filter design and application, resampling, spectral and time–frequency analysis, analytic signals, wavelets, smoothing, feature extraction, and LTI systems in one crate.
Digital filter design accepts band edges as Nyquist ratios, and the design can be checked with `response` and the closed-form magnitude.
Causal filtering shifts phase, while forward–backward filtering cancels phase but squares the magnitude; choose according to the task.
Use [Choosing a method](choosing/) to determine the operation and boundary assumptions for your task, and [Mathematics and algorithms](algorithms/) to examine the derivations and error properties.
