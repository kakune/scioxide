For conjugate gradients (CG) on a symmetric positive definite matrix, the iteration count is approximately proportional to the square root of the condition number $\kappa$.
For finite-difference and finite-element matrices, refining the mesh increases the condition number and therefore the unpreconditioned iteration count.
**Preconditioning** selects a matrix $\mathbf{M}$ approximating $\mathbf{A}$ whose inverse action $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{r}$ is inexpensive, clustering the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}$ to reduce iterations.
Sparse LU and Cholesky factorizations represent $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{A}$ itself, but fill makes their storage and work substantial.
Incomplete factorizations discard some fill according to rules, building $\mathbf{A} \approx \tilde{\mathbf{L}}\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$ while keeping the factors small.
This page explains the two families of dropping rules (structure-based levels and value-based thresholds), pivot breakdown and repair, simple preconditioners, and use of `IncompleteFactor`.

## The role of preconditioning

Instead of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, a preconditioned iterative method solves $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ applied on the left, or $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$ ($\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{y}$) with it applied on the right.
scioxide-linalg GMRES uses right preconditioning, so it minimizes the original residual $\lVert \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} \rVert_2$ and tests convergence using its norm even with preconditioning.
Each iteration computes $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{r}$ once, so applying $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ must cost roughly as much as multiplication by $\mathbf{A}$ to be worthwhile.
For an incomplete factorization $\mathbf{M} = \tilde{\mathbf{L}}\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$, $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{r}$ is computed by two sparse triangular substitutions, with work proportional to the number of factor nonzeros.
The closer $\mathbf{M}$ is to $\mathbf{A}$, the closer $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}$ is to the identity and the fewer iterations are needed, but the larger the factors become.
Choose preconditioning by balancing factor size (factorization and per-application work) against iteration count.
A preconditioner for CG must be Hermitian positive definite.
IC(0) and ICT have the form $\mathbf{M} = \tilde{\mathbf{L}}\tilde{\mathbf{L}}^{\mathsf{H}}$, so they satisfy this condition if factorization completes with positive pivots.

## Level-based dropping: IC(0) and ILU(k)

The update $a_{ij} \leftarrow a_{ij} - a_{ik}a_{kj}/a_{kk}$ at step $k$ of Gaussian elimination creates fill at positions originally equal to zero.
**ILU(0)** discards every update outside the nonzero pattern $\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{A})$ of $\mathbf{A}$, restricting the factor structure to $\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{A})$.
This gives $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\tilde{\mathbf{U}} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{R}$, with the error $\mathbf{R}$ having entries only outside $\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{A})$.
scioxide-sparse also adds unstored diagonal positions to the factor structure as zero-valued entries.
**IC(0)** is the Cholesky counterpart with the same structure for Hermitian positive definite matrices.
scioxide-sparse IC(0) stores only the lower triangle including the diagonal; for a symmetric structure with every diagonal stored, its factor has $(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}) + n)/2$ entries.

**ILU(k)** assigns fill levels and retains only levels at most $k$.
Original nonzeros and diagonals have level 0 and other positions level $\infty$; whenever row $j$ eliminates row $i$, update

$$
\mathrm{lev}(i, m) \leftarrow \min\bigl(\mathrm{lev}(i, m),\ \mathrm{lev}(i, j) + \mathrm{lev}(j, m) + 1\bigr)
$$

according to this rule.
A path in $G(\mathbf{A})$ connecting $i$ and $m$ with all intermediate vertex indices less than $\min(i, m)$ is called a [fill path](oxref:sparse-elimination-graph-fill) between $i$ and $m$.
The level at position $(i, m)$ equals the shortest fill-path edge count minus 1 (Hysom and Pothen, 2001).
Levels are combinatorial quantities determined solely by structure, independent of values.
Thus the ILU(k) structure depends only on the nonzero pattern of $\mathbf{A}$; increasing $k$ enlarges the factors and brings $\mathbf{M}$ closer to $\mathbf{A}$.
scioxide-sparse computes these levels row by row during numerical factorization rather than determining the structure during symbolic analysis.

If $\mathbf{A}$ is an M-matrix (a nonsingular matrix with nonpositive off-diagonal entries and an entrywise nonnegative inverse, such as a finite-difference Laplacian), incomplete factorization completes with positive pivots for any predetermined set of off-diagonal positions to discard (Meijerink and van der Vorst, 1977).
For general Hermitian positive definite matrices, discarded updates can make an intermediate IC(0) pivot nonpositive and cause breakdown.

## Threshold dropping: ILUT and ICT

Since levels ignore values, they may retain small fill and discard large fill.
**ILUT** drops by magnitude (Saad's dual-threshold method).
scioxide-sparse defines $s_i$ as the largest block Frobenius norm in block row $i$ of the original matrix, including diagonal and upper-triangular blocks, and uses the dropping threshold $\tau s_i$ (where $\tau$ is `with_drop_tolerance`).
With block size 1, $s_i$ is the maximum absolute entry in row $i$.

- A lower-triangular block is discarded before it is used for elimination if its norm before division by the diagonal pivot is less than $\tau s_i$.
  The discarded block is not used in subsequent elimination of that row.
  This differs from Saad's ILUT, which tests the multiplier after division.
- An upper-triangular block is discarded if its norm after row elimination is less than $\tau s_i$.
- Then `with_max_off_diagonal(p)` limits the lower- and upper-triangular blocks retained in each row to at most $p$ each, in descending norm order.
  Lower-triangular multipliers after division and final upper-triangular values are compared; equal norms favor the smaller column index.
  Multipliers discarded by this cap have already been used in row elimination, and their updates are not undone.
  Diagonal blocks are never discarded.

Value-based tests also apply to original entries of $\mathbf{A}$.
Blocks with norm exactly $\tau s_i$ are retained, so $\tau = 0$ disables value-based dropping and retains all fill, including zero-valued blocks.

**ICT** is the Cholesky counterpart of this idea.
A lower-triangular block $l_{ij}$ in row $i$ is updated using blocks already retained in that row, then tested before the triangular solve with diagonal block $l_{jj}^{\mathsf{H}}$ (the counterpart of division in Cholesky).
A retained $l_{ij}$ and $l_{kj}$ computed in earlier rows ($j < k < i$) generate a fill candidate at position $(i, k)$.
The per-row cap $p$ applies only to the lower triangle, and the diagonal is recomputed using only lower-triangular blocks retained in that row.

For every method, symbolic analysis stores only the nonzero pattern of $\mathbf{A}$; fill positions are determined during numerical factorization.
Factor size is bounded by `max_factor_blocks` (the combined number of $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$ blocks including diagonals); exceeding it gives `ResourceLimit`.

Without the per-row cap $p$, discarded blocks never participate in later elimination, so ILUT and ICT factors match incomplete factorizations with the discarded position set specified in advance.
Thus for M-matrices, the preceding Meijerink–van der Vorst result also applies to threshold dropping, and factorization completes with positive pivots.

## Pivot breakdown and repair

Discarded updates in incomplete factorization can produce zero or negative pivots even when $\mathbf{A}$ is nonsingular.
For block size $b > 1$, pivots mean the scalar pivots arising in dense factorization of a diagonal block.
Select their treatment with `IncompletePivotPolicy`.

- `Strict` (the default) rejects exactly zero LU pivots and nonpositive Cholesky pivots (diagonal values before taking square roots) with `Singular`.
  Negative LU pivots are accepted.
- `Repair { absolute, relative }`, with $t = \max(\text{absolute},\ \text{relative} \cdot s_i)$, replaces pivots smaller in magnitude than $t$ and pivots rejected by `Strict` with pivots of magnitude $t$.
  Nonzero LU pivots retain their sign (phase for complex values); zero LU pivots and Cholesky pivots become $t$.
  Both lower bounds must be finite and nonnegative, with at least one positive; otherwise `InvalidInput` is returned before numerical computation.
  Read the number of repaired pivots from the diagnostic `repaired_pivots()`.

Repair changes only the preconditioner, leaving the input matrix unchanged.
Repair allows factorization to complete but does not ensure that the repaired preconditioner is effective.
If many pivots are repaired, adjust $\tau$ or the level, or consider another preconditioner.

## Block variants

Setting $b > 1$ in `block_size` of `IncompleteSymbolic::analyze(pattern, method, block_size, limits)` partitions the matrix into $b \times b$ blocks and applies the same rules at block level.
A block containing any stored entry is treated as dense, with unstored positions set to zero.
Block magnitudes are measured by the Frobenius norm.
Diagonal blocks use dense LU with partial pivoting for LU methods and dense Cholesky for Cholesky methods.
For systems with $b$ degrees of freedom per node, coupling within a node is treated exactly inside each block, often making this more stable than scalar incomplete factorization.
$b$ must divide the dimension.
BSR matrices are analyzed by their scalar-entry structure.

`blocks()` returns stored blocks as (block row, block column, row-major values), ordered by row and column.
For LU methods, off-diagonal blocks belong to the unit lower-triangular factor $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}$ and the upper-triangular factor $\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$, while each diagonal block contains its own dense LU factors.
`diagonal_pivots(row)` returns the diagonal block's row permutation in that block row (empty for Cholesky methods).

## Jacobi and identity preconditioners

The simplest preconditioner is the diagonal-only Jacobi preconditioner $\mathbf{M} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{A})$.
`JacobiPreconditioner::new(matrix)` constructs reciprocals from the stored diagonal of a square CSR matrix.
It returns `DimensionMismatch` for a nonsquare matrix, `Singular` for a missing or zero diagonal, and `NumericalInstability` for reciprocal overflow.
Construction finds each row's diagonal by binary search and costs $O(n + \sum_i \log \mathrm{nnz}_i)$; application costs $O(n)$.
`inverse_diagonal()` returns the reciprocal array.
Jacobi alone can greatly reduce iterations when diagonal scales vary widely by row, but it does not change condition-number growth under mesh refinement.
`IdentityPreconditioner::new(n)` is $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{I}$, used to express unpreconditioned iteration through the same calling form that accepts a preconditioner.

Every preconditioner implements scioxide-core's `Preconditioner` and can be passed to scioxide-linalg iterative methods as `Preconditioning::Inverse(&preconditioner)`.
The standard form `apply(rhs)` of `JacobiPreconditioner` and `IdentityPreconditioner` returns the action in a new array; the borrowed form `apply_into(rhs, options, output, workspace, scope)` accepts a root or enclosing-solver context and writes it into `output` ([calling forms](oxref:sparse-execution-forms)).
For `IncompleteFactor`, `solve(rhs)` and `solve_into(rhs, operation, options, output, workspace, scope)` serve those roles, and `solve_transpose` and `solve_adjoint` also apply $\mathbf{M}^{-\mathsf{T}}$ and $\mathbf{M}^{-\mathsf{H}}$.

## Symbolic analysis, factorization, and workspaces

1. `IncompleteSymbolic::analyze(pattern, method, block_size, limits)` analyzes a square structure.
   `method` is `IncompleteMethod::Ilu { level }`, `Ilut`, `Ic0`, or `Ict`; `Ilu { level: usize::MAX }` or a zero or nondividing $b$ returns `InvalidInput`, and a nonsquare structure returns `DimensionMismatch`.
2. `IncompleteFactor::factor(symbolic, matrix, options)` validates `IncompleteOptions` (dropping and repair) once, allocates workspace, and performs the first numerical factorization.
   The scoped form `factor_scoped(symbolic, matrix, options, solve_options, scope)` performs the same factorization, accepting `SparseSolveOptions` and a root or enclosing-solver context.
   IC(0) and ICT return `InvalidInput` unless the matrix is exactly Hermitian.
   The one-shot constructors `IncompleteFactor::ilu0(&a)` and `ic0(&a)` perform symbolic analysis and factorization for ILU(0) and IC(0) with structure-determined limits ($\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}) + n$ blocks, no byte limit) and `IncompleteOptions::new()`.
   `IncompleteOptions::new()` specifies no dropping ($\tau = 0$), no per-row cap, and `Strict`.
   `diagnostics()` returns `IncompleteDiagnostics` (`factor_blocks()`, `dropped_blocks()`, `repaired_pivots()`) for the last successful factorization.
   These are observations about the factors, not measures of preconditioner accuracy.
3. `refactor(matrix, options, workspace, scope)` refactorizes new values with matching structure without allocation.
   If the workspace has not been reserved to the size required by this analysis, it returns `DimensionMismatch`.
   Any failure preserves the previous factors and diagnostics.
   Create workspace `IncompleteWorkspace` with the analysis's `workspace(())`, or reserve it with `try_reserve` for `IncompleteRequirements` (`symbolic_bytes()`, `factor_bytes()`, `workspace_bytes()`, `total_bytes()`) returned by `workspace_requirements(())`.
   This workspace is only for refactorization; prepare the shared `SparseSolveWorkspace` with `factor.workspace(rhs)` for preconditioner application and solves.

The required limits `IncompleteLimits::new(max_factor_blocks, max_workspace_bytes)` bound stored block count and logical bytes across analysis, factors, and workspace.
When `SparseSolveOptions::with_budgets` sets an iteration limit for `factor_scoped` or `refactor`, factorization counts one iteration per block row and returns `ConvergenceFailure` at the limit.

### Experimental model

The following two cells compare preconditioners on a five-point finite-difference matrix ($N = k^2$) on a $k \times k$ grid.
The diffusion coefficients are $a_x$ ($x$ direction, neighboring indices $\pm 1$ apart) and $a_y = 1$ ($y$ direction, indices $\pm k$ apart); the diagonal is $2(a_x + a_y)$ and neighboring entries are $-a_x$ and $-a_y$.
For every $a_x > 0$, $\mathbf{A}$ is an M-matrix, and these cells omit the per-row cap $p$, so every incomplete factorization completes with positive pivots.
Since $\mathbf{M} = \tilde{\mathbf{L}}\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$ for LU methods is generally not Hermitian, all preconditioners use GMRES (restart 50, iteration limit 1000), which accepts every preconditioner, solving to $\lVert \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} \rVert_2 \le 10^{-8}\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert_2$.
For an exact solution with grid symmetry such as $\mathbf{x}^\ast = \mathbf{1}$, the right-hand side contains only symmetric eigenvectors, so unpreconditioned GMRES may finish in fewer iterations than for a general right-hand side.
Instead, construct the right-hand side $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^\ast$ from the nonsymmetric exact solution $x^\ast_p = \sin(p + 1)$.
Compare factor sizes using the number of nonzeros in $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$ used per application divided by $\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})$.
Cholesky methods store only $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}$ and share the diagonal with $\tilde{\mathbf{U}} = \tilde{\mathbf{L}}^{\mathsf{H}}$, so the nonzero count is $2 \times (\text{stored blocks}) - N$.

The following block defines what the two cells share.
`diffusion` builds the matrix from five diagonals with `sparse_diagonals`, dropping the $x$-direction couplings across grid-row boundaries as explicit zeros.
`gmres` solves GMRES with the right-hand side above and returns the iteration count, termination reason, and maximum difference from the exact solution in `Trial`.
`incomplete` performs symbolic analysis and numerical factorization, returning the factors and the combined nonzero count of $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$.
The factor block limit is $N(2k + 1)$, since naturally ordered five-point fill stays inside an envelope of bandwidth $k$ ([envelope theorem](oxref:sparse-rcm-envelope)).

```rust
//| id: sparse-ilu-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Five-point diffusion matrix, preconditioned GMRES, and incomplete factorization"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-sparse, scioxide-linalg]
use scioxide_core::{Budget, LinearOperator, RealScalar, ResidualTolerance};
use scioxide_linalg::{GmresSolver, LinearSolveTermination, Preconditioning};
use scioxide_sparse::{
    CsrMatrix, ExplicitZeroPolicy, IncompleteFactor, IncompleteLimits, IncompleteMethod,
    IncompleteOptions, IncompleteSymbolic, sparse_diagonals,
};

/// Five-point differences on a k × k grid. Coupling a_x in the x direction (indices ±1), and coupling 1 in the y direction (indices ±k).
pub(crate) fn diffusion(k: usize, a_x: f64) -> Result<CsrMatrix<f64>, String> {
    let n = k * k;
    // Entry p couples vertices p and p + 1. It is 0 at a grid-row boundary (when p + 1 is divisible by k).
    let x: Vec<f64> = (1..n)
        .map(|p| if p % k == 0 { 0.0 } else { -a_x })
        .collect();
    let y = vec![-1.0_f64; n - k];
    let diagonal = vec![2.0 * (a_x + 1.0); n];
    let offsets = [-(k as isize), -1, 0, 1, k as isize];
    let stored = sparse_diagonals(n, n, &offsets, &[&y, &x, &diagonal, &x, &y])
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    stored
        .view()
        .to_csr(ExplicitZeroPolicy::Drop)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())
}

/// Result of one preconditioned GMRES solve.
pub(crate) struct Trial {
    pub(crate) iterations: usize,
    pub(crate) termination: LinearSolveTermination,
    pub(crate) error: f64,
}

/// Solve b = A x* (x*_p = sin(p + 1)) to ‖b − Ax‖₂ ≤ 10⁻⁸‖b‖₂ and measure the maximum difference from the exact solution.
pub(crate) fn gmres(
    a: &CsrMatrix<f64>,
    preconditioning: Preconditioning<'_, f64>,
) -> Result<Trial, String> {
    let exact: Vec<f64> = (0..a.rows())
        .map(|p| RealScalar::sin((p + 1) as f64))
        .collect();
    let mut b = vec![0.0_f64; a.rows()];
    let mut scratch = vec![0.0_f64; a.apply_workspace_len().map_err(|e| e.to_string())?];
    a.apply_into(&exact, &mut b, &mut scratch)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let tolerance = ResidualTolerance::new(0.0, 1e-8).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let limit = Budget::try_from(1000).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let restart = Budget::try_from(50).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let result = GmresSolver::new(tolerance, limit)
        .with_restart(restart)
        .solve_with_options(a, &b[..], preconditioning, None)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let error = result
        .solution()
        .as_slice()
        .iter()
        .zip(&exact)
        .fold(0.0_f64, |m, (x, y)| m.max((x - y).abs()));
    Ok(Trial {
        iterations: result.report().iterations(),
        termination: result.report().termination(),
        error,
    })
}

/// Incomplete factorization of a k × k grid matrix a with threshold tau and the number of L̃ and Ũ nonzeros used per application.
pub(crate) fn incomplete(
    a: &CsrMatrix<f64>,
    k: usize,
    method: IncompleteMethod,
    tau: f64,
) -> Result<(IncompleteFactor<f64>, usize), String> {
    let n = a.rows();
    // Under natural ordering, fill remains within bandwidth-k envelope, so the factors have at most N(2k + 1) blocks.
    let limits = IncompleteLimits::new(n * (2 * k + 1), 1 << 24);
    let symbolic = IncompleteSymbolic::analyze(a.structural_pattern(), method, 1, limits)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let options = IncompleteOptions::new().with_drop_tolerance(tau);
    let factor =
        IncompleteFactor::factor(symbolic, a.view(), options).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let stored = factor.diagnostics().factor_blocks();
    // Cholesky methods store only L̃ and share its diagonal with L̃ᴴ.
    let cholesky = matches!(method, IncompleteMethod::Ic0 | IncompleteMethod::Ict);
    Ok((factor, if cholesky { 2 * stored - n } else { stored }))
}
```

```rust
    { "key": "4", "label": "Iterations [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Termination reason" },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Maximum difference from exact solution [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "preconditioners" })
);
// Outcome: use the termination reason of the first row that failed to satisfy the tolerance.
let failure = trials
    .iter()
    .map(|t| t.termination)
    .find(|t| !t.is_success());
let outcome = match failure {
    None => Outcome::satisfied("GMRES satisfied the tolerance for all eight preconditioners"),
    Some(LinearSolveTermination::Breakdown(_)) => {
        Outcome::failed("GMRES broke down for a preconditioner")
    }
    Some(_) => Outcome::partial("GMRES stopped before satisfying the tolerance for a preconditioner"),
};
let most = trials.iter().map(|t| t.iterations).max().unwrap_or(0);
let error = trials.iter().fold(0.0_f64, |m, t| m.max(t.error));
emit_outcome!(
    outcome
        .status(failure.map_or("Converged".to_string(), |t| format!("{t:?}")))
        .diagnostic("Maximum iteration count", most as f64, None)
        .diagnostic("Maximum difference from exact solution", error, None)
);
```

At the defaults $k = 16$ ($N = 256$) and $\tau = 10^{-2}$, unpreconditioned GMRES converges in 44 iterations.
Jacobi also takes 44 iterations: all diagonal entries are 4, so Jacobi simply scales the entire matrix by a constant and leaves iteration unchanged.
IC(0) and ILU(0) both take 15 iterations.
For a symmetric matrix, both represent the same $\mathbf{M}$; IC(0) stores only 736 lower-triangular entries, but both use 1.00 times the $\mathbf{A}$ nonzeros per application.
ILU(1) uses a ratio of 1.37 and 10 iterations; ILU(2), 1.72 and 8 iterations: raising the level enlarges the factors and reduces iterations.
ICT discards 405 blocks and produces factors with the same per-application nonzero ratio of 1.72 as ILU(2), converging in the same 8 iterations.
ILUT discards 810 blocks, exactly twice as many as ICT, and retains the same 2086 blocks as ILU(2).
ILU(2), ICT, and ILUT all take 8 iterations, and their differences from the exact solution agree to the displayed digits.
For the isotropic Laplacian, threshold $10^{-2}$ gives factors of the same size as level 2.
Every maximum difference from the exact solution is on the order of $10^{-7}$.
This lies within the bound $\lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^\ast \rVert_2 \le 10^{-8}\,\kappa(\mathbf{A})\,\lVert \mathbf{x}^\ast \rVert_2$ determined by the relative residual tolerance $10^{-8}$ and condition number $\kappa(\mathbf{A}) = \cot^2(\pi/(2(k+1))) \approx 116$.

At $\tau = 10^{-1}$, ICT drops all fill, retains the same 736 blocks as IC(0), and returns to 15 iterations.
Under refinement, unpreconditioned iterations increase to 25, 44, and 55 at $k = 8, 16, 24$, and even IC(0) takes 11, 15, and 20.
Incomplete factorization reduces iteration counts but does not eliminate grid-size dependence.

### Thresholds and levels for anisotropic diffusion

Since levels ignore values, problems with strongly direction-dependent coupling retain small fill from weak couplings just like fill from strong couplings.
The following cell fixes $k = 16$ and selects the $x$-direction coupling $a_x$ to compare level-based ILU(k) ($k = 0, 1, 2, 3$) with threshold-based ICT ($\tau = 10^{-1}, \dots, 10^{-4}$).
The chart plots the $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{U}}$ nonzero ratio horizontally and GMRES iterations vertically, showing the factor-size/iteration tradeoff for the two rules.
Points farther toward the lower left achieve fewer iterations with less storage and work.

```rust
//| id: sparse-ilu-anisotropic
//| ref: sparse-ilu-anisotropic
//| title: "Threshold and level factor sizes and iterations for anisotropic diffusion"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [sparse-ilu-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-sparse, scioxide-linalg]
//| inputs:
//|   coupling: {"type":"select","label":"x-direction coupling a_x","description":"The y-direction coupling is a_y = 1.","options":[{"label":"a_x = 1 (isotropic)","value":"1"},{"label":"a_x = 0.1","value":"0.1"},{"label":"a_x = 0.01","value":"0.01"},{"label":"a_x = 0.001","value":"0.001"}],"value":"0.01"}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"isotropic","label":"a_x = 1 (isotropic)","description":"When coupling strengths are uniform, threshold methods offer no advantage over level methods.","inputs":{"coupling":"1"}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/0/3","equals":6}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":1.0,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/1/3","equals":3}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/5/1","approx":{"value":0.6052631579,"rtol":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/5/3","equals":6}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/6/1","approx":{"value":1.3700657895,"rtol":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/6/3","equals":3}
//|   - case: isotropic
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/3/3","equals":7}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/6/1","approx":{"value":4.1447368421,"rtol":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"tradeoff","path":"/rows/6/3","equals":4}
use scioxide_linalg::Preconditioning;
use scioxide_sparse::IncompleteMethod;

let a_x: f64 = coupling.parse().map_err(|_| "Cannot parse a_x as a number")?;
let k = 16;
let a = diffusion(k, a_x)?;
let nnz = a.nnz() as f64;
let mut rows = Vec::new();
let (mut levels, mut thresholds) = (Vec::new(), Vec::new());
// Level rule: ILU(k), k = 0, 1, 2, 3 (retained positions depend on structure, not values).
for level in 0..4 {
    let (factor, entries) = incomplete(&a, k, IncompleteMethod::Ilu { level }, 0.0)?;
    let trial = gmres(&a, Preconditioning::Inverse(&factor))?;
    let ratio = entries as f64 / nnz;
    let dropped = factor.diagnostics().dropped_blocks();
    let status = format!("{:?}", trial.termination);
    rows.push((
        format!("ILU({level})"),
        ratio,
        dropped,
        trial.iterations,
        status,
    ));
    levels.push([ratio, trial.iterations as f64]);
}
// Threshold rule: ICT, τ = 10⁻¹, …, 10⁻⁴ (discard blocks below threshold τ s_i).
for (q, power) in [(1, "¹"), (2, "²"), (3, "³"), (4, "⁴")] {
    let (factor, entries) = incomplete(&a, k, IncompleteMethod::Ict, 10.0_f64.powi(-q))?;
    let trial = gmres(&a, Preconditioning::Inverse(&factor))?;
    let ratio = entries as f64 / nnz;
    let dropped = factor.diagnostics().dropped_blocks();
    let status = format!("{:?}", trial.termination);
    rows.push((
        format!("ICT(10⁻{power})"),
        ratio,
        dropped,
        trial.iterations,
        status,
    ));
    thresholds.push([ratio, trial.iterations as f64]);
}
let fixed2 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Factor" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "L̃ and Ũ nonzeros / nnz(A) [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Discarded blocks [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Iterations [count]", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Termination reason" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "tradeoff" })
);
let series = vec![
    serde_json::json!({ "name": "Level ILU(k), k = 0–3", "points": levels, "style": { "showSymbol": true } }),
    serde_json::json!({ "name": "Threshold ICT, τ = 10⁻¹–10⁻⁴", "points": thresholds, "style": { "showSymbol": true } }),
];
emit_line_chart!(
    &series,
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "L̃ and Ũ nonzeros / nnz(A) [-]",
        "yLabel": "GMRES iterations [count]"
    })
```

At the default $a_x = 0.01$, ILU(0) uses the same 1.00 times $\mathbf{A}$ nonzeros per application and takes 6 iterations; ILU(1)–ILU(3) use ratios of 1.37–2.38 and all take 3 iterations.
ICT produces factors with ratio 0.61 at $\tau = 10^{-1}$ and $10^{-2}$ and converges in 6 iterations, matching ILU(0) with ratio 1.00.
The $x$-direction neighbor magnitude 0.01 lies below threshold $\tau s_i \ge 0.0202$ at $\tau \ge 10^{-2}$ since $s_i = 2.02$, so ICT also discards the original weak couplings in $\mathbf{A}$.
At $\tau = 10^{-3}$, it takes 3 iterations with ratio 1.37, matching ILU(1).
On the chart, the six-iteration threshold points lie left of the level point, while the smallest factors achieving three iterations have ratio 1.37 under either rule.

For isotropic $a_x = 1$, iteration counts agree at equal factor sizes (15 at 1.00 and 8 at 1.72), and threshold points never lie below and left of level points.
If the threshold is too large, such as $\tau = 10^{-1}$ at $a_x = 0.1$, ICT takes 14 iterations with ratio-0.61 factors, exceeding ILU(0)'s 11.
Threshold methods inspect values and can shrink factors for uneven coupling strengths, but $\tau$ must be chosen for the problem.

## Summary

Incomplete factorization gives factors $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\tilde{\mathbf{U}} \approx \mathbf{A}$ with fill discarded by rules, and applies the preconditioner through two triangular substitutions.
IC(0) and ILU(k) choose retained positions solely by structure; raising level $k$ enlarges factors in exchange for fewer iterations.
ILUT and ICT drop by magnitude, using threshold $\tau$ and per-row cap $p$ to control factor size and approximation; for anisotropic problems, dropping weak couplings can shrink factors.
When comparing factor sizes, account for Cholesky methods storing only $\tilde{\mathbf{L}}$ and compare the nonzeros used per application.
If discarded updates cause pivot breakdown, `IncompletePivotPolicy::Repair` can repair pivots to continue factorization; inspect repair counts in the diagnostics as an indication of preconditioner quality.
Refinement increases iterations even with incomplete factorization; use multigrid preconditioning for elliptic problems requiring grid-independent convergence.
