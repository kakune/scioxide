Separating variables in wave or diffusion equations on cylindrically symmetric domains, such as vibrating circular membranes, circular waveguides, and heat conduction in cylinders, produces a second-order linear ODE in the radial direction whose solutions are not elementary functions.
This page covers its solutions: the cylindrical Bessel functions $J_\nu$, $Y_\nu$, $I_\nu$, and $K_\nu$ and Hankel functions $H_\nu^{(1)}$ and $H_\nu^{(2)}$.
After definitions and properties, it explains stable directions of the three-term recurrence in order, methods used by `scioxide-special` in different argument and order regions, scaled and logarithmic forms, derivatives, accuracy guarantees, and the API.
Airy functions used at large-order turning points are covered in [Airy functions and turning points](../airy-functions/), half-integer-order spherical Bessel functions in [Spherical Bessel, spherical Hankel, and Riccati–Bessel functions](../spherical-bessel-kelvin-and-riccati/), and Kelvin functions, zero-order Bessel functions in the $e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}$ direction, in [Kelvin functions and the skin effect](../kelvin-functions/).
Bessel integrals such as `bessel_j0_integral` are covered in [Struve functions and integrals of Bessel and Struve functions](../struve-parabolic-and-bessel-integrals/).

## Bessel's equation and solution classification

Separating a solution of the Helmholtz equation $\nabla^2\psi + k^2\psi = 0$ in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ as $\psi = R(r)\,e^{\mathrm{i}\nu\theta}e^{\mathrm{i}k_z z}$ makes $R$, viewed as a function of $x = k_r r$ ($k_r^2 = k^2 - k_z^2$), satisfy Bessel's differential equation (DLMF 10.2.1).

$$
x^2 y'' + x y' + (x^2 - \nu^2)\,y = 0.
$$

$\nu$ is called the order, and $x$ the argument.

### Fundamental solutions and physical roles

The Bessel function of the first kind $J_\nu$ is regular at the origin and has the defining series (DLMF 10.2.2).

$$
J_\nu(z) = \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-z^2/4)^k}{k!\,\Gamma(\nu + k + 1)}.
$$

The Bessel function of the second kind $Y_\nu$ is defined by $Y_\nu = (J_\nu\cos\nu\pi - J_{-\nu})/\sin\nu\pi$ (DLMF 10.2.3, using the limit for integer orders) and diverges as $x \to 0$ ($Y_0(x) \sim \frac{2}{\pi}\ln x$).
Solutions on domains containing the origin (circular membranes and circular waveguides) can be expressed using $J_\nu$ alone, while annular domains excluding the origin also require $Y_\nu$.

The modified Bessel functions $I_\nu$ and $K_\nu$ solve $x^2 y'' + x y' - (x^2 + \nu^2)\,y = 0$ and occur in the diffusion equation and cylindrical electrostatic and magnetostatic problems.

$$
I_\nu(z) = \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z^2/4)^k}{k!\,\Gamma(\nu + k + 1)}, \qquad
K_\nu(z) = \frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{I_{-\nu}(z) - I_\nu(z)}{\sin\nu\pi}.
$$

$I_\nu$ is regular at the origin and grows like $e^{x}/\sqrt{2\pi x}$, while $K_\nu$ decays like $\sqrt{\pi/(2x)}\,e^{-x}$ (DLMF 10.40.1–2).
The identity $K_{-\nu} = K_\nu$ holds.

Hankel functions are linear combinations representing traveling waves (DLMF 10.4.3).

$$
H_\nu^{(1)}(z) = J_\nu(z) + \mathrm{i}Y_\nu(z), \qquad H_\nu^{(2)}(z) = J_\nu(z) - \mathrm{i}Y_\nu(z), \qquad
H_\nu^{(1)}(x) \sim \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}(x - \nu\pi/2 - \pi/4)}.
$$

With time factor $e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}$, $H_\nu^{(1)}(kr)$ is an outgoing cylindrical wave satisfying the Sommerfeld radiation condition.
`hankel_1` and `hankel_2` return complex values `Complex<T::Real>` even for real inputs; for real $x > 0$, the real part equals `bessel_j` and the imaginary part equals $\pm$`bessel_y`.

The following Wronskians (DLMF 10.5.2, 10.5.5, 10.28.2) can be used for numerical checks.

$$
\mathscr{W}\{J_\nu, Y_\nu\}(z) = \frac{2}{\pi z}, \qquad
\mathscr{W}\{H_\nu^{(1)}, H_\nu^{(2)}\}(z) = -\frac{4\mathrm{i}}{\pi z}, \qquad
\mathscr{W}\{K_\nu, I_\nu\}(z) = \frac{1}{z}.
$$

Domains for real arguments differ by function.
$J_\nu$ and $I_\nu$ accept $x < 0$ only for integer orders, using $J_n(-x) = (-1)^n J_n(x)$ and $I_n(-x) = (-1)^n I_n(x)$; noninteger orders return `DomainError` (`OutsideDomain`).
For negative noninteger orders, $J_\nu$ and $I_\nu$ diverge at $x = 0$ and return `DomainError` (`Pole`).
$Y_\nu$ and Hankel functions accept only $x > 0$; $x = 0$ gives `DomainError` (`Pole`), and $x < 0$ gives `DomainError` (`OutsideDomain`).
$K_\nu$ accepts $x \ge 0$ and returns the limiting value $+\infty$ at $x = 0$.
Complex values use the principal branch with a cut on the negative real axis; on the cut, the sign of the zero imaginary part selects the upper or lower side.

## Recurrence direction and stability

Bessel functions satisfy three-term recurrences between adjacent orders (DLMF 10.6.1, 10.29.1).

$$
C_{\nu-1}(z) + C_{\nu+1}(z) = \frac{2\nu}{z}C_\nu(z) \quad (C = J, Y, H^{(1)}, H^{(2)}), \qquad
I_{\nu-1}(z) - I_{\nu+1}(z) = \frac{2\nu}{z}I_\nu(z).
$$

$K_\nu$ satisfies $K_{\nu+1} = K_{\nu-1} + (2\nu/z)K_\nu$.
For $\nu > x$, $J_\nu$ rapidly decreases with order while $Y_\nu$ rapidly increases.
When one solution becomes arbitrarily small relative to another, the smaller is called the minimal solution and the larger the dominant solution.

Using the recurrence toward increasing order (forward) amplifies dominant-solution contamination in the initial roundoff error by a factor $Y_\nu/J_\nu$, so the minimal solution $J_\nu$ cannot be computed stably forward.
Toward decreasing order (backward), the minimal solution becomes dominant, so $J_\nu$ and $I_\nu$ are stable backward, and $Y_\nu$ and $K_\nu$ forward.
In the oscillatory region $\nu < x$, both solutions have comparable magnitudes, so neither direction amplifies errors.
The [Error growth by recurrence direction](#error-growth-by-recurrence-direction) cell below demonstrates this difference numerically.

### Miller backward recurrence

Miller's method sets $f_{M+1} = 0$ and $f_M = 1$ (an arbitrary small value) at an order $M$ sufficiently above the largest desired order, runs the recurrence backward, and finally normalizes the sequence using a known relation.
Because the minimal solution dominates backward, $f_n$ approaches a constant multiple of $J_n$ as it moves away from $M$.
Normalization relations include $J_0(x) + 2\sum_{k\ge1}J_{2k}(x) = 1$ (DLMF 10.12.4), closed-form low-order values, and Wronskians.
Work is proportional to $M$, choosing $M$ somewhat larger than both the largest order and the argument.

In `scioxide-special`, real spherical Bessel functions $j_n$ ($n > x$) and $i_n$ and Riccati–Bessel functions $x\,j_n(x)$ ($n > \lvert x\rvert$) use Miller's method normalized by closed-form low-order values.
Cylindrical Bessel functions with complex arguments and orders use Miller's method normalized by a Wronskian at moderate $\lvert z\rvert$.
Cylindrical Bessel functions with real arguments and orders do not use Miller's method.

### Steed's method and Temme series

Real $J_\nu$ and $Y_\nu$ ($x > 2$) are computed with Steed's method (the method of Barnett and colleagues).
Split the order as $\nu = n + u$ ($n$ integer, $\lvert u\rvert \le 1/2$), and first compute the ratio $J_{\nu+1}/J_\nu$ with continued fraction CF1.
This continued fraction selects the same minimal solution as backward recurrence, allowing ratios to be followed backward to $J_u$.
Next, compute $p + \mathrm{i}q = (J_u' + \mathrm{i}Y_u')/(J_u + \mathrm{i}Y_u)$ at $u$ using continued fraction CF2.
CF2 converges rapidly for $x \gtrsim 2$.
The two continued fractions and Wronskian $J_uY_u' - J_u'Y_u = 2/(\pi x)$ determine the magnitudes of $J_u$ and $Y_u$; $Y$ is then advanced to order $\nu$ by forward recurrence.
The backward recurrence is normalized by the CF1 ratio, avoiding the need to start at large order as in Miller's method.

For $x \le 2$, CF2 converges slowly, so $Y_\nu$ and $K_\nu$ use Temme series to compute values at two orders with $\lvert u\rvert \le 1/2$ and then recur forward.
Temme series handle the limit $u \to 0$ within the formula, avoiding apparent singularities from $1/\sin(u\pi)$ at integer orders.
For $K_\nu$, Steed's CF2 at $2 < x < 40$ or Hankel asymptotics at $x \ge 40$ compute $K_u$ and $K_{u+1}$, followed by forward recurrence.

### Real evaluation regions

Cylindrical Bessel functions with real orders and arguments use an `f64` kernel; `f32` also computes in `f64` before rounding.
Only regions involving cancellation (near zeros of $Y$, large series terms, phase reduction, and so on) use two-word arithmetic.

| Function and order | Region | Method |
|---|---|---|
| $J$, $Y$ ($\nu = 0, 1$) | $x \le 8$ | Rational approximations with zero factors extracted (coefficients from Boost.Math) |
| $J$, $Y$ ($\nu = 0, 1$) | $x > 8$ | Amplitude from rational approximations in $8/x$ and a reduced phase |
| $J$, $Y$ ($\nu = 0, 1$) | $8 < x < 256$ and within $1/8$ of a zero | Taylor polynomials centered at each zero |
| $J$, $Y$ | $\nu \ge 1000$ and $\nu > 2\sqrt{x}$ | Uniform asymptotics (Debye expansions away from the turning point $x = \nu$, Olver expansions using [Airy functions](../airy-functions/) near the turning point, DLMF §10.19–10.20) |
| $J$ | $x \le 2$ | Defining series |
| $Y$ | $x \le 2$ | Temme series and forward recurrence |
| $J$, $Y$ | $x \ge 20$ and $\nu^2 \le 4x$ (only when $\lvert 4\nu^2 - 1\rvert \le x/4$ for $x < 32$); for $x \ge 256$, up to $\nu^2 \le 16x$ with two-word summation | Hankel asymptotics (DLMF §10.17, only when terms reach `f64` relative precision) |
| $J$, $Y$ | Other $x > 2$ | Steed's method |
| $I$ ($\lvert\nu\rvert < 32$) | $x > 20$ | Hankel asymptotics (DLMF §10.40, only when accepted) |
| $I$ ($\lvert\nu\rvert < 32$) | Otherwise | Defining series |
| $K$ ($\lvert\nu\rvert < 32$) | $x \le 2$, $2 < x < 40$, $x \ge 40$ | Temme series, Steed's CF2, or Hankel asymptotics, followed by forward recurrence |
| $I$, $K$ | $\lvert\nu\rvert \ge 32$ | Debye uniform asymptotics (DLMF §10.41) |
| Scaled $I_0$ | $[0, 8]$ and $[8, \infty)$ | Chebyshev expansions (Cephes coefficients) |

Negative orders $\nu < -1/2$ of $J$ and $Y$ are reflected to positive orders (DLMF §10.4); negative noninteger orders of $I$ use $I_{-\nu} = I_\nu + (2/\pi)\sin(\nu\pi)K_\nu$ (DLMF 10.27.2).
Hankel asymptotics are accepted only if terms decrease to `f64` relative precision before diverging; otherwise evaluation returns to Steed's method.

For complex arguments and orders, evaluation uses defining and Temme series near the origin, Wronskian-normalized Miller backward recurrence for moderate $\lvert z\rvert$, and Hankel asymptotics for large $\lvert z\rvert$.
If Hankel expansions cannot be accepted for $\lvert\nu\rvert \ge 64$, Debye expansions compute $K$ at two adjacent orders in the right half-plane outside the turning circle with $\lvert\arg z\rvert \le 3\pi/4$; they are accepted only when terms decrease to roundoff level.
Even near the imaginary axis, $I$ and $J$ outside the turning circle are connected from this $K$, avoiding Miller recurrences that grow deeper with $\lvert z\rvert$.
The order phase $e^{\mathrm{i}\pi\nu}$ is reduced accurately and held separately from the argument's exponential scale.

## Scaled forms, logarithmic forms, and derivatives

For exponentially growing or decaying functions, compare scaled forms with exponential factors removed before the values themselves exceed the `f64` range.
Scaling factors are as follows; for real arguments the factors for $J$ and $Y$ are 1.

| Function | Returned value | Cases kept within range |
|---|---|---|
| `bessel_j_scaled`, `bessel_y_scaled` | $e^{-\lvert\operatorname{Im}z\rvert}J_\nu(z)$, $e^{-\lvert\operatorname{Im}z\rvert}Y_\nu(z)$ | Complex $z$ with large imaginary part |
| `bessel_i_scaled` | $e^{-\lvert\operatorname{Re}z\rvert}I_\nu(z)$ | Large $\lvert\operatorname{Re}z\rvert$ |
| `bessel_k_scaled` | $e^{z}K_\nu(z)$ (including phase $e^{\mathrm{i}\operatorname{Im}z}$ for complex arguments) | Large $\operatorname{Re}z$ |
| `hankel_1_scaled`, `hankel_2_scaled` | $e^{-\mathrm{i}z}H_\nu^{(1)}(z)$, $e^{\mathrm{i}z}H_\nu^{(2)}(z)$ | Large negative or positive $\operatorname{Im}z$ |

`bessel_k_log` returns $\ln K_\nu(x)$ for real $\nu$ and $x > 0$, yielding finite values even for small $x$ and large $\lvert\nu\rvert$ where $K_\nu(x)$ overflows, or large $x$ where it underflows.
For $\lvert\nu\rvert \ge 32$, Debye expansions are evaluated directly in logarithmic form; for $x < 10^{-8}$ and $\lvert\nu\rvert \ge 3/2$, the leading term $\ln(\Gamma(\lvert\nu\rvert)/2) - \lvert\nu\rvert\ln(x/2)$ is used.
The logarithmic error from omitted corrections (approximately the relative error in $K_\nu$) is less than $x^2/(4(\lvert\nu\rvert - 1))$.
Unlike `bessel_k`, it has no limiting value at $x = 0$ and returns `DomainError` (`Pole`).

Derivative functions such as `bessel_j_derivative` compute the argument derivative of order $k$ by passing $k$ as `degree`.
They use binomial sums of functions with different orders (DLMF §10.6, §10.29), giving work proportional to $k$.

$$
J_\nu^{(k)}(z) = 2^{-k}\sum_{m=0}^{k}(-1)^m\binom{k}{m}J_{\nu-k+2m}(z), \qquad
I_\nu^{(k)}(z) = 2^{-k}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom{k}{m}I_{\nu-k+2m}(z).
$$

$Y$ and Hankel functions use the same signs as $J$; $K$ uses a sum of the same form as $I$ multiplied by $(-1)^k$.
`bessel_i_scaled_derivative` and `bessel_k_scaled_derivative` return the unscaled function’s derivative of order $k$ multiplied by the scaling factor; the factor itself is not differentiated.
For first derivatives only, at real $x > 0$ and $\nu \ge -1/2$, `derivative()` on results from `bessel_j_with_options` and `bessel_y_with_options` also returns $J_\nu'$ and $Y_\nu'$, sharing adjacent-order computations with the value.

### Normalized J and weighted integrals

`bessel_lambda` returns the Jahnke–Emde Lambda function $\Lambda_\nu(x) = \Gamma(\nu + 1)(x/2)^{-\nu}J_\nu(x)$ for real $\nu \ge 0$ and finite $x$.

$$
\Lambda_\nu(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-x^2/4)^k}{k!\,(\nu + 1)_k}, \qquad \Lambda_\nu(0) = 1.
$$

$(\nu+1)_k$ is the rising factorial (Pochhammer symbol).
It does not form $\Gamma(\nu + 1)$ and $(x/2)^\nu$ separately, so values remain finite even at large $\nu$ where either would overflow.
For $x^2 \le 16(\nu + 1)$, it sums this series; for larger $x$, it multiplies `bessel_j` by a logarithmic gamma factor.

`bessel_weighted_integral` returns $\int_0^1 t^\mu J_\nu(2at)\,dt$ (argument order: `a`, `exponent` ($\mu$), `order` ($\nu$)).
The integral converges at $t = 0$ if $\mu + \nu > -1$; negative integer orders require $\mu + \lvert\nu\rvert > -1$.
For $a^2 \le 4(\nu + 1)$, it sums the termwise-integrated series; for larger $a$, it smooths endpoint $t = 0$ with substitution $t = u^q$, then bisects composite 32-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature until two successive levels agree.
Estimated error comes from series truncation or quadrature differences.
Options use `BesselIntegralOptions`.

## Accuracy guarantees

A unit in the last place (ULP) is the spacing of floating-point numbers at the exact result.
Distinguish ULP targets checked against independently generated high-precision references from error estimates returned by forms with options.

For real orders and arguments, `bessel_j`, `bessel_y`, `bessel_i`, `bessel_k`, `bessel_i_scaled`, `bessel_k_scaled`, `hankel_1`, and `hankel_2` target at most 64 ULP for `f64` and `f32`.
This target is verified over tested ranges, not established for every accepted argument.
Tested ranges for $J$ and $Y$ include orders below 1000 and orders near $\sqrt{x}$ with arguments up to $x = 10^8$.
Near zeros, relative errors are not meaningful, so assessment uses absolute-error limits derived from condition numbers.
Real `bessel_j_scaled` and `bessel_y_scaled` equal `bessel_j` and `bessel_y` and follow the same targets.

There is no uniform ULP contract for complex arguments and orders, `hankel_1_scaled`, `hankel_2_scaled`, derivatives, `bessel_k_log`, `bessel_lambda`, or `bessel_weighted_integral`.
For these, read the estimated error in forms with options (the error in `ValueWithError`, not an enclosure) and use family-specific ranges checked against reference data as guidance.

## API

Every function has a standard form returning only the value, a form with options returning `SpecialFunctionResult`, and a scoped API accepting a solve scope.
Work limits (`Budget`, not accuracy requirements) are supplied through `BesselOptions` and `BesselIntegralOptions`, both defaulting to 65536 units.
Cylindrical Bessel and Hankel functions use `NumericScalar` and accept real (`f32`, `f64`) and complex inputs.
The order `order` is `impl Parameter<T>`; a complex argument also permits a real order.

Error classifications are shared within the family.
Non-finite inputs return `NonFinite`; domain violations and poles return `DomainError` (`OutsideDomain`, `Pole`); unrepresentable values return `NumericalInstability` (`Overflow`); evaluations stopped at work limits return `ConvergenceFailure` (`IterationLimit`).
Derivative functions return `ConvergenceFailure` if `degree` exceeds the remaining work allowance.
`bessel_weighted_integral` returns `DomainError` (`Pole`) for exponents making the integral divergent at the endpoint ($\mu + \nu \le -1$, or $\mu + \lvert\nu\rvert \le -1$ for negative integer orders), and `NumericalInstability` (`Breakdown`) if quadrature subintervals can no longer be bisected.

## Error growth by recurrence direction

The following cell chooses argument $x$ and maximum order $N$, constructs $J_n(x)$ and $Y_n(x)$ for orders $n = 0, \dots, N$ using three recurrences, and compares relative errors against `bessel_j` and `bessel_y`.
Forward $J$ and $Y$ start from the $n = 0, 1$ values returned by `bessel_j` and `bessel_y`.
Miller's $J$ recurs backward from even $M = \max(N, \lceil x\rceil) + 30$ with $f_{M+1} = 0$ and $f_M = 10^{-200}$ and normalizes with $J_0 + 2\sum J_{2k} = 1$.
Because the recurrence itself is the subject, the cell runs it explicitly and uses `bessel_j` and `bessel_y` as reference values.
The table shows the three relative errors at $n = N$ and the maximum relative residual of the Wronskian $J_{n+1}Y_n - J_nY_{n+1} = 2/(\pi x)$ formed from API values.

```rust
//| id: special-bessel-recurrence-direction
//| ref: special-bessel-recurrence-direction
//| title: "J and Y errors by recurrence direction"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-special]
//| inputs:
//|   argument: {"type":"range","label":"Argument x [-]","description":"J is the minimal solution for n > x.","min":1,"max":30,"step":1,"value":10}
//|   max_order: {"type":"range","label":"Maximum order N [-]","description":"Largest order reached by the recurrence.","min":10,"max":60,"step":5,"value":40}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"oscillatory","label":"x = 30, N = 25","description":"All orders lie in the oscillatory region n < x, and forward J retains accuracy.","inputs":{"argument":30,"max_order":25}}
//|   - {"id":"small-argument","label":"x = 1, N = 60","description":"Almost the entire range has n > x, and forward J loses digits within a few orders.","inputs":{"argument":1,"max_order":60}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/0","range":{"min":1e10}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/2","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"max":1e-14}}
//|   - case: oscillatory
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/0","range":{"max":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"max":1e-12}}
//|   - case: small-argument
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/0","range":{"min":1e50}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/1","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"errors","path":"/rows/0/3","range":{"max":1e-14}}
use scioxide_special::{bessel_j, bessel_y};

let x = argument;
let n_max = max_order as usize;
let mut reference_j = Vec::new();
let mut reference_y = Vec::new();
for n in 0..=n_max + 1 {
    reference_j.push(bessel_j(n as f64, x).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?);
    reference_y.push(bessel_y(n as f64, x).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?);
}

// Forward: C_{n+1} = (2n/x) C_n − C_{n−1}. Start from the API values at n = 0, 1.
let forward = |start: [f64; 2]| {
    let mut values = vec![start[0], start[1]];
    for n in 1..n_max {
        values.push(2.0 * n as f64 / x * values[n] - values[n - 1]);
    }
    values
};
let forward_j = forward([reference_j[0], reference_j[1]]);
let forward_y = forward([reference_y[0], reference_y[1]]);

// Miller: recur backward from even M and normalize with J_0 + 2 Σ J_{2k} = 1.
let start = (n_max.max(x.ceil() as usize) + 30) / 2 * 2;
let mut miller = vec![0.0; start + 2];
miller[start] = 1e-200;
for n in (1..=start).rev() {
    miller[n - 1] = 2.0 * n as f64 / x * miller[n] - miller[n + 1];
}
let norm = miller[0] + 2.0 * miller[2..=start].iter().step_by(2).sum::<f64>();

let relative = |a: f64, b: f64| (a - b).abs() / b.abs();
let mut series = [Vec::new(), Vec::new(), Vec::new()];
let mut wronskian = 0.0_f64;
for n in 0..=n_max {
    let errors = [
        relative(forward_j[n], reference_j[n]),
        relative(miller[n] / norm, reference_j[n]),
        relative(forward_y[n], reference_y[n]),
    ];
    // Omit zero errors from logarithmic axes.
    for (line, error) in series.iter_mut().zip(errors) {
        if error > 0.0 {
            line.push([n as f64, error]);
        }
    }
    let w = reference_j[n + 1] * reference_y[n] - reference_j[n] * reference_y[n + 1];
    let exact = 2.0 / (std::f64::consts::PI * x);
    wronskian = wronskian.max(relative(w, exact));
}
let [forward_j_line, miller_line, forward_y_line] = series;
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Forward J", "points": forward_j_line }),
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Miller J", "points": miller_line }),
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "Forward Y", "points": forward_y_line }),
    ],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Degree n [-]",
        "yLabel": "Relative error against API values [-]",
        "yType": "log",
        "xMin": 0.0,
        "xMax": 60.0,
        "annotations": [{ "kind": "line", "axis": "x", "value": x, "label": "n = x" }]
    })
);

let sci2 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Forward J error (n = N) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Miller J error (n = N) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Forward Y error (n = N) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Maximum relative Wronskian residual [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    vec![(
        relative(forward_j[n_max], reference_j[n_max]),
        relative(miller[n_max] / norm, reference_j[n_max]),
        relative(forward_y[n_max], reference_y[n_max]),
        wronskian,
    )],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "errors" })
);
```

With the default $x = 10$, forward $J$ errors remain at roundoff level until $n \approx x$, then grow by about one decimal order per order for $n > x$, exceeding the value itself at $n = 26$.
Over the same range, Miller $J$ and forward $Y$ errors stay below $10^{-13}$.
If all orders lie in the oscillatory region, as with $x = 30$ and $N = 25$, forward $J$ also retains accuracy.
The Miller $J$ error then increases to about $3 \times 10^{-14}$ because the normalization sum $J_0 + 2\sum J_{2k}$ contains terms of differing signs and suffers cancellation.
For $x = 1$, the forward $J$ relative error already reaches $10^{-6}$ at $n = 7$, leaving only about six significant digits.
This is why $J$ and $I$ are used backward, and $Y$ and $K$ forward.
The relative Wronskian residual is at most $10^{-15}$ for every input, showing consistency between the API's $J_n$ and $Y_n$.

## Summary

Select cylindrical radial solutions from $J$, $Y$, and Hankel functions according to regularity at the origin and radiation conditions.
For exponentially varying $I$ and $K$ or complex arguments with large imaginary parts, use scaled forms or `bessel_k_log`.
Use three-term recurrence backward for minimal solutions ($J$, $I$) and forward for dominant solutions ($Y$, $K$); real $J$ and $Y$ respect these directions through Steed's method, and complex arguments and spherical Bessel functions through Miller's method.
For real orders and arguments, $J$, $Y$, $I$, $K$, and Hankel functions target 64 ULP over tested ranges; in other cases, assess accuracy through estimated errors in forms with options.
For applications, check intensity and energy flux in [Fraunhofer diffraction by a circular aperture and cylindrical waves](../../examples/cylindrical-wave/).
