When alternating current flows through a cylindrical conductor, higher frequencies concentrate the current nearer the surface: the skin effect.
This current distribution is expressed by order-zero modified Bessel functions with arguments rotated toward $e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}$; their real and imaginary parts are the Kelvin functions $\operatorname{ber}$, $\operatorname{bei}$, $\operatorname{ker}$, and $\operatorname{kei}$.
This page explains definitions and properties, evaluation in `scioxide-special`, scaled forms and derivatives, accuracy, and APIs, then computes the AC resistance of a cylindrical conductor.
It uses the definitions of $I_0$ and $K_0$ and complex-argument Bessel functions from [Cylindrical Bessel and Hankel functions](../airy-bessel-and-hankel/).

## Skin effect and Kelvin-function definitions

For alternating current of angular frequency $\omega$ in a cylindrical conductor with conductivity $\sigma$ and permeability $\mu$, using the electrical-engineering time convention $e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}$ and neglecting displacement current, the complex current-density amplitude $J(r)$ satisfies the following equation.

$$
J'' + \frac{1}{r}J' - \mathrm{i}\,\omega\mu\sigma\,J = 0.
$$

With skin depth $\delta = \sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)}$, the solution regular at the origin is $J(r) \propto I_0(\sqrt{2}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}r/\delta)$.
Kelvin functions are defined as real and imaginary parts of these order-zero Bessel functions (DLMF 10.61.1–2).

$$
\operatorname{ber}x + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{bei}x = I_0(x\,e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}) = J_0(x\,e^{3\mathrm{i}\pi/4}), \qquad
\operatorname{ker}x + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{kei}x = K_0(x\,e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}).
$$

`scioxide-special` provides only order-zero Kelvin functions; general orders such as $\operatorname{ber}_\nu$ are not provided.
For general orders, use $\operatorname{ber}_\nu x + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{bei}_\nu x = J_\nu(x\,e^{3\mathrm{i}\pi/4})$ and $\operatorname{ker}_\nu x + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{kei}_\nu x = e^{-\nu\pi\mathrm{i}/2} K_\nu(x\,e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4})$ (DLMF §10.61) with complex-argument `bessel_j` and `bessel_k`.

### Series, asymptotics, and Wronskians

Near the origin, use the series (DLMF 10.65.1).

$$
\operatorname{ber}x = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m (x/2)^{4m}}{[(2m)!]^2}, \qquad
\operatorname{bei}x = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m (x/2)^{4m+2}}{[(2m+1)!]^2}.
$$

$\operatorname{ber}$ and $\operatorname{bei}$ are even entire functions with $\operatorname{ber}0 = 1$ and $\operatorname{bei}0 = 0$.
$\operatorname{ker}$ has a logarithmic singularity at $x = 0$ ($\operatorname{ker}x \sim -\ln(x/2) - \gamma$; $\gamma$ is Euler's constant), while $\operatorname{kei}$ is continuous at $x \ge 0$ with $\operatorname{kei}0 = -\pi/4$.
For large $x$, the leading terms are as follows (DLMF 10.67.1–2).

$$
\operatorname{ber}x + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{bei}x \sim \frac{e^{x/\sqrt{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\,e^{\mathrm{i}(x/\sqrt{2} - \pi/8)}, \qquad
\operatorname{ker}x + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{kei}x \sim \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2x}}\,e^{-x/\sqrt{2}}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}(x/\sqrt{2} + \pi/8)}.
$$

$\operatorname{ber}$ and $\operatorname{bei}$ oscillate while growing as $e^{x/\sqrt{2}}$; $\operatorname{ker}$ and $\operatorname{kei}$ oscillate while decaying as $e^{-x/\sqrt{2}}$.
Applying $\mathscr{W}\{I_0, K_0\}(z) = -1/z$ (DLMF 10.28.2) at $z = x\,e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}$ gives the following real and imaginary identities.

$$
\operatorname{ber}x\operatorname{ker}'x - \operatorname{ber}'x\operatorname{ker}x - \operatorname{bei}x\operatorname{kei}'x + \operatorname{bei}'x\operatorname{kei}x = -\frac{1}{x}, \qquad
\operatorname{ber}x\operatorname{kei}'x - \operatorname{ber}'x\operatorname{kei}x + \operatorname{bei}x\operatorname{ker}'x - \operatorname{bei}'x\operatorname{ker}x = 0.
$$

## Evaluation of Kelvin functions

For $\lvert x\rvert \le 10$, $\operatorname{ber}$ and $\operatorname{bei}$ sum the series above in double-word arithmetic, representing about 106 bits with two `f64` values.
Terms alternate; at $x = 10$ the largest terms are 5–12 times the result, with still larger ratios near zeros, so summing only in `f64` loses digits to cancellation.
For $\lvert x\rvert > 10$, evaluate $I_0(\lvert x\rvert e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4})$ by series below 20 and asymptotic expansion at or above 20 (DLMF 10.67).
For $20 \le \lvert x\rvert < 28$, add exponentially small terms proportional to $K_0$ (times $e^{-\sqrt{2}\lvert x\rvert}$) to the asymptotic expansion of $I_0$.
$\operatorname{ker}$ and $\operatorname{kei}$ evaluate $K_0(x\,e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4})$ with logarithmic-term series in double-word arithmetic below 20 (DLMF 10.31), and asymptotic expansions at or above 20.
For $\lvert x\rvert < 10^{-150}$, use the general complex-argument Bessel kernel.
Derivatives such as $\operatorname{ber}'$ are evaluated alongside values from the same expansions.
Options forms for derivatives such as `berp_with_options` return second derivatives in `derivative()`, obtained from the differential equation as $\operatorname{ber}'' = -\operatorname{ber}'/x - \operatorname{bei}$ and $\operatorname{bei}'' = -\operatorname{bei}'/x + \operatorname{ber}$ (the same form applies to $\operatorname{ker}$ and $\operatorname{kei}$).

### Scaled forms and joint evaluation

Scaled forms multiply growing functions by $e^{-\lvert x\rvert/\sqrt{2}}$ and decaying functions by $e^{x/\sqrt{2}}$.
`ber_scaled`, `bei_scaled`, `berp_scaled`, and `beip_scaled` multiply by $e^{-\lvert x\rvert/\sqrt{2}}$; `ker_scaled`, `kei_scaled`, `kerp_scaled`, and `keip_scaled` multiply by $e^{x/\sqrt{2}}$.
Functions such as `berp_scaled` multiply $\operatorname{ber}'$ by the factor; they are not derivatives of `ber_scaled`.
In `f64`, $\operatorname{ber}$, $\operatorname{bei}$, and their derivatives overflow for $\lvert x\rvert$ above about 1010, giving `NumericalInstability` (`Overflow`); scaled forms remain finite.

`kelvin` returns $[\operatorname{ber}x, \operatorname{bei}x, \operatorname{ker}x, \operatorname{kei}x]$ together for $x > 0$.
One evaluation at $x\,e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}$ gives $\operatorname{ber}$ and $\operatorname{bei}$, and another gives $\operatorname{ker}$ and $\operatorname{kei}$, so work is comparable to one call each to `ber` and `ker`.
The options form `kelvin_with_options` returns four `SpecialFunctionResult` values, each also containing its derivative.
The four components share one work budget; each pair's work is charged to its first component.
`kelvin` returns `DomainError` (`Pole`) at the singularity $x = 0$ of $\operatorname{ker}$ and `DomainError` (`OutsideDomain`) at $x < 0$.

## Assessing accuracy

Kelvin functions have no uniform ULP specification.
Comparison tests check values, derivatives, and second derivatives against independently generated `f32` and `f64` references.
Errors are measured relative to the magnitude of the function pair ($\max(\lvert\operatorname{ber}x\rvert, \lvert\operatorname{bei}x\rvert)$ for $\operatorname{ber}$), so near one component's zero this measures absolute rather than relative error.
At reference-data points, tests confirm that `f64` error is at most $512\varepsilon$ times this unit and that options-form estimates (the error in `ValueWithError`, not an enclosure) cover actual errors.
This measured limit applies to reference-data points, not every accepted argument; inspect options-form estimates when accuracy matters.

## API

Every function has standard, options, and scoped forms.
Kelvin functions use `RealScalar` and accept only real numbers (`f32`, `f64`).
Options use `KelvinOptions`, containing only a work budget (`Budget`, not an accuracy request; default 65536 units).

| Standard form | Form with options | Scoped API | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ber`, `bei` | `ber_with_options`, `bei_with_options` | `ber_scoped`, `bei_scoped` | $\operatorname{ber}x$, $\operatorname{bei}x$ |
| `ker`, `kei` | `ker_with_options`, `kei_with_options` | `ker_scoped`, `kei_scoped` | $\operatorname{ker}x$, $\operatorname{kei}x$ |
| `ber_scaled`, `bei_scaled` | `ber_scaled_with_options`, `bei_scaled_with_options` | `ber_scaled_scoped`, `bei_scaled_scoped` | $e^{-\lvert x\rvert/\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{ber}x$, etc. |
| `ker_scaled`, `kei_scaled` | `ker_scaled_with_options`, `kei_scaled_with_options` | `ker_scaled_scoped`, `kei_scaled_scoped` | $e^{x/\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{ker}x$, etc. |
| `berp`, `beip` | `berp_with_options`, `beip_with_options` | `berp_scoped`, `beip_scoped` | $\operatorname{ber}'x$, $\operatorname{bei}'x$ |
| `kerp`, `keip` | `kerp_with_options`, `keip_with_options` | `kerp_scoped`, `keip_scoped` | $\operatorname{ker}'x$, $\operatorname{kei}'x$ |
| `berp_scaled`, `beip_scaled` | `berp_scaled_with_options`, `beip_scaled_with_options` | `berp_scaled_scoped`, `beip_scaled_scoped` | $e^{-\lvert x\rvert/\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{ber}'x$, etc. |
| `kerp_scaled`, `keip_scaled` | `kerp_scaled_with_options`, `keip_scaled_with_options` | `kerp_scaled_scoped`, `keip_scaled_scoped` | $e^{x/\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{ker}'x$, etc. |
| `kelvin` | `kelvin_with_options` | `kelvin_scoped` | $[\operatorname{ber}, \operatorname{bei}, \operatorname{ker}, \operatorname{kei}]$ (options forms also return each component's derivative) |

Non-finite inputs return `NonFinite`.
$\operatorname{ker}$, $\operatorname{kei}$, and their derivatives and scaled forms return `DomainError` (`OutsideDomain`) for $x < 0$; $\operatorname{ker}$ and $\operatorname{ker}'$ return `DomainError` (`Pole`) for $x = 0$.
$\operatorname{ber}$, $\operatorname{bei}$, and their derivatives accept every finite $x$, returning `NumericalInstability` (`Overflow`) only when unscaled values overflow.

## AC resistance and skin effect

For a cylindrical conductor of radius $a$, the ratio of AC resistance per unit length $R$ to DC resistance $R_0 = 1/(\pi a^2\sigma)$ is expressed by Kelvin functions with $q = \sqrt{2}\,a/\delta$ (Ramo, Whinnery and Van Duzer, 1994).

$$
\frac{R}{R_0} = \frac{q}{2}\,\frac{\operatorname{ber}q\operatorname{bei}'q - \operatorname{bei}q\operatorname{ber}'q}{(\operatorname{ber}'q)^2 + (\operatorname{bei}'q)^2}.
$$

The series and asymptotic forms above independently give its low- and high-frequency limits.

$$
\frac{R}{R_0} \approx 1 + \frac{q^4}{192} \quad (q \ll 1), \qquad
\frac{R}{R_0} \approx \frac{q}{2\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{a}{2\delta} + \frac{1}{4} \quad (q \gg 1).
$$

The leading high-frequency term $a/(2\delta)$ is the ratio of resistance $1/(2\pi a\delta\sigma)$, assuming current flows only in a surface layer of thickness $\delta$, to $R_0$.
The current-density distribution is $\lvert J(r)/J(a)\rvert = \lvert\operatorname{ber}(qr/a) + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{bei}(qr/a)\rvert / \lvert\operatorname{ber}q + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{bei}q\rvert$; for $q \gg 1$, it decays from the surface as $e^{-(a-r)/\delta}\sqrt{a/r}$.

The next cell selects $a/\delta$, computes $R/R_0$ from `kelvin_with_options` values and derivatives, compares both limits, and plots $\lvert J(r)/J(a)\rvert$.
It also shows the corresponding frequency $f = 1/(\pi\mu_0\sigma\delta^2)$ for copper wire of radius $a = 1$ mm ($\sigma = 5.8 \times 10^7$ S/m, $\mu = \mu_0$).
The final two columns show residuals of two Kelvin Wronskian identities at the same point ($x\,\times$ the left side $+ 1$, and $x\,\times$ the left side), checking consistency of values and derivatives.

```rust
//| id: special-kelvin-asymptotic-check
//| ref: special-kelvin-asymptotic-check
//| title: "Compute cylindrical-conductor AC resistance and current distribution using Kelvin functions"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-special]
//| inputs:
//|   radius_over_depth: {"type":"range","label":"Radius to skin-depth ratio a/δ [-]","description":"Proportional to the square root of frequency.","min":0.2,"max":20,"step":0.2,"value":3}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"low-frequency","label":"a/δ = 0.6","description":"Current is nearly uniform, with R/R₀ near 1 + q⁴/192.","inputs":{"radius_over_depth":0.6}}
//|   - {"id":"high-frequency","label":"a/δ = 20","description":"Current concentrates in a thin surface layer, with R/R₀ near a/(2δ) + 1/4.","inputs":{"radius_over_depth":20}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":1.768131652509737,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/5","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/6","range":{"max":1e-13}}
//|   - case: low-frequency
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":1.0026941818853217,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/2","approx":{"value":1.0027,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|   - case: high-frequency
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":10.254679114660355,"rtol":1e-11}}
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/3","approx":{"value":10.25,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"resistance","path":"/rows/0/5","range":{"max":1e-12}}
use scioxide_core::RealScalar;
use scioxide_special::{KelvinOptions, SpecialFunctionResult, kelvin_with_options};

let q = std::f64::consts::SQRT_2 * radius_over_depth;
let parts = |x: f64| -> Result<[(f64, f64); 4], String> {
    let results =
        kelvin_with_options(x, KelvinOptions::default()).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let pair = |r: &SpecialFunctionResult<f64>| -> Result<(f64, f64), String> {
        let value = r.value().ok_or("Missing value")?;
        let slope = r.derivative().ok_or("Missing derivative")?;
        Ok((*value.value(), *slope.value()))
    };
    Ok([
        pair(&results[0])?,
        pair(&results[1])?,
        pair(&results[2])?,
        pair(&results[3])?,
    ])
};
let [(ber, dber), (bei, dbei), (ker, dker), (kei, dkei)] = parts(q)?;
let ratio = 0.5 * q * (ber * dbei - bei * dber) / (dber * dber + dbei * dbei);
// Low- and high-frequency limits (independent checks derived from series and asymptotic forms).
let low = 1.0 + q.powi(4) / 192.0;
let high = q / (2.0 * std::f64::consts::SQRT_2) + 0.25;
// Kelvin Wronskian identities (real and imaginary parts of the I₀ and K₀ Wronskian).
let real_part = q * (ber * dker - dber * ker - bei * dkei + dbei * kei) + 1.0;
let imaginary_part = q * (ber * dkei - dber * kei + bei * dker - dbei * ker);
// Frequency f = 1/(π μ₀ σ δ²) for copper wire with a = 1 mm.
let depth = 1.0e-3 / radius_over_depth;
let mu0_sigma = 4.0e-7 * std::f64::consts::PI * 5.8e7;
let frequency = 1.0 / (std::f64::consts::PI * mu0_sigma * depth * depth);

let surface = RealScalar::hypot(ber, bei);
let mut profile = Vec::new();
let mut skin = Vec::new();
for index in 1..=100 {
    let s = f64::from(index) / 100.0;
    let [(b, _), (c, _), _, _] = parts(q * s)?;
    profile.push([s, RealScalar::hypot(b, c) / surface]);
    // Near-surface approximation e^{−(a−r)/δ} √(a/r) (reference curve using standard f64 functions).
    skin.push([s, (-(1.0 - s) * radius_over_depth).exp() / s.sqrt()]);
}
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "|J(r)/J(a)| (Kelvin functions)", "points": profile }),
        serde_json::json!({
            "name": "e^{−(a−r)/δ} √(a/r)",
            "points": skin,
            "style": { "lineType": "dashed" }
        }),
    ],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Radial position r/a [-]",
        "yLabel": "Current-density amplitude ratio [-]",
        "yType": "log",
        "xMin": 0.0,
        "xMax": 1.0,
        "yMin": 1e-9,
        "yMax": 10.0
    })
);

let sig6 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 6 });
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let sci2 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Frequency f [Hz] for copper wire with a = 1 mm", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "1", "label": "R/R₀ (Kelvin functions) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "1 + q⁴/192 [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "a/(2δ) + 1/4 [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "q = √2 a/δ [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig6 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Real Wronskian residual [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Imaginary Wronskian residual [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci2 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    vec![(
        frequency,
        ratio,
        low,
        high,
        q,

        imaginary_part.abs(),
    )],
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "resistance" })

```

For the default $a/\delta = 3$ (about 39 kHz in copper wire), $R/R_0 = 1.7681$, differing from the high-frequency limit $1.75$ by 1%.
At $a/\delta = 0.6$, the relative difference between $R/R_0 = 1.002694$ and the low-frequency limit $1.0027$ is $6 \times 10^{-6}$; at $a/\delta = 20$, that between $10.2547$ and the high-frequency limit $10.25$ is $5 \times 10^{-4}$; both shrink toward $q \to 0$ and $q \to \infty$, respectively.
These limits are independently derived from Kelvin series and asymptotic forms, checking values and derivatives from both low and high frequencies.
In the current-density chart, increasing $a/\delta$ concentrates current in a surface layer of thickness about $\delta$, overlapping the dashed approximation near the surface.
For small $a/\delta$, the distribution is nearly uniform and the dashed approximation is unusable.
Both Wronskian residuals stay of order $10^{-15}$ or smaller for every input.

## Summary

Kelvin functions are real and imaginary parts of $I_0$ and $K_0$ in the direction $e^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}$; `scioxide-special` provides only order zero, so use complex-argument `bessel_j` and `bessel_k` for general orders.
$\operatorname{ber}$ and $\operatorname{bei}$ for $\lvert x\rvert \le 10$, and $\operatorname{ker}$ and $\operatorname{kei}$ below 20, use series with double-word cancellation control; larger arguments use asymptotic expansions.
Above $\lvert x\rvert$ of about 1010, $\operatorname{ber}$ and $\operatorname{bei}$ overflow, so use scaled forms; when all four values are needed, `kelvin` halves the work.
With no uniform ULP specification, check accuracy using options-form estimates, Wronskian identities, and known limits.
See [Fraunhofer diffraction by a circular aperture and cylindrical waves](../../examples/cylindrical-wave/) for cylindrical wave radiation and diffraction, and [Kelvin, Struve, and parabolic cylinder functions and Bessel integrals](../../api/kelvin-struve-parabolic/) for individual specifications.
