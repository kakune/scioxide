Special functions arise when solving wave or Schrödinger equations by separation of variables, computing cumulative probabilities, or integrating distributions in statistical mechanics.
Computing a function value is only part of the calculation: physical quantities must be converted to dimensionless arguments, and results checked against closed forms, limits, conservation laws, or other algorithms.
Every example in this section derives functions from a concrete subject and parameters, evaluates them with scioxide-special, and checks them independently of the implementation.

## Shared procedure for the examples

### Construct dimensionless arguments

Special-function arguments are dimensionless quantities.
Divide dimensional variables by a problem-specific length, time, energy, or temperature before passing them.
For circular apertures, construct $x = ka\sin\theta$ from radius $a$ and wavenumber $k$; for simple pendulums, construct parameter $m = \sin^2(\theta_0/2)$ from maximum angle $\theta_0$.
For solid heat capacity, form $x_D = \Theta_D/T$ using the Debye temperature; for electron gases, use $\mu/k_BT$ as the unknown and $\theta = k_BT/E_F$ as the input.
Nondimensionalization allows the same function values to apply at different physical scales and reveals material-independent curves, such as heat capacity $C_V/3R$.

### Check conventions and normalization

Even identically named functions can have different normalizations or argument conventions across references.
Elliptic integrals accept parameter $m = k^2$, Fermi–Dirac integrals are normalized by $1/\Gamma(j+1)$, and Debye functions use normalization $D_n(0) = 1$.
When copying a formula from a reference, first check conventions at a known point (such as $F_{1/2}(0)$ or the large-$x$ asymptotic form of $D_3$).

### Round-trip forward and inverse problems

For inverse problems such as quantiles and chemical potentials, substitute the computed solution into the forward function and inspect the residual.
A small round-trip residual shows only consistency between inverse and forward functions; it cannot detect a shared error, such as incorrect normalization.
In probability tails, small densities produce large quantile condition numbers (the ratio of relative output error to relative input error), so a small round-trip residual can accompany a large relative quantile error.

### Compare limits and other algorithms

Compare values from formulas or algorithms independent of the implementation.
Check that the pendulum period ratio approaches $1 + \theta_0^2/16$ at small angles, Debye heat capacity approaches the $T^3$ law at low temperatures, and at low energies the energy dependence of Coulomb penetrability is governed by the Gamow factor $\exp(-2\pi\eta)$ while $P_0\,e^{2\pi\eta}$ for the $s$ wave approaches a constant.
Also compare different algorithms, such as Brent numerical solutions and Lambert W closed forms, or the two heat-capacity formulas using `debye_3` and `transport_4`.
None of these checks proves an error bound for the compared values.
Display differences in tables and explain their sources in the text: truncation, rounding, conditioning, or model approximation.

## Summary

The examples follow the same procedure: construct dimensionless arguments from physical quantities, evaluate scioxide-special functions, and check with closed forms, limits, conservation laws, or another algorithm.
When applying them to your own problem, first check function conventions and normalization at a known point, then verify that discrepancies from limiting formulas decrease at the expected rate.
For inverse problems, compare limiting formulas or other algorithms as well as round-trip residuals.
