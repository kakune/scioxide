Equations whose unknown appears in both a polynomial and an exponential generally cannot be solved by inverses of elementary functions.
Those reducible to $x = a + b\,e^{cx}$ have closed forms in the Lambert W function $W$, which solves $W e^W = z$.
This example transforms the blackbody spectral-peak equation and the current equation for a diode with series resistance into this form and solves them with `lambert_w0`.
Both solutions are compared with direct solutions of the same equations using scioxide-rootfind's Brent method; Wien's displacement law is also compared with CODATA constants.

See [Lambert W and Wright ω functions](../../algorithms/lambert-w-and-wright-omega/) for branches, branch points, and evaluation methods.

## Wien's displacement law and an exact transcendental solution

Planck's law gives the energy radiated per unit wavelength (spectral radiance) by a blackbody at temperature $T$ as follows.

$$
B_\lambda(\lambda, T) = \frac{2 h c^2}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_B T)} - 1}
$$

Here $h$ is the Planck constant, $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum, and $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant.
With dimensionless variable $x = hc/(\lambda k_B T)$, $B_\lambda$ is proportional to $x^5/(e^x - 1)$ at fixed $T$.
Differentiating with respect to $x$ and setting the derivative to 0 gives the following equation.

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{x^n}{e^x - 1} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = n\,(1 - e^{-x})
$$

Energy per unit wavelength has $n = 5$.
The same equation takes $n = 3$ for energy per unit frequency $B_\nu \propto x^3/(e^x - 1)$, $n = 4$ for photon count per unit wavelength, and $n = 2$ for photon count per unit frequency.
The peak position depends on the variable with respect to which the spectral density is expressed.

### Transform to Lambert W form

Setting $w = x - n$ in $x = n(1 - e^{-x})$ gives $w = -n e^{-x} = -n e^{-n} e^{-w}$ and hence the following form.

$$
w\,e^{w} = -n\,e^{-n}
$$

For $n \ge 2$, the right-hand side $-n e^{-n}$ lies in $(-1/e, 0)$, so there are two real solutions.
The solution on $W_{-1}$ is $w = -n$, or $x = 0$, the trivial solution.
The principal-branch solution gives the peak in the following closed form.

$$
x_n = n + W_0\!\left(-n\,e^{-n}\right)
$$

For $n = 5$, $x_5 = 4.965114231744276\ldots$ makes the product of peak wavelength and temperature constant.

$$
\lambda_{\max} T = \frac{hc}{k_B\,x_5} = b
$$

This is Wien's displacement law, and $b$ is the Wien displacement constant.
The 2019 SI revision made $h$, $c$, and $k_B$ defined values, so $b$ is determined by calculation.
CODATA 2018 (Tiesinga et al. 2021) gives $b = 2.897\,771\,955 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}$; the frequency-peak constant ($n = 3$) is $b' = k_B x_3/h = 5.878\,925\,757 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{Hz\,K^{-1}}$.

### Compute and check the peak positions

The next cell computes $x_n = n + W_0(-n e^{-n})$ with `lambert_w0` for $n = 2, 3, 4, 5$.
As an independent check, it solves the same equation $g(x) = x - n(1 - e^{-x}) = 0$ using scioxide-rootfind's Brent method with interval $[n - 1, n]$.
Since $g(n - 1) = n e^{1-n} - 1 < 0$ and $g(n) = n e^{-n} > 0$, the interval brackets only the peak solution and excludes the trivial solution $x = 0$.
The second table gives peak positions as wavelengths and frequencies at the input temperature $T$.
The default $T = 5772\ \mathrm{K}$ is the nominal solar effective temperature defined by IAU 2015 Resolution B3.

```rust
//| id: special-lambert-wien-diode
//| ref: special-lambert-wien-diode
//| title: "Wien displacement roots and comparison with Brent's method"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [special-lambert-si-constants]
//| crates: [scioxide-special, scioxide-rootfind, scioxide-core]
//| inputs:
//|   temperature: {"type":"range","label":"Temperature T [K]","description":"Temperature for peak wavelengths and frequencies. 5772 K is the solar effective temperature; 2.7255 K is the cosmic microwave background temperature.","min":2.7255,"max":10000,"step":0.0005,"value":5772}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"cmb","label":"T = 2.7255 K","description":"Cosmic microwave background. The peak moves to millimeter wavelengths.","inputs":{"temperature":2.7255}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"roots","path":"/rows/3/2","approx":{"value":4.965114231744276,"rtol":1e-15}}
//|       - {"artifact":"roots","path":"/rows/3/4","range":{"max":1e-15}}
//|       - {"artifact":"roots","path":"/rows/1/4","range":{"max":1e-15}}
//|       - {"artifact":"roots","path":"/rows/3/6","equals":"ResidualTolerance"}
//|       - {"artifact":"peaks","path":"/rows/3/2","approx":{"value":2897.771955,"rtol":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"peaks","path":"/rows/1/3","approx":{"value":58.78925757,"rtol":1e-9}}
//|       - {"artifact":"peaks","path":"/rows/3/4","approx":{"value":0.502038,"rtol":1e-5}}
//|   - case: cmb
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"peaks","path":"/rows/3/4","approx":{"value":1063.2,"rtol":1e-4}}
use scioxide_core::{Interval, RealScalar};
use scioxide_rootfind::{Brent, ScalarFn, ScalarRootOptions, ScalarRootSolver};
use scioxide_special::lambert_w0;

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let sig16 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 16 });
let sig10 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 10 });
let forms = [
    (2_u32, "Photon count per unit frequency"),
    (3, "Energy per unit frequency"),
    (4, "Photon count per unit wavelength"),
    (5, "Energy per unit wavelength"),
];
let mut roots = Vec::new();
let mut peaks = Vec::new();
for (n, name) in forms {
    let n_real = f64::from(n);
    // Closed form x_n = n + W₀(−n e^{−n}).
    let closed =
        n_real + lambert_w0(-n_real * RealScalar::exp(-n_real)).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    // Independent check: solve g(x) = x − n(1 − e^{−x}) on [n − 1, n] with Brent's method.
    let g = ScalarFn::new(move |x: f64| Ok(x - n_real * (1.0 - RealScalar::exp(-x))));
    let bracket = Interval::new(n_real - 1.0, n_real).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let solved = Brent
        .solve(&g, bracket, &ScalarRootOptions::default())
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let brent = solved.point();
    roots.push((
        n,
        name,
        closed,
        brent,
        (closed - brent).abs() / closed,
        solved.iterations(),
        format!("{:?}", solved.termination()),
    ));
    // hc/(k x) [μm K], k x/h [GHz/K], and peak wavelength [μm] and frequency [THz] at temperature T.
    let wavelength_constant = PLANCK * LIGHT_SPEED / (BOLTZMANN * closed) * 1e6;
    let frequency_constant = BOLTZMANN * closed / PLANCK * 1e-9;
    peaks.push((
        n,
        name,
        wavelength_constant,
        frequency_constant,
        wavelength_constant / temperature,
        frequency_constant * temperature * 1e-3,
    ));
}
let root_columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "n", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Spectral representation" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "x (W₀)[-]", "type": "number", "format": sig16 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "x (Brent) [-]", "type": "number", "format": sig16 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Relative difference [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Brent iterations", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Termination status" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    root_columns,
    roots,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "roots" })
);
let peak_columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "n", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Spectral representation" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "hc/(k x) [μm·K]", "type": "number", "format": sig10 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "k x/h [GHz/K]", "type": "number", "format": sig10 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Peak wavelength [μm]", "type": "number", "format": { "significantDigits": 6 } },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Peak frequency [THz]", "type": "number", "format": { "significantDigits": 6 } }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    peak_columns,
    peaks,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "peaks" })
);
```

In the first table, the relative difference between closed-form $x_n$ and Brent's solution is at most $1.8 \times 10^{-16}$ for all four $n$, agreeing at the level of double-precision rounding.
Brent's method stops after 4–6 iterations with termination `ResidualTolerance`, indicating that the residual at the returned point meets the tolerance.
For $n = 5$, $x_5 = 4.965114231744276$ gives $hc/(k_B x_5) = 2897.771955\ \mu\mathrm{m\,K}$, agreeing with CODATA's $b$ to 10 digits.
For $n = 3$, $k_B x_3/h = 58.789257576\ \mathrm{GHz/K}$ also agrees with the 10 digits $5.878\,925\,757 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{Hz\,K^{-1}}$ given by CODATA for $b'$.

In the second table, the default $T = 5772\ \mathrm{K}$ gives the energy-per-wavelength peak at $0.50204\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (green).
The energy-per-frequency peak is $339.3\ \mathrm{THz}$, corresponding to wavelength $0.8835\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (near infrared).
The different peaks are consistent: converting $B_\lambda\,d\lambda = B_\nu\,d\nu$ introduces $d\nu/d\lambda = -c/\lambda^2$ and changes the density's shape.
The cmb case ($T = 2.7255\ \mathrm{K}$, the cosmic microwave background temperature from Fixsen 2009) gives a wavelength peak of $1.063\ \mathrm{mm}$ and a frequency peak of $160.2\ \mathrm{GHz}$.
Since $x_n$ is temperature independent, changing temperature leaves the first table unchanged; peak frequency varies in proportion to $T$ and peak wavelength inversely with $T$.

## Current through a diode with series resistance

The current $I$ and junction voltage $V_D$ of a semiconductor diode obey the Shockley equation $I = I_s\,[e^{V_D/(n V_T)} - 1]$.
Here $I_s$ is saturation current, $n$ is the ideality factor, and $V_T = k_B T/e$ is the thermal voltage ($25.85\ \mathrm{mV}$ at $T = 300\ \mathrm{K}$).
Real devices have electrode and semiconductor resistances in series, so applying terminal voltage $V$ gives $V_D = V - I R_s$, and the current satisfies the following equation.

$$
I = I_s\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{V - I R_s}{n V_T}\right) - 1\right]
$$

Since $I$ appears both in the exponent and on the left-hand side, elementary functions cannot express $I$ in terms of $V$.

### Transform to Lambert W form

Add $I_s$ to both sides and set $y = (I + I_s) R_s/(n V_T)$.
Using $V - I R_s = (V + I_s R_s) - (I + I_s) R_s$ gives the following equation.

$$
y\,e^{y} = \frac{I_s R_s}{n V_T}\exp\!\left(\frac{V + I_s R_s}{n V_T}\right)
$$

The right-hand side is positive, so applying $W_0$ gives a closed form for the current.

$$
I(V) = \frac{n V_T}{R_s}\,W_0\!\left[\frac{I_s R_s}{n V_T}\exp\!\left(\frac{V + I_s R_s}{n V_T}\right)\right] - I_s
$$

At large $V$, the exponential in the argument on the right exceeds the double-precision range (when the exponent exceeds about 709.8).
Using the logarithm of the argument $\ell = \ln(I_s R_s/(n V_T)) + (V + I_s R_s)/(n V_T)$ gives $W_0(e^\ell) = \omega(\ell)$, so the Wright ω function computes the same value without overflow.

As $R_s \to 0$, this expression reduces to the Shockley equation.
At high current, the junction voltage $V_D = n V_T \ln(I/I_s + 1)$ grows only logarithmically with $I$, so $I = (V - V_D)/R_s$ is approximately proportional to $V/R_s$, and resistance determines the current.
On a semilogarithmic chart, the low-current region follows the straight line of the Shockley equation and bends below it at high current.

### Compute and check the current

The next cell computes current in three ways at the input $R_s$ and voltage $V$ for illustrative values $I_s = 2.5\ \mathrm{nA}$, $n = 1.8$, and $T = 300\ \mathrm{K}$ (not values for a specific device).
The methods are the closed form above using `lambert_w0`, the Wright ω form using `wright_omega`, and scioxide-rootfind's Brent method.
Brent's method solves the logarithmic equation $h(I) = \ln(I/I_s + 1) - (V - I R_s)/(n V_T) = 0$ on interval $[0, V/R_s]$.
Since $h$ increases monotonically with $I$ and satisfies $h(0) < 0$ and $h(V/R_s) > 0$, the interval brackets exactly one solution and avoids exponential overflow.
The last row, $V = 40\ \mathrm{V}$, demonstrates overflow of the argument to `lambert_w0`.
The chart plots current from $V = 0.05$ to $1.19\ \mathrm{V}$ on semilogarithmic axes; the dashed curve is the Shockley equation for $R_s = 0$.

```rust
//| id: special-lambert-diode-series-resistance
//| ref: special-lambert-diode-series-resistance
//| title: "Current through a diode with series resistance"
//| run: reactive
//| uses: [special-lambert-si-constants]
//| crates: [scioxide-special, scioxide-rootfind, scioxide-core]
//| inputs:
//|   series_resistance: {"type":"range","label":"Series resistance R_s [Ω]","description":"Larger values limit high currents more strongly through resistance.","min":0.05,"max":10,"step":0.05,"value":0.5}
//|   voltage: {"type":"range","label":"Terminal voltage V [V]","description":"Voltage for the second table row and the chart's vertical line.","min":0.3,"max":1.2,"step":0.01,"value":0.8}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"large-resistance","label":"R_s = 10 Ω","description":"The resistance-dominated current range starts at a lower voltage.","inputs":{"series_resistance":10}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"currents","path":"/rows/1/4","range":{"max":1e-14}}
//|       - {"artifact":"currents","path":"/rows/1/5","range":{"max":1e-14}}
//|       - {"artifact":"currents","path":"/rows/4/1","equals":null}
//|       - {"artifact":"currents","path":"/rows/4/5","range":{"max":1e-14}}
//|   - case: large-resistance
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"currents","path":"/rows/1/5","range":{"max":1e-14}}
//|       - {"artifact":"currents","path":"/rows/4/5","range":{"max":1e-14}}
use scioxide_core::{Interval, RealScalar};
use scioxide_rootfind::{Brent, ScalarFn, ScalarRootOptions, ScalarRootSolver};
use scioxide_special::{lambert_w0, wright_omega};

let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let sig10 = serde_json::json!({ "significantDigits": 10 });
let saturation = 2.5e-9; // I_s [A]
let ideality = 1.8; // n [-]
let thermal = ideality * BOLTZMANN * 300.0 / ELEMENTARY_CHARGE; // n V_T [V]
let rs = series_resistance;
// Logarithm ℓ of the closed-form W₀ argument. ℓ remains finite even when e^ℓ is out of range.
let log_argument =
    |v: f64| RealScalar::ln(saturation * rs / thermal) + (v + saturation * rs) / thermal;
let current_w0 = |v: f64| {
    lambert_w0(RealScalar::exp(log_argument(v)))
        .ok()
        .map(|w| thermal / rs * w - saturation)
};
let current_omega = |v: f64| {
    wright_omega(log_argument(v))
        .map(|w| thermal / rs * w - saturation)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())
};

let mut rows = Vec::new();
for v in [0.4, voltage, 1.0, 2.0, 40.0] {
    let by_omega = current_omega(v)?;
    // Independent check: solve h(I) = ln(I/I_s + 1) − (V − I R_s)/(n V_T) on [0, V/R_s].
    let h = ScalarFn::new(move |i: f64| {
        Ok(RealScalar::ln_1p(i / saturation) - (v - i * rs) / thermal)
    });
    let bracket = Interval::new(0.0, v / rs).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let solved = Brent
        .solve(&h, bracket, &ScalarRootOptions::default())
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let by_brent = solved.point();
    let by_w0 = current_w0(v);
    rows.push((
        v,
        by_w0,
        by_omega,
        by_brent,
        by_w0.map(|i| (i - by_brent).abs() / by_brent),
        (by_omega - by_brent).abs() / by_brent,
        by_omega * rs,
    ));
}
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "V [V]", "type": "number", "format": { "significantDigits": 3 } },
    { "key": "1", "label": "I (W₀)[A]", "type": "number", "format": sig10, "nullText": "Argument out of range" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "I (ω)[A]", "type": "number", "format": sig10 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "I (Brent) [A]", "type": "number", "format": sig10 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Relative difference between W₀ and Brent [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3, "nullText": "-" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Relative difference between ω and Brent [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "6", "label": "Resistive voltage drop I R_s [V]", "type": "number", "format": sig10 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "currents" })
);

let mut with_resistance = Vec::new();
let mut ideal = Vec::new();
for k in 1..=115 {
    let v = 0.05 + 0.01 * (k - 1) as f64;
    with_resistance.push([v, current_omega(v)?]);
    // Shockley equation for R_s = 0 (reference curve).
    ideal.push([v, saturation * RealScalar::exp_m1(v / thermal)]);
}
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![
        serde_json::json!({ "name": "With series resistance", "points": with_resistance }),
        serde_json::json!({
            "name": "R_s = 0 (Shockley equation)",
            "points": ideal,
            "style": { "lineType": "dashed" }
        })
    ],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Terminal voltage V [V]",
        "yLabel": "Current I [A]",
        "yType": "log",
        "xMin": 0.05,
        "xMax": 1.2,
        "yMin": 1e-9,
        "yMax": 100.0,
        "annotations": [{ "kind": "line", "axis": "x", "value": voltage, "label": "Selected V" }]
    })
);
```

With the default $R_s = 0.5\ \Omega$, the relative differences between the three methods are at most $1.4 \times 10^{-15}$ at every voltage.
At $V = 0.4\ \mathrm{V}$, the current is $13.5\ \mu\mathrm{A}$ and the resistive voltage drop is only $6.8\ \mu\mathrm{V}$, giving nearly the same value as the Shockley equation.
At $V = 0.8\ \mathrm{V}$, the current is $45.1\ \mathrm{mA}$ and the drop is $22.5\ \mathrm{mV}$; at $2\ \mathrm{V}$, the current is $2.09\ \mathrm{A}$ and the resistance takes $1.04\ \mathrm{V}$, more than half the terminal voltage.
At $V = 40\ \mathrm{V}$, the argument $e^\ell$ to `lambert_w0` is out of range and leaves a blank, but both Wright ω and Brent return $77.75\ \mathrm{A}$, with junction voltage only $40 - 38.88 = 1.12\ \mathrm{V}$.

The dashed Shockley curve is straight on semilogarithmic axes, while the series-resistance curve departs downward once current exceeds roughly $n V_T/R_s$.
For the default $R_s = 0.5\ \Omega$, this scale is $93\ \mathrm{mA}$; in the large-resistance case ($R_s = 10\ \Omega$), it falls to $4.7\ \mathrm{mA}$, and current at $V = 0.8\ \mathrm{V}$ decreases to $9.50\ \mathrm{mA}$.
Agreement between the closed form and Brent checks the transformation $y\,e^y = \ldots$; both values solve the same physical model.
Comparison with a real device requires determining $I_s$, $n$, and $R_s$ from measurements.

## Summary

Shifting the unknown in an equation of the form $x = a + b\,e^{cx}$ to obtain $w e^w = z$ gives a closed-form solution with `lambert_w0`.
When two real solutions exist, choose the branch that gives the desired one: for Wien's displacement law, the trivial solution $x = 0$ lies on $W_{-1}$ and the peak on $W_0$.
The closed form agrees at rounding level with Brent's solution of the same bracketed equation, and the constants for $n = 5$ and $n = 3$ reproduce CODATA's $b$ and $b'$.
When the exponential argument of $W_0$ overflows, as in the diode equation, passing its logarithm to `wright_omega` computes the same value even at high current.
