All three topics on this page summarize samples in ways that ordinary moments such as the mean and variance cannot.
Angles are periodic, so their arithmetic mean depends on the location of the period boundary.
The uncertainty of a probability distribution (entropy) and differences between two distributions (KL divergence and Wasserstein distance) are defined as functionals of distribution functions or densities, rather than of the sample values themselves.
For each topic, this page explains the definitions, estimators and their bias, and the `scioxide-stats` APIs, and checks estimates against distributions with known theoretical values.

## Circular statistics and multidimensional directional statistics

### Mean direction from the resultant vector

The arithmetic mean of angles $\theta_i$ depends on where the period boundary is placed.
For example, $0.1$ and $2\pi-0.1$ both point near 0, but the arithmetic mean of their values represented in $[0,2\pi)$ is $\pi$, the opposite direction.
Circular statistics map each angle to the point $(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)$ on the unit circle and summarize the direction and length of their average (Mardia and Jupp, 2000).

For weights $w_i\ge0$ with sum $W=\sum_iw_i>0$, define the components of the mean resultant vector by

$$
\bar C=\frac1W\sum_{i=1}^nw_i\cos\theta_i,\qquad \bar S=\frac1W\sum_{i=1}^nw_i\sin\theta_i
$$

Define the mean resultant length $\bar R$, mean direction $\bar\theta$, circular variance $V$, and circular standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm c}$ by

$$
\bar R=\sqrt{\bar C^2+\bar S^2},\qquad \bar\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(\bar S,\bar C),\qquad V=1-\bar R,\qquad \sigma_{\mathrm c}=\sqrt{-2\ln\bar R}
$$


We have $0\le\bar R\le1$; when all angles coincide, $\bar R=1$, and when they spread evenly around the circle, $\bar R$ approaches 0.
The mean direction is undefined when $\bar R=0$.
For a normal distribution with standard deviation $\sigma$ wrapped around the circle (a wrapped normal distribution), the population value of $\bar R$ is $e^{-\sigma^2/2}$, so $\sigma_{\mathrm c}$ equals $\sigma$.
When angles are concentrated, $\sigma_{\mathrm c}$ can be read on the same scale as an ordinary standard deviation.

`Statistic::circular(options)` selects this summary, and computing it with `statistic` returns `StatisticValue::Circular(CircularSummary)`.
`CircularSummary` contains the mean direction `mean`, mean resultant length `resultant_length`, circular variance `variance`, and circular standard deviation `standard_deviation`.
Weights accept `Unweighted`, `Frequency`, and `Mass`.
`CircularOptions` specifies the period interval $[\mathrm{low},\mathrm{high})$ and maps a value $x$ to an angle $\theta=2\pi(x-\mathrm{low})/(\mathrm{high}-\mathrm{low})$.
The default is $[0,2\pi)$ in radians; for degrees, supply $[0,360)$, and for times of day, supply $[0,24)$ through `with_low` and `with_high`.
The mean direction is returned in the same interval $[\mathrm{low},\mathrm{high})$, and the circular standard deviation in the same units as the period.

The computation costs $O(n)$ and accumulates sums of $\sin$ and $\cos$ with compensated summation.
If the range of weighted terms does not fit in `f64`, the computation is repeated with arithmetic that extends the exponent range.
$\bar R$ is computed with a scaled hypot rather than a sum of squares, so it remains nonzero even when squaring the components would underflow.
If $\bar R\le8\varepsilon$ (where $\varepsilon$ is the scalar type's machine epsilon), rounding error determines the mean direction, so `mean` is `None`.
`standard_deviation` is `None` when $\bar R=0$.
An error is returned when the weights sum to 0, the period interval is not positive and finite, or an angle is NaN or infinite.

### Theoretical values for the von Mises distribution

The von Mises distribution $\mathrm{vM}(\mu,\kappa)$, the circular counterpart of a normal distribution, has density

$$
f(\theta)=\frac{\exp\{\kappa\cos(\theta-\mu)\}}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)}
$$

Here, $I_\nu$ is the modified Bessel function of the first kind, and $\kappa\ge0$ is the concentration parameter.
Since $\mathrm E\cos(\theta-\mu)=A_1(\kappa)=I_1(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$ and $\mathrm E\sin(\theta-\mu)=0$, the population mean resultant length is $A_1(\kappa)$ and the mean direction is $\mu$.
The recurrence $I_2(\kappa)=I_0(\kappa)-2I_1(\kappa)/\kappa$ gives $\mathrm E\cos2(\theta-\mu)=1-2A_1/\kappa$, and for $\mu=0$ we obtain

$$
\operatorname{Var}\cos\theta=1-\frac{A_1}{\kappa}-A_1^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}\sin\theta=\frac{A_1}{\kappa}
$$


For $n$ independent samples, expanding as $\bar R\approx\bar C+\bar S^2/(2\bar C)$ gives the first-order approximations to the expectation and standard error (SE) of $\bar R$:

$$
\mathrm E\bar R\approx A_1+\frac{1}{2n\kappa},\qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar R)\approx\sqrt{\frac{1-A_1/\kappa-A_1^2}{n}},\qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar\theta)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\kappa A_1}}
$$


The $\bar S^2$ term biases $\bar R$ upward relative to $A_1$; the bias has magnitude $1/(2n\kappa)$.
This approximation applies when $nA_1^2$ is sufficiently greater than 1; when $\kappa$ is small and $A_1$ is comparable to its SE, the distribution of $\bar R$ approaches a Rice distribution and the normal approximation breaks down.

For the von Mises–Fisher distribution $\mathrm{vMF}(\boldsymbol\mu,\kappa)$ on the three-dimensional unit sphere, the expectation of $t=\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\mu$ is $A_3(\kappa)=\coth\kappa-1/\kappa$, with $\mathrm Et^2=1-2A_3/\kappa$.
The same expansion gives $\mathrm E\bar R\approx A_3+1/(n\kappa)$ and $\operatorname{SE}(\bar R)\approx\sqrt{(1-2A_3/\kappa-A_3^2)/n}$.

### Multidimensional direction vectors

For directional data in $\mathbb R^d$ (three-dimensional wind direction vectors, crystal orientations, or observations on a sphere), normalize each vector to a unit vector $\mathbf u_i=\mathbf x_i/\lVert\mathbf x_i\rVert$ and form the mean resultant vector $\bar{\mathbf R}=W^{-1}\sum_iw_i\mathbf u_i$.
Its length $\lVert\bar{\mathbf R}\rVert$ measures concentration, and $\bar{\mathbf R}/\lVert\bar{\mathbf R}\rVert$ is the mean direction.
For each batch of a view whose vector component axis is specified by `BatchView::with_coordinates`, `directional_statistics_into(view, normalization, options, directions, reports, workspace, context)` writes $d$ mean-direction components to `directions` and writes `DirectionalSummary` (the length `resultant_length` and `direction_defined` indicating whether the direction is defined) to `reports`.
`DirectionNormalization::Normalize` normalizes each vector without overflow and rejects zero vectors with an error.
`DirectionNormalization::RequireUnit(t)` requires $\lvert\lVert\mathbf x\rVert-1\rvert\le t$ before normalization.
When the length is at most $8\varepsilon$, every mean-direction component is 0 and `direction_defined` is false.
Allocate workspace with the `DirectionalRequirements` returned by `DirectionalWorkspace::workspace_requirements(view, options, context)`; the cost per batch is $O(nd)$.

### Compare von Mises samples with theoretical values

It compares $\bar R$, the mean direction, and circular standard deviation with theoretical values and SEs derived from $A_1(\kappa)$ computed by `bessel_i` in `scioxide-special`, and checks that the mean direction expressed with a daily period of $[0,24)$ agrees with the unit conversion.

## Entropy and information-theoretic measures

### Shannon entropy and KL divergence

For a discrete distribution $P=(p_1,\dots,p_k)$ and reference distribution $Q=(q_1,\dots,q_k)$, define Shannon entropy and Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence with logarithm base $b>1$ by

$$
H(P)=-\sum_{i:\,p_i>0}p_i\log_bp_i,\qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\Vert\,Q)=\sum_{i:\,p_i>0}p_i\log_b\frac{p_i}{q_i}
$$

(Cover and Thomas, 2006, §2.1, §2.3).
The unit is nat for base $e$ and bit for base 2.
We have $0\le H(P)\le\log_bk$, with the upper bound attained by the uniform distribution.
We have $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\ge0$, with equality only when $P=Q$ (Gibbs' inequality).
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality, so it is not a distance.
If a component has $p_i>0$ and $q_i=0$, then $D_{\mathrm{KL}}=+\infty$; this means $P$ assigns positive probability to an event impossible under $Q$.

With the default base $e$, `entropy(p)` returns $H(P)$ and `relative_entropy(p, q)` returns $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)$.
The detailed form `entropy_with_options(p, q, options)` computes entropy when `None` is passed to `q`, and KL divergence when `Some(q)` is passed; `EntropyOptions` selects the base (`with_base`) and mass handling (`with_normalization`).
`MassNormalization::Normalize` (the default) divides nonnegative masses by their sum to form probabilities; `MassNormalization::RequireUnit(t)` requires the sum to be within $t$ of 1 before dividing by it.
For aggregation along an axis, the selector `Statistic::entropy(options)` chooses the same quantities (treating observations as probability masses).
The computation costs $O(k)$, accumulates sums with compensated summation, and evaluates $-\ln p_i$ as $\ln\{1+(1-p_i)/p_i\}$ for components with more than half the total mass, preserving relative accuracy even for $p_i$ close to 1.

The result is an extended-real value `InformationValue`, distinguishing finite `Finite(S)`, `PositiveInfinity` for divergent KL divergence, and `NegativeInfinity` for zero spacings caused by tied observations in a differential-entropy estimator.
Infinity is a mathematically correct value, rather than overflow.
`InvalidInput` is returned when the base is not finite and greater than 1, `p` and `q` have different lengths, masses are negative, NaN, or infinite, masses sum to 0 (including empty input), or the conditions of `RequireUnit` are not met.

### Estimating differential entropy from spacings

The differential entropy of a continuous density $f$ is $h(f)=-\int f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx$.
Using the quantile function $Q=F^{-1}$, it can be written as $h=\int_0^1\ln Q'(u)\,du$, and $Q'(u)$ can be approximated by spacings between order statistics.
Vasicek (1976) constructed the following estimator from a sorted sample $x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}$ and window half-width $m<n/2$:

$$
\hat h_{\mathrm V}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\left\{\frac{n}{2m}\left(x_{(i+m)}-x_{(i-m)}\right)\right\}
$$

(using $x_{(1)}$ and $x_{(n)}$ for indices outside the range).
At an endpoint, the spacing covers only half the window but is still divided by $2m$, so $\hat h_{\mathrm V}$ has a downward bias in small samples.
`DifferentialEntropyMethod` selects the following estimators.

- `Vasicek` is the formula above.
- `Ebrahimi` replaces the endpoint factor $2m$ with $c_im$, adjusted to the actual number of spacings used ($c_i=1+(i-1)/m$ for $i\le m$, $c_i=1+(n-i)/m$ for $i>n-m$, and 2 otherwise), reducing endpoint bias (Ebrahimi et al., 1994).
- `VanEs` adds the harmonic-number correction $\sum_{k=m}^n1/k+\ln\{m/(n+1)\}$ to the average logarithm of the forward spacings $x_{(i+m)}-x_{(i)}$ (van Es, 1992).
- `Correa` replaces each spacing with the slope of a least-squares regression of ranks against $x_{(i-m)},\dots,x_{(i+m)}$, smoothing spacing fluctuations (Correa, 1995).
- `Auto` (the default) selects `VanEs` for $n\le10$, `Ebrahimi` for $n\le1000$, and `Vasicek` for larger samples.

The default window half-width is $m=\lfloor\sqrt n+1/2\rfloor$.
`differential_entropy(values)` uses the defaults; the detailed form `differential_entropy_with_options(values, options)` estimates with the estimator (`with_method`), window (`with_window`), and base (`with_base`) in `DifferentialEntropyOptions`.
The selector for aggregation along an axis is `Statistic::differential_entropy(options)`.
The cost is $O(n\log n)$ for sorting and $O(n)$ for estimation ($O(nm)$ for `Correa`), with one allocated copy of the sample.
`InvalidInput` is returned for an empty sample, a window that does not satisfy $0<2m<n$ (the default window requires $n\ge5$), an invalid base, or a nonpositive local regression slope in `Correa`; NaN and infinity return `NonFinite`.
When repeated observations produce a zero spacing, the estimate is `NegativeInfinity`.
The estimators do not accept weights.

### Check against closed forms and the normal distribution

The first table in the next cell checks entropy and KL divergence for the probability mass function of the binomial distribution $\mathrm{Bin}(10,p)$.
Entropy is computed in bits with the selector `Statistic::entropy` and compared with the direct sum $-\sum p\log_2p$.
KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{Bin}(10,p)\Vert\mathrm{Bin}(10,1/2))$ is ten times the value for one trial because the distribution is a sum of independent trials:

$$
10\left\{p\ln(2p)+(1-p)\ln(2(1-p))\right\}
$$


The table also includes `PositiveInfinity` when the reference distribution has zero mass and `NegativeInfinity` for tied samples.
The second table computes each of four estimators 50 times on $n$ samples from the standard normal distribution and compares the means and SEs with the true value $h=\tfrac12\ln(2\pi e)\approx1.4189$ nat.

## One-dimensional probability distances and optimal transport

### Wasserstein-1 and energy distances

The Wasserstein-$p$ distance between probability distributions $\mu,\nu$ minimizes transport cost over couplings (joint distributions) $\pi$ that move $\mu$ to $\nu$:

$$
W_p(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\lVert x-y\rVert^p\,d\pi(x,y)\right)^{1/p}
$$

(Villani, 2009).
For $p=1$, $W_1$ is also called earth mover's distance.
On the real line, the optimal coupling is the monotone coupling pairing equal quantiles, so the distance can be written using the distribution functions $F,G$ as

$$
W_1(F,G)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert F(x)-G(x)\rvert\,dx=\int_0^1\lvert F^{-1}(u)-G^{-1}(u)\rvert\,du
$$

(Vallender, 1973).
For independent $X,X'\sim F$ and $Y,Y'\sim G$, the energy distance (Székely and Rizzo, 2013) is defined by

$$
D(F,G)^2=2\,\mathrm E\lvert X-Y\rvert-\mathrm E\lvert X-X'\rvert-\mathrm E\lvert Y-Y'\rvert=2\int_{-\infty}^\infty\{F(x)-G(x)\}^2\,dx
$$


$D=0$ and $F=G$ are equivalent, and $D$ is a distance between distributions.

For normal distributions $N(0,1)$ and $N(\Delta,\sigma^2)$, since $F^{-1}(u)=z_u$ and $G^{-1}(u)=\Delta+\sigma z_u$, we have $W_1=\mathrm E\lvert\Delta+(\sigma-1)Z\rvert$ ($Z\sim N(0,1)$).
Using the expected absolute value of $a+bZ$ (the mean of a folded normal distribution),

$$
\mathrm E\lvert a+bZ\rvert=b\sqrt{2/\pi}\,e^{-a^2/(2b^2)}+a\{1-2\Phi(-a/b)\}\qquad(b>0)
$$

we obtain $W_1$ with $a=\Delta$ and $b=\lvert\sigma-1\rvert$ ($\lvert\Delta\rvert$ if $b=0$).
The energy distance follows from $X-Y\sim N(-\Delta,1+\sigma^2)$, $\mathrm E\lvert X-X'\rvert=2/\sqrt\pi$, and $\mathrm E\lvert Y-Y'\rvert=2\sigma/\sqrt\pi$.
For reference, $W_2=\sqrt{\Delta^2+(\sigma-1)^2}$.

### Distances between empirical distributions and bias

`distribution_distance(left, right, metric)` computes the distance between two samples' empirical distributions using `DistributionDistance::Wasserstein` ($W_1$) or `DistributionDistance::Energy` ($D$).
It sorts the combined samples and uses the fact that the step function $F_n-G_m$ is constant between adjacent points to evaluate the integral exactly as a sum over intervals.
The cost is $O((n+m)\log(n+m))$, and sums use compensated summation.
Supply weights and the missing-data policy through the detailed form `distribution_distance_with_options(left, left_weights, right, right_weights, metric, options)`; each side independently accepts no weights, frequencies, or relative masses.
Weights are divided by their sum to form probabilities, and points with zero weight are ignored.
To reuse sorting storage, allocate workspace with the `DistanceRequirements` returned by `DistanceWorkspace::workspace_requirements(n, m, options)` and call `DistanceWorkspace::evaluate`.
`InvalidInput` is returned for mismatched weight lengths, negative or nonfinite weights, zero total weight (including an empty sample), or NaN or infinite points with positive weight; `ResourceLimit` is returned when sorting storage exceeds the limit.

Distances between empirical distributions are biased estimators of population distances.
For the squared energy distance, $n^{-2}\sum_{i,j}\lvert x_i-x_j\rvert$ includes zeros for $i=j$, so its expectation is

$$
\mathrm E\hat D^2=D^2+\frac{\mathrm E\lvert X-X'\rvert}{n}+\frac{\mathrm E\lvert Y-Y'\rvert}{m}
$$


There is no simple formula for the bias of $W_1$, but it is nonnegative because $\mathrm E\lvert F_n-G_m\rvert\ge\lvert F-G\rvert$ at every point, and is of order $O(n^{-1/2})$ where the distributions overlap.
Even when $F=G$, $\hat W_1$ is not 0.

`scioxide-stats` does not provide one-dimensional $W_p$ ($p\ne1$).
For two unweighted samples of the same size $n$, pairing sorted values gives $\bigl(n^{-1}\sum_i\lvert x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}\rvert^p\bigr)^{1/p}$, which equals $W_p$ between the empirical distributions and can therefore be computed directly from the sorted samples.

### Multidimensional optimal transport

For point sets $\{\mathbf x_i\}$ (probabilities $a_i$) and $\{\mathbf y_j\}$ (probabilities $b_j$) in $\mathbb R^d$, $W_1$ is the optimal value of the following linear transport program with costs $c_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf x_i-\mathbf y_j\rVert_2$:

$$
\min_{\pi\ge0}\sum_{ij}c_{ij}\pi_{ij}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_j\pi_{ij}=a_i,\quad\sum_i\pi_{ij}=b_j
$$


Its dual maximizes $\sum_ia_iu_i+\sum_jb_jv_j$ subject to $u_i+v_j\le c_{ij}$, and its optimal value equals that of the primal.

`wasserstein_distance_nd(left, right, admission, options, solver)` constructs the cost matrix in $O(nmd)$ from two `BatchView` views with one batch each (with the component axis specified by `with_coordinates`) and calls the user-supplied `TransportSolver` once.
`scioxide-stats` has no linear-programming solver of its own; for example, `SimplexTransportSolver` (the sparse revised simplex method) from `scioxide-optimize` can be supplied.
The solver receives `TransportProblem` (probabilities `source` and `target`, normalized costs `costs`, and scale `cost_scale`), writes the solution to the coupling and dual variables in `TransportCandidate`, and returns `Converged`, `BudgetExhausted`, or `Stopped` from `StatsTermination`.
Costs are normalized by dividing by the largest absolute coordinate $s$, and distances are multiplied by $s$ to restore the original units.
The returned solution is independently checked with $O(nm)$, and `TransportResult` contains the marginal constraint residual `marginal_residual`, dual constraint violation `dual_residual`, and duality gap `normalized_duality_gap`.
A solution declared `Converged` becomes `InvalidInput` if it does not satisfy the `TransportOptions` tolerances (`feasibility` for marginal constraints and `optimality` for dual violations and the duality gap).
`distance` is `Some` only when the coupling satisfies the marginal constraints; unless `Converged`, it is the cost of a coupling that need not be optimal.
To reuse workspace, allocate $O(nm)$ storage with `TransportWorkspace::reserve(rows, cols, options)` and pass it to the scoped form `wasserstein_distance_nd_scoped`.
The scoped form counts the solver call as one evaluation in the parent computation; if no evaluation budget remains, it returns `BudgetExhausted` or `Stopped` without a distance.

## Summary

Map angles to points on the unit circle and average them; summarize the mean resultant length $\bar R$ and mean direction with `Statistic::circular`, and direction vectors in $\mathbb R^d$ with `directional_statistics_into`.
For von Mises samples, $\bar R$ exceeds $A_1(\kappa)=I_1/I_0$ by $1/(2n\kappa)$, so compare with theory using both this bias and the SE.
Compute discrete entropy and KL divergence with `entropy`, `relative_entropy`, and `Statistic::entropy`; variants of `InformationValue` distinguish infinities from divergence and ties.
Spacing estimators of differential entropy have downward bias in small samples; `Ebrahimi` reduces bias by correcting the number of endpoint spacings, while `VanEs` has the smallest bias for $n=10$.
One-dimensional $W_1$ and energy distance can be computed exactly from empirical distributions with `distribution_distance`; multidimensional $W_1$ is returned by `wasserstein_distance_nd` with an injected `TransportSolver` after verifying the solution.
