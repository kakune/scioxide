For count data, contingency tables test whether two categorical variables are independent or two groups have equal proportions.
When expected counts are large, a chi-squared approximation yields p values; for small or sparse tables, approximation errors increase and exact tests are needed.
Exact tests include conditional Fisher and unconditional Barnard and Boschloo tests, distinguished by which margins are fixed (the sampling design).
When many hypotheses are tested simultaneously, each test's $\alpha$ cannot control the number of false positives, so p values are adjusted.
When independent studies test the same null hypothesis, their p values can be combined into one.
This page explains the assumptions and null distributions of these methods and checks actual rejection and error rates in cells.

## Contingency-table representation and basic statistics

Represent an $R$-row, $C$-column contingency table with `ContingencyView::new(counts, rows, columns)`, which borrows a row-major count sequence `&[usize]`.
No copy is made; `rows()`, `columns()`, and `counts()` return its shape and contents.
`margins_into(row, column)` writes row sums $r_i$ and column sums $c_j$ to caller-owned `u128` storage and returns the total $N$.
`expected_into(output, rows, columns)` writes expected counts under independence:

$$
E_{ij} = \frac{r_i c_j}{N}
$$


Both cost $O(RC)$, summing counts exactly with integer arithmetic.

Association strength is measured by a nominal association coefficient `contingency_association(table, measure)` constructed from Pearson's chi-squared statistic $X^2$ (without continuity correction).
`AssociationMeasure` has three variants.

- `CramerV`: $V = \sqrt{X^2/(N\min(R-1, C-1))}$, lying in $[0, 1]$.
- `TschuprowT`: $T = \sqrt{X^2/(N\sqrt{(R-1)(C-1)})}$, lying in $[0, 1]$ and unable to reach 1 for nonsquare tables.
- `Pearson`: contingency coefficient $C = \sqrt{X^2/(X^2 + N)}$, whose maximum depends on table dimensions and is less than 1.

For a $2 \times 2$ table $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, `odds_ratio(table)` returns the sample odds ratio $ad/(bc)$.
It returns 0 when $ad = 0$ and $+\infty$ when $bc = 0$; if both are 0, it returns `InvalidInput`.
`relative_risk(table)` treats rows as exposure groups and the first column as events, returning the relative risk $(a/(a+b))/(c/(c+d))$.
Transposing the table leaves the odds ratio unchanged, but relative risk depends on the roles of rows and columns.
Confidence intervals for odds ratios and relative risks are not provided.
Construct an approximate log-odds-ratio interval from Woolf's standard error $\sqrt{1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d}$, or use the coefficient interval from logistic regression in [GLMs](../regression-anova-and-glm/).

## Chi-squared independence tests and Cressie–Read statistics

The Cressie–Read power-divergence family measures differences between observed counts $O_{ij}$ and expected counts $E_{ij}$.

$$
2NI^{\lambda} = \frac{2}{\lambda(\lambda+1)}\sum_{i,j} O_{ij}\left[\left(\frac{O_{ij}}{E_{ij}}\right)^{\lambda} - 1\right]
$$

$\lambda = 1$ gives Pearson's chi-squared statistic $X^2 = \sum (O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}$, while the limit $\lambda \to 0$ gives the likelihood-ratio statistic (G test) $G^2 = 2\sum O_{ij}\ln(O_{ij}/E_{ij})$.
Other common choices are $\lambda = -1/2$ (Freeman–Tukey), $\lambda = -1$ (modified log-likelihood), and $\lambda = 2/3$ (recommended by Cressie and Read).
Under the null hypothesis of independence, for every $\lambda$ the statistic approaches a chi-squared distribution with $(R-1)(C-1)$ degrees of freedom as $N \to \infty$.
Construct $\lambda$ with `CressieReadParameter::new(lambda)`; constants `PEARSON` ($\lambda = 1$) and `G_TEST` ($\lambda = 0$) are available.

`power_divergence_contingency_test(table, divergence, correction)` returns this statistic and its chi-squared upper-tail probability in `ChiSquareResult` (`statistic()`, `p_value()`, `degrees_of_freedom()`).
For Pearson's statistic on a $2 \times 2$ table, `ContinuityCorrection::HalfUnit` applies Yates' continuity correction $\sum (|O - E| - 1/2)_+^2/E$.
This corrects the approximation of a discrete statistic by a continuous distribution; it has no effect on tables larger than $2 \times 2$, and requesting it for a statistic other than Pearson's returns `InvalidInput`.
The one-shot `chi2_contingency_test(table)` uses Pearson's statistic and applies Yates' correction only to $2 \times 2$ tables.
A zero row or column sum produces zero expected counts, so both return `InvalidInput`.

To test whether one sequence of counts follows a specified distribution, use `power_divergence_test(observed, expected, divergence, ddof)`.
When `expected` is `None`, the null hypothesis assigns equal probabilities; degrees of freedom $k - 1$ are reduced by the number `ddof` of parameters estimated to construct expected counts.
If observed and expected totals differ by more than relative $64\varepsilon$, it returns `InvalidInput`.

The chi-squared distribution is a large-sample approximation, so p values from `ChiSquareResult` are not exact probabilities.
Cochran's commonly used guideline requires no expected count below 1 and no more than 20% of cells with expected counts below 5.

## Exact tests

### Fisher's conditional exact test

Conditioning on both row and column sums gives a (multivariate) hypergeometric distribution of tables under the null hypothesis of independence.
In a $2 \times 2$ table, only the top-left count $a$ is free; with row sums $r_1, r_2$, column sum $c_1$, and total $N$, its probability is

$$
P(a) = \frac{\dbinom{r_1}{a}\dbinom{r_2}{c_1 - a}}{\dbinom{N}{c_1}}, \qquad \max(0, c_1 - r_2) \le a \le \min(r_1, c_1)
$$


`fisher_exact_test(table)` obtains a two-sided p value by summing probabilities of all tables with the observed margins whose probability is at most the observed table's (with relative comparison tolerance $64\varepsilon$).
For $2 \times 2$ tables, `Less` and `Greater` in the detailed form `fisher_exact_test_with_options` use the lower and upper tails of the top-left count.
The statistic is the sample odds ratio for $2 \times 2$ tables and the observed table's probability for larger tables.
For $R \times C$ tables, it enumerates all tables with the same margins (the Freeman–Halton extension) and accepts only two-sided tests.
The table enumeration limit is `max_work` and the workspace limit is `max_workspace_bytes`; exceeding them returns `ConvergenceFailure` and `ResourceLimit` respectively.
`Asymptotic` returns `InvalidInput`.

The conditional distribution with fixed margins contains no null parameter (the common success probability), enabling exact p values.
However, $a$ takes few values and p values are discrete, so a test with size exactly $\alpha$ cannot be constructed.
Consequently, Fisher's actual rejection rate is below $\alpha$ (conservative), and for designs with only one margin fixed it has less power than unconditional tests.

### Barnard and Boschloo unconditional exact tests

In designs such as two-group comparative trials, only group sizes (column sums $n_1, n_2$) are fixed, while the total number of successes is random.
Columns are independent binomial samples $X_1 \sim B(n_1, \pi_1)$ and $X_2 \sim B(n_2, \pi_2)$, and the first row contains successes.
Under the null hypothesis $\pi_1 = \pi_2 = \pi$, the probability of a table at least as extreme as observed,

$$
p(\pi) = \sum_{(x_1, x_2):\, T(x_1, x_2) \ge T_{\text{obs}}} \binom{n_1}{x_1}\binom{n_2}{x_2}\pi^{x_1 + x_2}(1-\pi)^{N - x_1 - x_2}
$$

depends on the unknown nuisance parameter $\pi$.
Unconditional exact tests use $\sup_{\pi \in [0,1]} p(\pi)$ as the p value, ensuring that the actual rejection rate never exceeds $\alpha$ for any $\pi$.

- `barnard_exact_test(table)` orders tables by the Wald statistic $T = (\hat p_1 - \hat p_2)/\hat\sigma$.
  Select the variance estimate through `UnconditionalOptions::with_pooled(VariancePooling)`: `Pooled` (the default, a common proportion) or `Unpooled` (group-specific proportions).
- `boschloo_exact_test(table)` orders tables by the one-sided Fisher p value.
  It is uniformly more powerful than the one-sided Fisher test; its two-sided p value is twice the smaller one-sided p value, capped at 1.

To compute $\sup_\pi p(\pi)$, represent the tail probability as a polynomial in $\pi$ in Bernstein form, then use branch and bound to bracket the supremum to width `UnconditionalOptions::tolerance()` (default $10^{-9}$).
The result `ExactContingencyResult` returns the upper endpoint of this bracket $p_{\text{lower}} \le p \le p_{\text{upper}}$ as `p_value()` (a conservative value), and its lower endpoint as `p_value_lower()`.
The bracket accounts only for terminating branch and bound, excluding rounding error.
If evaluation of `(n₁ + 1)(n₂ + 1)` tables and branch-and-bound work exceeds `max_work`, it returns `ConvergenceFailure`.
Pass the alternative and work limits through `UnconditionalOptions::with_classical(ClassicalOptions)`.
`Asymptotic` and a `tolerance` outside $(0, 1)$ return `InvalidInput`.
The detailed forms are `barnard_exact_test_with_options` and `boschloo_exact_test_with_options`.

The `design()` of `ExactContingencyResult` returns the sampling design conditioned on by the test as `ContingencyDesign`.
`FixedMargins` fixes both row and column sums (Fisher); `FixedColumns` fixes only column sums for two independent binomial samples (Barnard and Boschloo).
`statistic()` is the odds ratio for Fisher $2 \times 2$, observed table probability for $R \times C$, Wald statistic for Barnard, and Fisher p value for Boschloo; `tables()` is the number of tables evaluated.

McNemar's test for paired binary data and the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test for stratified $2 \times 2$ tables are not provided.
McNemar's exact test equals `binomial_test(b, b + c, 0.5)` applied to discordant pairs $b$ and $c$.
Obtain a stratum-adjusted odds ratio with logistic regression including strata as predictors ([GLMs](../regression-anova-and-glm/)).

### Compare tests on a 2 × 2 table

The next cell takes success and failure counts for two groups and applies chi-squared, Fisher, Barnard, and Boschloo tests to the same table.
Following the Barnard and Boschloo conventions, construct `ContingencyView` with columns as groups and the first row as successes, and pass the transpose with rows as groups to `relative_risk`.
The third table independently checks Fisher's p value by counting the hypergeometric probabilities above with `u128` integers and summing them for the closed-form value.
For inputs with zero counts causing a zero margin, the table displays the test's error kind.

### Choosing tests for sparse tables

A test's actual size is the sum of probabilities of rejected tables under the null.
There are only $(n_1 + 1)(n_2 + 1)$ $2 \times 2$ tables with fixed column sums $n_1, n_2$; checking every table rejected by each test at $\alpha$ and weighting by binomial probabilities at common success probability $\pi$ yields the actual rejection rate without Monte Carlo error.

$$
\text{actual rejection rate}(\pi) = \sum_{(x_1, x_2) \in \text{rejection region}} \binom{n_1}{x_1}\binom{n_2}{x_2}\pi^{x_1+x_2}(1-\pi)^{n_1+n_2-x_1-x_2}
$$

The next cell computes this sum at $\pi = 0.01, 0.02, \dots, 0.99$ (using `pmf` in `Binomial` for binomial probabilities) and compares uncorrected Pearson, Yates-corrected, Fisher, Barnard, and Boschloo tests at $\alpha = 0.05$.
Tables with zero total successes or failures have a zero margin and cannot be tested, so they are counted as nonrejections.

For small or sparse tables, the chi-squared approximation fails to maintain test size, while Fisher maintains it conservatively.
Choose Barnard or Boschloo when the design fixes only column sums; choose Fisher when conditioning on both row and column sums is natural for the design, as in randomization tests.

## Multiple comparisons and p-value adjustment

### Definitions of error rates

Suppose $m$ null hypotheses $H_1, \dots, H_m$ are tested simultaneously and $m_0$ are true.
Let $R$ be the number rejected and $V$ the number of rejected true nulls (false positives).
Testing each at level $\alpha$ gives a probability $1 - (1 - \alpha)^{m_0}$ of at least one false positive under independence, approximately 0.64 for $m_0 = 20$.
Multiplicity can be controlled by two criteria.

- The family-wise error rate (FWER) $P(V \ge 1)$ is the probability of any false positive.
- The false discovery rate (FDR) $E[V/\max(R, 1)]$ is the expected proportion of false positives among rejected hypotheses.
  If all null hypotheses are true ($m_0 = m$), FDR equals FWER; otherwise FDR $\le$ FWER.

scioxide-stats provides methods that replace each p value with an adjusted value $\tilde p_i$ and reject hypotheses satisfying $\tilde p_i \le \alpha$.
Below, $p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}$ denotes sorted p values.

### Controlling FWER

Select with `FamilywiseMethod`.

- `Bonferroni`: $\tilde p_i = \min(1, m p_i)$.
  It uses only Boole's inequality $P(\bigcup A_i) \le \sum P(A_i)$, so FWER $\le \alpha$ holds regardless of dependence between tests.
- `Holm`: the step-down method $\tilde p_{(i)} = \min\bigl(1, \max_{j \le i} (m - j + 1)\, p_{(j)}\bigr)$.
  Derived from closed testing, it controls FWER under arbitrary dependence, with adjusted p values no greater than Bonferroni's.

Šidák, Hochberg, and Hommel methods are not provided.
Use Holm to control FWER under arbitrary dependence.

### Controlling FDR

Select with `FalseDiscoveryMethod`.

- `BenjaminiHochberg`: the step-up method $\tilde p_{(i)} = \min\bigl(1, \min_{j \ge i} m\, p_{(j)}/j\bigr)$.
  When tests are independent or satisfy positive regression dependence (PRDS: conditional on each true-null test statistic, the probability that the statistic vector lies in an increasing set increases with that value), FDR $\le (m_0/m)\alpha \le \alpha$ holds.
- `BenjaminiYekutieli`: multiply the BH values by $c(m) = \sum_{k=1}^m 1/k \approx \ln m + 0.577$.
  It controls FDR under arbitrary dependence, but applies a correction of about 3.6 at $m = 20$, substantially reducing power.

Adaptive methods strengthening BH by estimating $m_0$, such as Storey's q values, are not provided.

### API for p-value adjustment

`adjust_pvalues(&p, method)` adjusts one sequence of p values as one family and returns `Vec<f64>` in the original order.
Specify the method with `PValueAdjustment::Familywise(FamilywiseMethod)` or `PValueAdjustment::FalseDiscovery(FalseDiscoveryMethod)`.
It allocates sorting workspace and costs $O(m \log m)$; to change the workspace byte limit, pass `StatisticsOptions` to the detailed form `adjust_pvalues_with_options(&p, method, options)`.
Empty p values or values outside $[0, 1]$ return `InvalidInput`; missing and nonfinite values are not accepted.

To adjust many equal-width families (lanes), allocate workspace once with `PValueAdjustmentWorkspace::new(lanes, width, options, context)` and call `adjust_pvalues_into(pvalues, width, method, options, output, workspace, context)`.
`pvalues` and `output` concatenate lanes of width `width`, with each lane adjusted separately.
Pass the same `options` and `context` used to construct the workspace; lanes are processed in parallel to the extent these allow.

## Combining independent p values

When $k$ independent studies test the same null hypothesis, combine their p values $p_1, \dots, p_k$ into one test.
Under the null, p values of continuous statistics are uniformly distributed, so the distribution of their function yields a combined p value.
`combine_pvalues(&p, method, weights)` selects the following methods with `PValueCombination` and returns `CombinedPValue` (`statistic()`, `p_value()`).

| Method | Statistic | Null distribution and p value |
|---|---|---|
| `Fisher` | $X = -2\sum_i \ln p_i$ | Upper-tail chi-squared probability with $2k$ degrees of freedom |
| `Pearson` | $X = 2\sum_i \ln(1 - p_i)$ | $P(\chi^2_{2k} \le -X)$. Becomes small when $p_i$ is near 0 |
| `Tippett` | $X = \min_i p_i$ | $1 - (1 - X)^k$ |
| `Stouffer` | $Z = \sum_i w_i \Phi^{-1}(1 - p_i)/\sqrt{\sum_i w_i^2}$ | Standard-normal upper-tail probability. The only method accepting weights |
| `MudholkarGeorge` | $L = \sum_i \ln((1 - p_i)/p_i)$ | Upper-tail probability comparing $L\sqrt{3(5k+4)/(k\pi^2(5k+2))}$ with a t distribution with $5k + 4$ degrees of freedom |

Fisher's method responds strongly to a very small p value in one study; Stouffer's is suited to combining weak effects across many studies.
Stouffer weights, such as the square roots of study sizes, must be nonnegative and finite; all-zero weights return `InvalidInput`.
A p value of 0 or 1 may produce an infinite statistic; when both 0 and 1 occur, Stouffer and logit methods return `InvalidInput`.
The null distributions assume independent uniform p values, so they are uncalibrated for discrete-test or dependent p values (next cell).

## Example: multiple-comparison error rates and combined-p-value rejection rates

The next cell repeats $m$ one-sided z tests $R$ times, measuring each adjustment's FWER, FDR, and power, and rejection rates for p-value combinations.
$m_0 = \lceil \pi_0 m \rceil$ null hypotheses are true; the remainder have a mean shift of $\mu$.
The statistics are $z_i = \sqrt{\rho}\, W + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\, e_i + \mu\,\mathbf 1\{i > m_0\}$ ($W$ and $e_i$ are independent standard-normal variates), with $\rho$ the correlation of every pair (equicorrelation).
The p values are $p_i = 1 - \Phi(z_i)$, computed with the normal survival function.
Equicorrelation with $\rho > 0$ satisfies PRDS, so BH should control FDR even with dependence.
The null for combining p values is the global null (all $H_i$ are true); the rejection rate when the true-null proportion is 1 is its size.
FWER and combined-rejection intervals in the table are Clopper–Pearson 95% intervals; errors in the FDR column are standard errors across repetitions.

## Summary

Test contingency-table independence with the Cressie–Read family's chi-squared approximations when expected counts are large, and use exact tests for small tables.
Choose exact tests by sampling design: Fisher when conditioning on both margins, Barnard or Boschloo when only column sums (group sizes) are fixed.
For $2 \times 2$ tables with fixed column sums, uncorrected chi-squared tests exceed test size and Fisher rejects at only about one third of nominal, while unconditional tests maintain size and reject at close to $\alpha$.
For multiple comparisons, choose Bonferroni or Holm (valid under arbitrary dependence) to control the chance of any false positive, or BH (independence or PRDS) or BY (arbitrary dependence) to control the false-positive proportion among discoveries.
Combining p values assumes independent continuous p values; dependent p values lead to excessive rejection of the global null.
