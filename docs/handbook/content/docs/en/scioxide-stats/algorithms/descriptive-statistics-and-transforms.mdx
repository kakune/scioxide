Descriptive statistics summarize a sample's center (location), spread, asymmetry (skewness), and tail weight (kurtosis) in a few numbers.
Summary values depend on the definitions used (degrees-of-freedom corrections, quantile definitions, and weight meanings) and on computation accuracy.
Measured data also often have long right tails or only positive values, departing from the assumptions of regression and ANOVA models based on normal distributions.
A power transformation is a one-parameter monotone transformation that maps such samples closer to a normal shape.

This page explains how `scioxide-stats` selects, aggregates, and standardizes descriptive statistics, then derives Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson transformations, maximum-likelihood estimates and confidence intervals for their parameter $\lambda$, normal probability plots, and the probability plot correlation coefficient (PPCC).

## Aggregating and standardizing descriptive statistics

### Statistic selectors and type restrictions

`Statistic<T, M>` is a statistic selector parameterized by scalar type `T` and weight model `M`.
Means, variances, extrema, geometric, harmonic, and power means, quantiles, median absolute deviations (MAD), trimmed means, L-moments, modes, expectiles, interquartile ranges, percentile ranks, tie correction factors, circular statistics, and entropy can be passed to the same function.
Use `statistic(values, weights, selector)` for one slice and `statistics_into` below for each batch of a strided array.
Select the median by passing probability 0.5 and the quantile convention `QuantileRule` to `Statistic::quantile`.

Some combinations of statistics and weights are undefined.
For example, an “unbiased sample variance” with relative-mass weights `Mass` is undefined because their sum does not represent sample size.
`scioxide-stats` makes such combinations compile errors instead of runtime errors.
Each variant carries a value `StatisticCapability<T, M, K>` that can only be created inside a constructor of `Statistic`.
Constructors exist only for defined combinations of `T` and `M`, so the existence of a `Statistic` value guarantees through its type that the combination can be computed.
Users do not handle `StatisticCapability` directly.

| Constructor | `T` | `M` | Type-level condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| `sum`, `mean` | Real or complex | `Unweighted` for complex; every weight model for real | `M: LocationModel<T>` |
| `variance(ddof)`, `standard_deviation(ddof)` | Real or complex | `Unweighted` | None |
| `geometric_mean`, `harmonic_mean`, `power_mean`, `quantile`, `circular` | Real | `Unweighted`, `Frequency`, `Mass` | `M: EmpiricalWeights` |
| `expectile` | Real | `Unweighted`, `Mass` | `M: ExpectileWeights` |
| Others (extrema, MAD, trimmed means, L-moments, mode, interquartile range, entropy, etc.) | Real | `Unweighted` | None |

`LocationModel<T>` is the trait for weight models supporting sums and means.
Unweighted `Unweighted` implements it for real and complex scalars; `Frequency`, `Mass`, `Reliability`, and `Analytic` implement it only for real scalars.
`ExpectileWeights` is a trait implemented only by `Unweighted` and `Mass`, which support expectiles (locations from asymmetric least squares).
Expectile definitions and algorithms appear in [Mathematics of empirical distributions, sample quantiles, and interpolation conventions](oxref:stats-quantile-expectiles).

The result `StatisticValue<T>` has separate variants for each value category.
`Location(T)` holds quantities with the observation type, such as sums, means, extrema, and L-moments; `Dispersion(T::Real)` holds variance and standard deviation (real even for complex samples); `Order` holds quantities with conventions and diagnostics, such as quantiles, modes, and expectiles; `Circular` holds circular statistics; and `Information` holds entropy.
Use `scalar()` to read an ordinary real value.
The variance of a complex sample is defined using the sum of squared absolute deviations $\lvert x_i-\bar x\rvert^2$.

### L-moments

The order-$r$ L-moment $\lambda_r$ is the expectation of a signed linear combination of order statistics $X_{1:r}\le\cdots\le X_{r:r}$ from a sample of size $r$ (Hosking, 1990).

$$
\lambda_r = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(-1)^k\binom{r-1}{k}\,\mathrm E\,X_{r-k:r}
$$

$\lambda_1$ is the mean; $\lambda_2=\tfrac12\mathrm E(X_{2:2}-X_{1:2})$ is half the expected difference between two observations and measures spread.
For $r\ge3$, use the ratios $\tau_r=\lambda_r/\lambda_2$; $\tau_3$ is L-skewness and $\tau_4$ is L-kurtosis.
L-skewness satisfies $-1<\tau_3<1$ and, unlike ordinary skewness and kurtosis, does not require third or fourth moments to exist.
Values therefore fluctuate less for heavy-tailed distributions and small samples.

The sample L-moment $l_r$ is the U-statistic averaging this combination over all subsamples of $r$ selected from $n$, and is an unbiased estimator of $\lambda_r$.
Using the sorted sample $x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}$, $l_r$ becomes a weighted sum of $x_{(i)}$.
`Statistic::l_moment(r, normalization)` computes this $l_r$: `LMomentNormalization::Raw` returns $l_r$ in the observation units, while `Standardized` returns the ratio $t_r=l_r/l_2$ for $r\ge3$ (the same value as `Raw` for $r\le2$).
The computation combines one sort costing $O(n\log n)$ and a sum costing $O(nr^2)$, using a recurrence for ratios of combinations and compensated summation.
It returns `InvalidInput` if $1\le r\le n$ fails, or if `Standardized` and $l_2=0$ (a constant sample).

| Distribution | Mean $\lambda_1$ | $\lambda_2$ | $\tau_3$ | $\tau_4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform $U(0,1)$ | $1/2$ | $1/6$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| Exponential (rate 1) | $1$ | $1/2$ | $1/3$ | $1/6$ |
| Logistic (location 0, scale 1) | $0$ | $1$ | $0$ | $1/6$ |
| Standard normal | $0$ | $1/\sqrt\pi\approx0.5642$ | $0$ | $0.1226$ |

These population values come from the tables in Hosking (1990) and are used for comparison in the cell below.

### Axis reductions and workspace

For statistics of each row or column of a multidimensional array, specify array strides and the sample axis with `BatchView` and compute with `statistics_into(view, selector, options, output, workspace, context)`.
`output[b]` contains the statistic of batch $b$.
`StatisticsOptions` combines missing-data and nonfinite-value handling (reject or omit), execution policy, and the workspace byte limit; `ExecutionContext` supplies parallel workers.
Each batch gives the same result in parallel and sequential execution.
`statistics(view, selector)` and `statistics_with_options` also allocate workspace internally and return results in `Vec`.

`StatisticsWorkspace::workspace_requirements(geometry, window, options, context)` returns the required workspace as `StatisticsRequirements`.
The type of `geometry` is constrained by `StatisticsGeometry` and accepts either `BatchView` or a `(StridedLayout, Axis)` pair before values are available.
A pair estimates the unweighted case, so workspace can be allocated once before loading data and reused to aggregate arrays of the same shape.
`StatisticsGeometry` is a sealed trait; users cannot add implementations.
Workspace estimated with `window` set to `None` supports `statistics_into`, standardization with `standardize_axes_into`, and ranking with `rank_axes_into`; estimate rolling `rolling_statistics_into` with the window `RollingWindow`.
After reservation, borrowed forms allocate nothing and leave outputs unchanged on failure.

Sums, means, variances, standard deviations, extrema, geometric, harmonic, and power means, circular statistics, and entropy need no workspace; order-based quantities such as quantiles, MAD, trimmed means, and L-moments use storage for sorting a sample copy.

### z-score standardization

The z score of a sample $x_1,\dots,x_n$ uses its mean $\bar x$ and standard deviation with denominator degrees of freedom $n-\mathrm{ddof}$,

$$
s=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-\mathrm{ddof}}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}
$$

and is defined as $z_i=(x_i-\bar x)/s$.
$\mathrm{ddof}=0$ uses the population standard deviation; $\mathrm{ddof}=1$ uses the square root of the unbiased variance.
`standardize(scores, reference, ddof)` standardizes a separate sample `scores` with $\bar x$ and $s$ from the reference sample `reference`.
Passing the same slice twice gives ordinary z scores; passing different samples gives a z-map measuring a treatment group against a control group.
For complex samples, $s^2=\sum\lvert x_i-\bar x\rvert^2/(n-\mathrm{ddof})$, dividing the real and imaginary deviations separately by $s$.
Means and sums of squares use compensated summation; the mean is retained as a sum of two `f64` values and deviations $x_i-\bar x$ are formed from both parts, reducing loss of significant digits in differences for samples with large common values.
Values with components outside the range of $2^{-64}\le\lvert x\rvert<2^{65}$ use arithmetic with an extended exponent range, avoiding intermediate overflow.

For repeated standardization, use `StandardizationWorkspace::workspace_requirements(n, options)` to obtain `StandardizationRequirements` for lengths up to $n$, and call `standardize_into` with workspace reserved through `try_reserve`.
When the reference sample satisfies $n\le\mathrm{ddof}$, its standard deviation is 0, or the reference sample or `scores` contains NaN or infinity, `standardize` and `standardize_into` return `InvalidInput`; an overflowing z score returns `NumericalInstability`.
Axis-wise `standardize_axes_into(view, ddof, options, output, workspace, context)` standardizes each batch by its own mean and standard deviation and concatenates outputs in batch order.
It rejects nonfinite values with `NonFinite` and rejects policies omitting missing or nonfinite observations with `InvalidInput` because every observation needs a z score.

### Compare quantile grids with population values

The next cell constructs a three-row, $n$-column array by evaluating the uniform, exponential, and logistic quantile functions $Q$ at equally spaced probabilities $u_i=(i-1/2)/n$; `statistics_into` computes each row's mean, standard deviation ($\mathrm{ddof}=1$), $l_2$, $t_3$, and $t_4$, displayed alongside the population values above.
A quantile grid is a sample without randomness, so differences between sample and population quantities decrease at a fixed rate as $n$ grows, distinguishable from random fluctuations.
Workspace is allocated from the `(StridedLayout, Axis)` pair before array values are created; the same workspace also computes row-wise z scores.
The second table checks that each z-score row has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and that its first z score agrees with a manual calculation using the table's mean and standard deviation.

For the exponential distribution, $t_3$ is 0.3343 (population $1/3$) and $t_4$ is 0.1679 ($1/6\approx0.1667$).

## Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson transformations

For positive observations $x>0$, Box and Cox (1964) introduced the transformation with power parameter $\lambda$:

$$
\psi_{\mathrm{BC}}(x;\lambda)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^\lambda-1}{\lambda} & (\lambda\ne0)\\[1ex]
\ln x & (\lambda=0)
\end{cases}
$$


Since $x^\lambda=e^{\lambda\ln x}=1+\lambda\ln x+O(\lambda^2)$, the limit $\lambda\to0$ equals $\ln x$, so $\psi_{\mathrm{BC}}$ is continuous in $\lambda$.
$\lambda=1$ only translates values without changing distribution shape; $\lambda<1$ compresses the right tail and $\lambda>1$ compresses the left tail.
For every $\lambda$ it is strictly increasing in $x$, preserving ranks.

Box–Cox transformations accept only positive values.
Yeo and Johnson (2000) proposed the following transformation for real observations including zeros and negative values.

$$
\psi_{\mathrm{YJ}}(x;\lambda)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(x+1)^\lambda-1}{\lambda} & (\lambda\ne0,\ x\ge0)\\[1ex]
\ln(1+x) & (\lambda=0,\ x\ge0)\\[1ex]
-\dfrac{(1-x)^{2-\lambda}-1}{2-\lambda} & (\lambda\ne2,\ x<0)\\[1ex]
-\ln(1-x) & (\lambda=2,\ x<0)
\end{cases}
$$

The $x\ge0$ branch is the Box–Cox transformation of $x+1$; the $x<0$ branch reflects it using exponent $2-\lambda$ on the $-x$ side.
The two branches join continuously at $x=0$ in value and first derivative (both are 1).

`PowerTransform::BoxCox` and `PowerTransform::YeoJohnson` represent these families.
Neither estimates a translation parameter ($\lambda_2$ in Box–Cox).
`apply(x, lambda)` transforms one value using $O(1)$, computing powers with `expm1` to avoid cancellation in the numerator of $(x^\lambda-1)/\lambda$ even near $\lambda=0$ ($\lambda=2$ for Yeo–Johnson's negative branch).
`log_jacobian(x, lambda)` returns $\ln\lvert d\psi/dx\rvert$ appearing in the likelihood below.
NaN or infinity in $x$ or $\lambda$ returns `NonFinite`, $x\le0$ for Box–Cox returns `InvalidInput`, and result overflow returns `NumericalInstability`.
Transform a whole slice with `power_transform_into(kind, samples, lambda, options, output, scratch, context)`, supplying storage of the sample's length for `output` and `scratch`.

## Parameter estimation by profile log-likelihood

### Likelihood of the transformed sample

Assume transformed values $y_i=\psi(x_i;\lambda)$ independently follow a normal distribution $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$.
By change of variables, the density of observation $x_i$ is the normal density of $y_i$ times the transformation derivative (Jacobian) $\lvert d\psi/dx\rvert$, giving log-likelihood

$$
\ell(\lambda,\mu,\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2+\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\left\lvert\frac{d\psi}{dx}(x_i;\lambda)\right\rvert
$$


The Jacobian term is $(\lambda-1)\ln x_i$ for Box–Cox; for Yeo–Johnson it is $(\lambda-1)\ln(1+x_i)$ when $x_i\ge0$ and $(1-\lambda)\ln(1-x_i)$ when $x_i<0$.
Without this term, simply reducing $\lambda$ to compress the spread of $y$ would increase the likelihood.

With $\lambda$ fixed, the maximum-likelihood estimators of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are $\bar y(\lambda)$ and $\hat\sigma^2(\lambda)=n^{-1}\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2$.
Substituting these to eliminate $\mu,\sigma^2$ gives the profile log-likelihood; omitting the constant $-\tfrac n2(1+\ln2\pi)$, it is

$$
\ell_p(\lambda)=-\frac n2\ln\hat\sigma^2(\lambda)+\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\left\lvert\frac{d\psi}{dx}(x_i;\lambda)\right\rvert
$$


The maximum-likelihood estimate $\hat\lambda$ of $\lambda$ maximizes $\ell_p$.
By likelihood-ratio asymptotics (Wilks' theorem), $2\{\ell_p(\hat\lambda)-\ell_p(\lambda_0)\}$ at the true value $\lambda_0$ approaches a $\chi^2$ distribution with one degree of freedom.
Thus,

$$
\left\{\lambda:\ \ell_p(\lambda)\ge\ell_p(\hat\lambda)-\tfrac12\chi^2_{1,\,0.95}\right\},\qquad \tfrac12\chi^2_{1,\,0.95}\approx1.9207
$$

The expression above gives an approximate 95% confidence interval for $\lambda$ (a profile likelihood interval).
In practice, a convenient value within the interval (such as $0$, $\pm1/2$, or $1$) is often selected for easier interpretation (Box and Cox, 1964).

### Algorithm and API

`power_transform_llf(kind, lambda, samples)` returns the above $\ell_p(\lambda)$ in $O(n)$.
Transformed-value variance uses centered online updates, while Jacobian terms use compensated summation.
To compute $\ln\hat\sigma^2$, take the logarithm in extended-exponent-range arithmetic before rounding the variance; the result remains correct even when $\hat\sigma^2$ underflows in `f64`.
Fewer than two observations or a constant transformed sample returns `InvalidInput`.

To search for $\hat\lambda$, prepare the objective $-\ell_p(\lambda)$ in `PreparedPowerFit` and minimize it with a user-supplied solver `FitSolver`.
`scioxide-stats` has no optimizer of its own; for example, `LbfgsBFitSolver` (bound-constrained L-BFGS-B) from `scioxide-optimize` can be supplied.
Use `power_transform_normmax(kind, samples, method, bounds, initial, solver)` for a one-time estimate.
This one-shot form returns an error for an unsuccessful search; the detailed form `power_transform_normmax_with_options` changes the memory limit and returns the report unchanged, and the scoped form `power_transform_normmax_scoped` accepts the parent computation context.
In the result `FitResult`, `parameters()` returns the estimate (`None` if execution stopped before a final evaluation), and `report()` returns a report containing termination status `StatsTermination` and evaluation counts.
`method` selects the criterion: `PowerFitMethod::Likelihood` maximizes $\ell_p$, and `PowerFitMethod::Pearson` maximizes the probability-plot correlation discussed next.

### Check $\hat\lambda$ and the likelihood interval

The next cell constructs $x=w^{1/\lambda_0}$ from values $w$ of a normal distribution $N(2,0.4^2)$ ($x=e^w$ for $\lambda_0=0$).
Since $x^{\lambda_0}=w$, $\psi_{\mathrm{BC}}(x;\lambda_0)=(w-1)/\lambda_0$ is normally distributed and the true $\lambda$ is $\lambda_0$ (values satisfying $w\le0$ are discarded, but their probability is about $3\times10^{-7}$).
It plots $\ell_p(\lambda)$ over $[-2,2]$, obtains $\hat\lambda$ with `power_transform_normmax_with_options` and `LbfgsBFitSolver` (both likelihood and correlation criteria), finds likelihood-interval endpoints by bisection, and reports whether the interval contains $\lambda_0$.
The sample and both estimates are passed to the cell in the next section.

//|   true_lambda: {"type":"range","label":"True λ₀ [-]","description":"Construct a sample for which x^λ₀ is normally distributed.","min":-1,"max":1,"step":0.25,"value":0}
//|   sample_size: {"type":"range","label":"Sample size n [count]","description":"Interval width shrinks approximately as 1/√n.","min":20,"max":400,"step":10,"value":60}

//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"square-root","label":"λ₀ = 0.5","description":"A sample made normal by a square-root transformation; the interval includes 0.5 and narrowly excludes 0.","inputs":{"true_lambda":0.5}}
//|   - {"id":"large-sample","label":"n = 400","description":"Interval width becomes about 0.35 times that at n = 60.","inputs":{"sample_size":400}}

//|       - {"path":"/outputs/2/state","equals":"satisfied"}
//|   - case: square-root
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"estimates","path":"/rows/0/2","approx":{"value":0.468,"atol":0.01}}

use scioxide_optimize::{LbfgsB, LbfgsBFitSolver};
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Normal, SeedableRng, SplitMix64};
use scioxide_stats::{

// Restrict w ~ N(2, 0.4²) to positive values and set x = w^(1/λ₀) (e^w for λ₀ = 0).
let n = sample_size as usize;
let mut rng = SplitMix64::from_seed(sampling_seed as u64);
let law = Normal::<f64>::new(2.0, 0.4).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut samples = Vec::with_capacity(n);
while samples.len() < n {
    let w = law.sample(&mut rng).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    if w > 0.0 {
        samples.push(if true_lambda == 0.0 {
            RealScalar::exp(w)
        } else {
            RealScalar::powf(w, 1.0 / true_lambda)
        });
    }
}

    .map(|i| {
        let lambda = -2.0 + 0.05 * f64::from(i);
        llf(lambda).map(|value| [lambda, value])
    })
    .collect::<Result<_, _>>()
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

let bounds = Bounds::new(vec![(Some(-3.0), Some(3.0))]).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;

    let mut solver = LbfgsBFitSolver::new(LbfgsB::default(), 1).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let fit = power_transform_normmax_with_options(
        PowerTransform::BoxCox,
        &samples,
        method,
        &bounds,
        1.0,
        &mut solver,
        StatisticsOptions::new(),
    )
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let termination = fit.report().termination();
    let lambda = fit.parameters().ok_or("Estimate is absent")?[0];
    Ok((lambda, format!("{termination:?}"), termination.is_success()))
};

let (pearson_lambda, pearson_status, _) = estimate(PowerFitMethod::Pearson)?;

let half_chi2 = 0.5 * 3.841458820694124;

    if gap(outer).map_err(|e| e.to_string())? < 0.0 {
        return Ok(None);

    let (mut inside, mut outside) = (likelihood_lambda, outer);
    for _ in 0..60 {
        let middle = 0.5 * (inside + outside);
        if gap(middle).map_err(|e| e.to_string())? < 0.0 {
            inside = middle;
        } else {
            outside = middle;
        }
    }
    Ok(Some(0.5 * (inside + outside)))
};
let (lower, upper) = (endpoint(-3.0)?, endpoint(3.0)?);
let covered = lower.is_none_or(|l| l <= true_lambda) && upper.is_none_or(|u| true_lambda <= u);

let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Reference" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Termination" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "λ̂ [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed3 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "ℓ_p(λ̂) [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed3 },
    {
        "key": "4",
        "label": "Lower 95% interval endpoint [-]",
        "type": "number",
        "format": fixed3,
        "nullText": "Below −3"
    },
    {
        "key": "5",
        "label": "Upper 95% interval endpoint [-]",
        "type": "number",
        "format": fixed3,
        "nullText": "Above 3"
    }
]);

let rows = vec![
    (
        "Likelihood",
        likelihood_status.clone(),
        likelihood_lambda,
        peak,
        lower,
        upper,
    ),
    (
        "Pearson correlation",
        pearson_status,
        pearson_lambda,
        pearson_llf,
        None,
        None,
    ),
];
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "estimates" })
);

let mut markers = vec![
    serde_json::json!({ "kind": "line", "axis": "x", "value": true_lambda, "label": "True λ₀" }),
    serde_json::json!({
        "kind": "line",
        "axis": "y",
        "value": peak - half_chi2,
        "label": "ℓ_p(λ̂) − 1.92"
    }),
];
if let (Some(from), Some(to)) = (lower, upper) {
    markers.push(serde_json::json!({
        "kind": "range",
        "axis": "x",
        "from": from,
        "to": to,
        "label": "95% likelihood interval"
    }));
}
emit_line_chart!(
    &vec![serde_json::json!({ "name": "Profile log-likelihood ℓ_p(λ)", "points": curve })],
    options = &serde_json::json!({
        "xLabel": "Box–Cox λ [-]",
        "yLabel": "ℓ_p(λ) (constant omitted) [-]",
        "annotations": markers
    })
);
export!(samples, samples);
export!(true_lambda, true_lambda);
export!(likelihood_lambda, likelihood_lambda);
export!(pearson_lambda, pearson_lambda);
let outcome = if converged {
    Outcome::satisfied("The likelihood-based search for λ̂ converged").explanation(if covered {
        "The 95% likelihood interval contains the true λ₀."
    } else {
        "For this sample, the 95% likelihood interval excludes the true λ₀."
    })
} else {
    Outcome::partial("The search stopped without converging")
        .explanation("The table's λ̂ is the value at termination and need not be the maximizer.")
};
emit_outcome!(
    outcome
        .status(likelihood_status)
        .diagnostic("λ̂", likelihood_lambda, None)
);

The interval also contains $\pm1/2$, so this sample alone cannot distinguish logarithmic and square-root transformations; the untransformed $\lambda=1$ lies outside it.
The correlation-based $\hat\lambda=-0.024$ is close to the likelihood estimate, with $\ell_p$ only $6.5\times10^{-4}$ below the maximum.
At $\lambda_0=0.5$, $\hat\lambda=0.468$ and the interval is $[-0.038,1.005]$.
At $n=400$, the interval shrinks to $[-0.154,0.218]$, about 0.35 times the width at $n=60$ and close to $\sqrt{60/400}\approx0.39$.
For the seed-21 sample, $\hat\lambda=-0.761$ and interval $[-1.353,-0.202]$ exclude the true value 0.
A 95% interval misses the true value in about 5% of samples in this way.
The example's $w$ has a small coefficient of variation $0.4/2=0.2$, and transformed distributions are close to normal for every $\lambda$, so the sample contains little information about $\lambda$.

## Normal probability plots and PPCC

### Filliben plotting positions

A normal probability plot (Q–Q plot) plots a sorted sample $x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}$ against normal quantiles $\Phi^{-1}(m_i)$.
If the sample follows a normal distribution $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then $x_{(i)}\approx\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(m_i)$, so points lie near a line with slope $\sigma$ and intercept $\mu$.
For a long right tail, upper-right points lie above the line; for short tails, points at both ends deviate inward from the line.

$m_i$ are plotting positions, a convention selecting representative values of the order statistics $U_{(i)}$ from $U(0,1)$.
Filliben (1975) used the following approximation to the medians of $U_{(i)}$:

$$
m_1=1-0.5^{1/n},\qquad m_n=0.5^{1/n},\qquad m_i=\frac{i-0.3175}{n+0.365}\quad(1<i<n)
$$


The endpoint values $m_1$ and $m_n$ are exactly the medians of $U_{(1)}$ and $U_{(n)}$.
Probability plots in `scioxide-stats` use these positions, rather than Blom's $(i-3/8)/(n+1/4)$ or Hazen's $(i-1/2)/n$.

### Probability plot correlation coefficient (PPCC)

The Pearson correlation $r$ of plotted points $(\Phi^{-1}(m_i),x_{(i)})$ is called the probability plot correlation coefficient (PPCC).
We have $r\le1$, approaching 1 as the points approach a straight line.
Filliben tabulated lower quantiles of the distribution of $r$ for samples from normal populations, producing a normality test that rejects for small $r$.
$r$ is invariant under location and scale changes, allowing shape comparisons without estimating $\mu$ or $\sigma$.

A power transformation can also select $\lambda$ to maximize the transformed sample's PPCC (`PowerFitMethod::Pearson`).
The likelihood criterion maximizes the normal-model likelihood, while the correlation criterion maximizes Q–Q linearity; the two $\hat\lambda$ generally differ, but both approach $\lambda_0$ when the normal model is correct.
The correlation criterion lacks a probabilistic interpretation like the likelihood interval, so use likelihood when an interval is needed.

`probplot(samples, model, parameters)` computes $Q(m_i)$ from the quantile function and parameters of distribution model `DistributionFitModel` (or a type implementing `FitModel`), returning a `ProbabilityPlot` pairing these quantiles with the sorted sample.
`theoretical()` is $Q(m_i)$, `ordered()` is the sorted sample, and `fit()` is `ProbabilityPlotFit` (least-squares slope `slope`, intercept `intercept`, and correlation `correlation`).
`correlation` is `None` when the sample is constant.
The computation consists of one sort costing $O(n\log n)$ and $n$ quantile evaluations.
Fewer than two observations returns `InvalidInput`; NaN or infinity returns `NonFinite`.
The detailed form `probplot_with_options` uses `max_bytes` in `StatisticsOptions` to limit the plot's two vectors, returning `ResourceLimit` if exceeded.

To select a distribution family's shape parameter (such as $\lambda$ for Tukey's lambda distribution or the Weibull shape) by maximizing PPCC, prepare the objective $-r(\theta)$ with `PreparedPpcc::new(samples, model, bounds, integer, options)` and maximize PPCC with `ppcc_max(problem, initial, solver)` (using `bounds` passed to `PreparedPpcc` for the bounds).
The detailed form `ppcc_max_with_options` and scoped form `ppcc_max_scoped` are also available.
`PreparedPpcc` sorts the sample once and computes model quantiles $n$ times per evaluation.

### Probability plot for the selected λ

## Summary

Select statistics with constructors of `Statistic`; undefined scalar-type and weight combinations are excluded at compile time.
L-moment ratios $t_3,t_4$ summarize shape without requiring higher moments; `LMomentNormalization` selects raw values or ratios.
Multidimensional reductions can allocate `StatisticsWorkspace` from shape alone and repeat with `statistics_into` and `standardize_axes_into`; specify the z-score convention with `ddof`.
Use Box–Cox for positive samples and Yeo–Johnson for samples including zeros or negative values to approach normality; report $\lambda$ with the profile-log-likelihood maximizer and likelihood interval down to $\ell_p(\hat\lambda)-1.92$.
Check transformed results with Filliben probability plots and PPCC; choosing a convenient $\lambda$ within the interval eases interpretation.
