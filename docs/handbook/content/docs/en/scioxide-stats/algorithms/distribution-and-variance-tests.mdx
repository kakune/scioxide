t tests and ANOVA assume normal samples or equal group variances.
The tests on this page take those assumptions themselves as null hypotheses.
The null hypotheses are “the sample follows the specified distribution $F$” for goodness-of-fit tests; “the sample follows some normal distribution” for normality tests; “the groups share a distribution” for two- and k-sample tests; and “the groups have equal variances (scales)” for equal-variance tests.
Failure to reject does not establish an assumption; small samples have low probability of detecting departures (power).
This page explains each test's null distribution and measures how actual rejection rates depart from the nominal $\alpha$ after estimating parameters or changing distribution shape.

All results are returned as `DistributionTestResult<S>`.
Alongside `statistic()` and `p_value()`, `method()` returns the null distribution used for the p value (`Exact`, `Asymptotic`, `Permutation { exact, replicates }`, or `MonteCarlo { replicates }` in `DistributionMethod`); `null_parameters()` returns null-parameter handling (`Known`, `Estimated`, or `Exchangeable` in `NullParameters`); and `observations()` and `groups()` return total observations and group count.
`monte_carlo_interval()` is a 95% Wilson interval for the upper-tail probability, supplied only for Monte Carlo and random-permutation p values.
Common settings `ClassicalOptions`, `TestCalibration` rules, and the relationship between one-shot and detailed forms match [Classical tests](oxref:stats-classical-results-and-common-settings).

## Goodness-of-fit tests based on the empirical distribution function

The empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of independent observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ is

$$
F_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf 1\{x_i \le x\}
$$


If the null distribution function $F$ is continuous and its parameters have not been estimated from data, $U_i = F(x_i)$ follows the uniform distribution $U(0, 1)$.
The null distribution of a statistic formed from differences between $F_n$ and $F$ therefore does not depend on $F$; once obtained, it can test any continuous distribution (the distribution-free property).
Below, $x_{(1)} \le \dots \le x_{(n)}$ denotes the sorted sample.

### Kolmogorov–Smirnov test

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic is the maximum difference between $F_n$ and $F$.

$$
D^+ = \max_i\left(\frac{i}{n} - F(x_{(i)})\right), \qquad
D^- = \max_i\left(F(x_{(i)}) - \frac{i-1}{n}\right), \qquad
D = \max(D^+, D^-)
$$

`ks_one_sample_statistic(x, cdf)` returns the pair $(D^+, D^-)$.
The detailed form `ks_one_sample_statistic_with_options` uses only the sorted-copy workspace limit `max_workspace_bytes` from `ClassicalOptions`.
If `cdf` returns a value outside $[0, 1]$ or decreases along the sorted sample, it returns `InvalidInput`.

According to the alternative, `ks_one_sample_test(x, cdf)` uses $D^+$ for `Greater`, $D^-$ for `Less`, and $D$ for two-sided tests.
Exact null distributions use the Birnbaum–Tingey sum for one-sided tests and powers of a Durbin matrix (the Marsaglia–Tsang–Wang method) for two-sided tests.
`Auto` uses exact distributions when $n^2$ is at most `max_work` ($n \le 316$ by default); larger samples use the Kolmogorov distribution of $\sqrt n D$ for two-sided tests and the asymptotic approximation $\exp(-2nD^2)$ for one-sided tests.
Computing the two-sided exact distribution takes $O(m^3 \log n)$ work for Durbin matrix dimension $m = 2\lfloor nD \rfloor + 1$; `max_work` also limits this work.
Thus, even when `Auto` selects an exact distribution, large $nD$ can exceed the work limit (for example, samples with $n = 200$ and $D \approx 0.12$ under the default limit).
With `Auto`, exhausting `max_work` or `max_workspace_bytes` during exact evaluation switches to the asymptotic approximation, and the result's `method()` reports `Asymptotic`.
Large $nD$ gives a small p value, so switching to an asymptotic approximation rarely changes the test conclusion.
A request for `Exact` instead returns `ConvergenceFailure` or `ResourceLimit` without switching; increase `with_max_work` limits through the detailed form `ks_one_sample_test_with_options`.
The detailed form also changes the alternative and calibration.
The null distribution assumes continuous $F$, so tied samples return `InvalidInput`.

### Cramér–von Mises and Anderson–Darling tests

KS considers only the maximum difference, making it sensitive to differences near the center and less sensitive to tail differences.
Statistics integrating squared differences over the whole distribution take the form

$$
T = n\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \bigl(F_n(x) - F(x)\bigr)^2\, \psi\bigl(F(x)\bigr)\, dF(x)
$$

Weight $\psi(u) = 1$ gives the Cramér–von Mises (CvM) statistic $W^2$, and $\psi(u) = 1/(u(1-u))$ gives the Anderson–Darling (AD) statistic $A^2$.
For a sorted sample they have the following forms.

$$
W^2 = \frac{1}{12n} + \sum_{i=1}^n \left(F(x_{(i)}) - \frac{2i-1}{2n}\right)^2, \qquad
A^2 = -n - \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (2i-1)\left[\ln F(x_{(i)}) + \ln\bigl(1 - F(x_{(n+1-i)})\bigr)\right]
$$

`cramer_von_mises_statistic(x, cdf)` returns $W^2$, while `cramer_von_mises_test(x, cdf)` sums the limiting distribution of $W^2$ using the Csörgő–Faraway series to compute a p value.
The series is summed until a term falls below $10^{-12}$; exceeding `max_work` terms returns `ConvergenceFailure`.
No exact finite-sample distribution is used, so `Exact` returns `InvalidInput`; only two-sided alternatives are accepted.
The detailed form `cramer_von_mises_statistic_with_options` changes only the workspace limit; `cramer_von_mises_test_with_options` additionally changes the series-term limit `max_work`.

`anderson_darling_statistic(x, log_cdf, log_survival)` returns $A^2$.
Accepting $\ln F$ and $\ln(1 - F)$ directly avoids divergent logarithms when $F$ rounds to 0 or 1 in the tails; both must be finite at every observation.
No function returns an AD-statistic p value; obtain it with `goodness_of_fit_test_into` in the next section.
The detailed form `anderson_darling_statistic_with_options` uses only the workspace limit.

### Calibration after parameter estimation

Estimating parameters of $F$ (such as normal mean and variance) from the same sample moves $F$ toward the sample, reducing its difference from $F_n$; the fixed-parameter null distribution is no longer valid.
Using estimated parameters in a KS test gives overly large p values and an actual rejection rate far below the nominal $\alpha$ (the Lilliefors problem).
In the normality-test cell below, the KS test estimating mean and standard deviation from normal samples of $n = 40$ has a rejection rate near 0 rather than 0.05.

Use `goodness_of_fit_test_into` for goodness-of-fit tests with estimated parameters.
This function accepts a parameter family through `GoodnessOfFitModel` (log CDF, log survival function, a sampling function, and a parameter-fitting function `fit`); select the statistic from `KolmogorovSmirnov`, `CramerVonMises`, and `AndersonDarling` in `GoodnessOfFitStatistic`.
It estimates parameters $\hat\theta$ from observations, repeatedly draws an equal-size sample from $F(\cdot\,; \hat\theta)$, re-estimates parameters, and computes the statistic (parametric bootstrap).
The fraction of repeated statistics at least as large as observed is the p value; refitting each repetition produces a null distribution including estimation effects.
The result's `GoodnessOfFitResult` contains `DistributionTestResult` (`method()` is `MonteCarlo` and `null_parameters()` is `Estimated`) only after all repetitions finish, and also returns the repetition report.
The caller supplies the plan `ResamplingPlan`, output, workspace, and execution context; there is no one-shot form.
Plan construction appears in [Resampling](../resampling/).

## Dedicated normality tests

Normality tests restrict the null to a “normal distribution with unknown mean and variance,” giving greater power than general goodness-of-fit tests.
All use null distributions with estimated parameters, and `null_parameters()` returns `Estimated`.

### Shapiro–Wilk test

The Shapiro–Wilk statistic divides the squared weighted sum of a sorted sample $x_{(i)}$ by the sum of squared deviations.

$$
W = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i x_{(i)}\right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2}
$$

Weights $a_i$ are determined by $a = m^\top V^{-1}/\lVert m^\top V^{-1}\rVert$ from the expected standard-normal order statistics $m$ and their covariance matrix $V$.
The numerator is proportional to the squared best linear unbiased estimate of the normal probability plot's slope (the plot of $x_{(i)}$ against $m_i$), while the denominator is proportional to a variance estimate, so $W$ is a ratio of two variance estimators.
For normal samples, $W$ is close to 1; skewness or heavy tails reduce it.
`shapiro_wilk_statistic(x)` computes $W$ using Royston's approximate weights $a_i$ and returns the value in $(0, 1]$.
The sample is shifted by its minimum and divided by its range before computation, so $W$ remains invariant under translation and scaling even for values near overflow.
The cost is $O(n \log n)$; `shapiro_wilk_statistic_with_options` changes only the workspace limit for $n$ values and $n/2$ weights.

`shapiro_wilk_test(x)` maps $W$ to an approximate standard-normal variate with Royston's (1992) normalizing transformation to compute the p value.
For $n = 3$, the null distribution of $W$ has the closed form $p = 1 - (6/\pi)\arccos\sqrt W$, which is used directly.
The transformation is valid for $3 \le n \le 5000$; outside that range it returns `InvalidInput`.
For $n > 5000$, compute $W$ with `shapiro_wilk_statistic`, then draw normal samples to construct a Monte Carlo null distribution of $W$.
`Exact` and one-sided alternatives return `InvalidInput`.
Tied samples are accepted, but many ties, as in rounded measurements, reduce $W$ and cause excessive rejection of normality.

### Skewness, kurtosis, and Jarque–Bera tests

Let $m_k$ denote the order-$k$ sample central moment; some tests use sample skewness $\sqrt{b_1} = m_3/m_2^{3/2}$ and sample kurtosis $b_2 = m_4/m_2^2$.
For a normal distribution, the population value of $\sqrt{b_1}$ is 0 and that of $b_2$ is 3.

- `skew_test(x)` maps $\sqrt{b_1}$ to an approximately standard-normal variate $Z_s$ with D'Agostino's transformation, using normal tail probabilities as p values.
  It requires $n \ge 8$ and accepts one-sided alternatives.
- `kurtosis_test(x)` standardizes $b_2$ with its exact normal-sample mean and variance, then applies the Anscombe–Glynn transformation to obtain $Z_k$.
  It requires $n \ge 5$, and use of the approximation is recommended for $n \ge 20$.
- `normality_test(x)` is the D'Agostino–Pearson omnibus test, comparing $K^2 = Z_s^2 + Z_k^2$ with a chi-squared distribution with two degrees of freedom.
  It requires $n \ge 8$.
- `jarque_bera_test(x)` compares $\mathrm{JB} = \frac n6\left(b_1 + (b_2 - 3)^2/4\right)$ with a chi-squared distribution with two degrees of freedom.
  This asymptotic distribution is accurate only for large samples; around $n = 40$, the rejection rate is below nominal.

Detailed forms `skew_test_with_options`, `kurtosis_test_with_options`, `normality_test_with_options`, `jarque_bera_test_with_options`, and `shapiro_wilk_test_with_options` can change the alternative (only skewness and kurtosis tests) and workspace limit; all have only asymptotic distributions and reject `Exact`.

### Rejection rates and power of normality tests

The next cell mixes standard-normal and centered-exponential variables:

$$
X = \sqrt{1 - p}\, Z + \sqrt{p}\,(E - 1), \qquad Z \sim N(0, 1),\ E \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1)
$$

For each mixing proportion $p = 0, 0.1, \dots, 0.6$, it draws samples of size $n$ from this distribution $R$ times and computes the proportion rejecting normality at $\alpha = 0.05$.
$X$ has mean 0, variance 1, skewness $2p^{3/2}$, and excess kurtosis $6p^2$.
At $p = 0$ the null is true, so the rejection rate represents actual test size.
At $p > 0$ the rejection rate is power; the fixed-parameter KS and CvM null $N(0, 1)$ also matches mean and variance, differing only in shape.
The KS test uses the one-shot `ks_one_sample_test`, with asymptotic p values in repetitions whose Durbin matrix work exceeds the default limit.
The tests compared are Shapiro–Wilk, D'Agostino–Pearson $K^2$, Jarque–Bera, skewness and kurtosis tests, KS and CvM tests against $N(0, 1)$, and a KS test against $N(\bar x, s^2)$ with mean and standard deviation estimated from the sample.
The table shows rejection rates at the selected mixing proportion and their Clopper–Pearson 95% intervals (intervals from `binomial_test`).

The $p = 0$ row shows test size; intervals for Shapiro–Wilk and fixed-parameter KS and CvM contain 0.05.

KS mainly considers central distribution differences, giving low power against tail-shape differences (skewness and kurtosis).

## Comparing two and k sample distributions

To test whether independent groups share a distribution, group labels are exchangeable under the null, enabling an exact distribution by enumerating label assignments.
The result's `null_parameters()` returns `Exchangeable`.

- `ks_two_sample_test(x, y)` uses the maximum difference between ECDFs $F_n$ and $G_m$ ($\max_t(F_n - G_m)$ for `Greater`, $\max_t(G_m - F_n)$ for `Less`, and the larger for two-sided tests).
  Exact p values count lattice paths in $O(nm)$ time and $O(m)$ workspace; `Auto` uses them when $nm$ is at most `max_work`.
  Above the limit, two-sided tests use the Kolmogorov distribution of $\sqrt{nm/(n+m)}\,D$ and one-sided tests use the approximation $\exp(-2nmD^2/(n+m))$.
  Both assume continuous distributions and return `InvalidInput` for ties.
  For tied or discrete data, use `ks_two_sample_permutation_test` (borrowed form `ks_two_sample_permutation_test_into`, result `KsPermutationResult`) to compute permutation p values.
- `cramer_von_mises_two_sample_test(x, y)` uses the two-sample CvM statistic $T = U/(nmN) - (4nm - 1)/(6N)$ ($U$ is the sum of squared differences between each group's sorted midranks and expected positions, $N = n + m$).
  `Auto` enumerates all assignments when $\binom{N}{n}$ is within the limit (exact even with ties), and otherwise uses an approximation to Anderson's limiting distribution.
  The approximation assumes untied data and returns `InvalidInput` when ties occur.
  Only two-sided alternatives are accepted.
- `epps_singleton_two_sample_test(x, y, points)` compares real and imaginary differences between group empirical characteristic functions at two frequencies `points` (usually `[0.4, 0.8]`), scaled by the pooled sample's semi-interquartile range.
  The statistic is a quadratic form with the inverse estimated covariance of the differences; it uses a chi-squared reference distribution with four degrees of freedom and the Epps–Singleton small-sample correction when both groups have fewer than 25 observations.
  It does not use ranks and therefore also applies to discrete data with many ties.
  Each group needs at least five observations; a numerically singular estimated covariance returns `Singular`.
- `anderson_k_sample_test(groups)` is the Scholz–Stephens k-sample Anderson–Darling test, with statistic $(A^2 - (k-1))/\sigma_n$ ($\sigma_n^2$ is the finite-sample null variance).
  Its p value is log-linearly interpolated from critical values at significance levels 0.25, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 0.01, 0.005, and 0.001, and clipped to $[0.001, 0.25]$.
  Endpoint values therefore indicate only a p-value range; use permutations if an exact p value is needed.
  Select tie handling with `AndersonKVariant`: `Midrank` (the one-shot default, for continuous and discrete data), `Right` (right-continuous ECDF, for discrete distributions), or `Continuous` (returns `InvalidInput` for ties).

Detailed forms `ks_two_sample_test_with_options` and `cramer_von_mises_two_sample_test_with_options` change the alternative (two-sided only for CvM), calibration, `max_work`, and workspace limit; `epps_singleton_two_sample_test_with_options` and `anderson_k_sample_test_with_options` have only asymptotic distributions and reject `Exact`.

## Tests of scale and variance homogeneity

Tests of equal group variances (scales) differ in whether they assume normality.

- `bartlett_test(groups)` compares the corrected normal-population likelihood-ratio statistic

  $$
  B = \frac{(N-k)\ln s_p^2 - \sum_i (n_i - 1)\ln s_i^2}{1 + \dfrac{1}{3(k-1)}\left(\sum_i \dfrac{1}{n_i - 1} - \dfrac{1}{N-k}\right)}
  $$

  with a chi-squared distribution with $k - 1$ degrees of freedom.
  $s_i^2$ is the unbiased variance of group $i$, $s_p^2$ is the pooled variance, and $N = \sum_i n_i$.
  The null distribution of $B$ depends on the normal fourth moment, so heavy-tailed or skewed distributions give actual rejection rates far above $\alpha$ even with equal variances (cell below).
- `levene_test(groups)` applies a one-way ANOVA F test to each observation's absolute deviation $|x_{ij} - c_i|$ from its group center, using the upper tail of an F distribution with $(k-1, N-k)$ degrees of freedom as the p value.
  Select the center with `VarianceCenter`.
  `Mean` (Levene's original test) suits symmetric light-tailed distributions; `Median` (Brown–Forsythe, the one-shot default) has stable rejection rates even for skewed distributions.
  `Trimmed { proportion }` uses the mean remaining after removing $\lfloor pn \rfloor$ observations at each group endpoint as the center, and also excludes those observations from deviation calculations and degrees of freedom.
  Medians and trimmed means use linear-time selection, giving cost $O(N)$.
- `fligner_test(groups)` is the Fligner–Killeen test: rank absolute deviations from group centers across all groups as $r$, replace them with normal scores $a = \Phi^{-1}(1/2 + r/(2(N+1)))$, and compare the variation $X^2 = \sum_i n_i(\bar a_i - \bar a)^2/s^2$ of group mean scores with a chi-squared distribution with $k - 1$ degrees of freedom.
  Select the center with the same `VarianceCenter` as Levene.
  The Conover–Johnson–Johnson (1981) comparison identifies it as a test with stable rejection rates and high power for nonnormal distributions.
- `mood_scale_test(x, y)` and `ansari_bradley_test(x, y)` are two-group scale rank tests.
  For pooled midranks $r$, Mood assigns scores $(r - (N+1)/2)^2$ and Ansari–Bradley assigns $\min(r, N+1-r)$; the statistic $z$ standardizes the sum of scores in $x$ by the null mean and variance computed from actual scores.
  $z > 0$ indicates greater spread in $x$, and one-sided alternatives follow this orientation.
  `Auto` enumerates all assignments when $\binom{N}{n}$ is within the limit and otherwise uses a normal approximation to $z$.
  Both assume equal group locations (medians); location shifts cannot be distinguished from scale differences.

Detailed forms `bartlett_test_with_options`, `levene_test_with_options(groups, center, options)`, and `fligner_test_with_options(groups, center, options)` have only chi-squared or F approximations and reject `Exact` and one-sided alternatives.
`mood_scale_test_with_options` and `ansari_bradley_test_with_options` can change the alternative, calibration, and `max_work`.

## Median test

`median_test(groups)` is Mood's median test, testing equal medians in $k$ independent groups.
It classifies observations above or below the pooled median to form a $2 \times k$ contingency table, then compares Pearson's chi-squared statistic with a chi-squared distribution with $k - 1$ degrees of freedom.
Select treatment of observations equal to the pooled median with `MedianTies`: `Below` (the one-shot default) counts them below, `Above` counts them above, and `Ignore` omits them from the table.
For two groups, Yates' continuity correction is applied when `ClassicalOptions::continuity` is `HalfUnit`.
The detailed form `median_test_with_options(groups, ties, options)` changes tie handling and continuity correction; it rejects `Exact` and one-sided alternatives.
The result `MedianTestOutcome` has public fields for the chi-squared test result `test`, pooled median `grand_median`, and each group's counts `table` of `[above, below]`.
The median test compares only location but reduces observations to two values, giving lower power than rank tests under normality.

## Batch execution and result types

To apply the same distribution test to many rows, pass the test kind `DistributionBatchTest`, matrix view (and second view `y` for two-sample tests), output, and `DistributionBatchWorkspace` to `distribution_tests_into`.
`DistributionBatchTest` comprises `Skew`, `Kurtosis`, `Normality`, `JarqueBera`, `Shapiro`, `KsOne(cdf)`, `CramerVonMisesOne(cdf)`, `KsTwo`, `CramerVonMisesTwo`, `EppsSingleton(points)`, `Mood`, `AnsariBradley`, `Bartlett`, `Levene(center)`, `Fligner(center)`, and `AndersonK(variant)`, each calling the detailed form of the corresponding test.
Batched equal-variance and k-sample tests are restricted to two groups (rows of `x` and `y`).
Workspace holds row copies and results, but each test also allocates its own workspace internally.

## Example: rejection rates for groups from the same distribution

Measure how assumption failures affect variance-homogeneity and equal-distribution tests using data under true null hypotheses.
The next cell draws three groups of size $n$ from the same distribution $R$ times, recording the fraction rejected by each test at $\alpha = 0.05$.
All three groups share a distribution, so every null is true and a calibrated test's rejection rate is close to 0.05.
Two-sample tests use the first two groups.
Choose normal, exponential (right-skewed), or t with three degrees of freedom (heavy-tailed, without a fourth moment) as the population.
The table's “Null distribution” is the `DistributionMethod` used in the first repetition; tests using exact distributions show `Exact`.

```rust
//| id: stats-distribution-and-variance-tests-api-1
//| ref: stats-distribution-and-variance-tests-api-1
//| title: "Measure rejection rates for equal-variance and distribution-comparison tests on groups from the same distribution"
//| run: button
//| crates: [scioxide-random, scioxide-stats]
//| inputs:
//|   population: {"type":"select","label":"Population","options":[{"label":"Normal N(0, 1)","value":"normal"},{"label":"Exponential Exp(1)","value":"exponential"},{"label":"t distribution (3 degrees of freedom)","value":"student"}],"value":"normal"}
//|   group_size: {"type":"range","label":"Size per group n [points]","min":10,"max":40,"step":5,"value":15}
//|   replicate_count: {"type":"range","label":"Replicate count R [count]","min":500,"max":2000,"step":500,"value":1000}
//|   seed: {"type":"integer","label":"Random seed","min":0,"max":100000,"value":5}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"skewed","label":"Exponential distribution","description":"For skewed distributions, Bartlett and mean-centered Levene reject at several times nominal even with equal variances.","inputs":{"population":"exponential"}}
//|   - {"id":"heavy-tail","label":"t distribution (3 degrees of freedom)","description":"For heavy tails, Bartlett's rejection rate exceeds 0.3, while median-centered Levene and Fligner–Killeen remain near nominal.","inputs":{"population":"student"}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/0/3","approx":{"value":0.044,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/3/3","approx":{"value":0.123,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/8/2","equals":"Exact"}
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/9/2","equals":"Asymptotic"}
//|   - case: skewed
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/0/3","approx":{"value":0.318,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/1/3","approx":{"value":0.171,"atol":1e-12}}
//|   - case: heavy-tail
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/0/3","approx":{"value":0.388,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/2/3","approx":{"value":0.043,"atol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"sizes","path":"/rows/4/3","approx":{"value":0.041,"atol":1e-12}}
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Exponential, Normal, SeedableRng, SplitMix64, StudentT};
use scioxide_stats::*;

let (alpha, n, replicates) = (0.05, group_size as usize, replicate_count as usize);
let normal = Normal::<f64>::new(0.0, 1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let exponential = Exponential::<f64>::new(1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let student = StudentT::<f64>::new(3.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut rng = SplitMix64::from_seed(seed as u64);
let names = [
    ("bartlett_test", "Equal variances"),
    ("levene_test (Mean)", "Equal variances"),
    ("levene_test (Median)", "Equal variances"),
    ("levene_test (Trimmed 0.1)", "Equal variances"),
    ("fligner_test (Median)", "Equal variances"),
    ("mood_scale_test", "Equal scales"),
    ("ansari_bradley_test", "Equal scales"),
    ("median_test", "Equal medians"),
    ("ks_two_sample_test", "Equal distributions"),
    ("cramer_von_mises_two_sample_test", "Equal distributions"),
    ("epps_singleton_two_sample_test", "Equal distributions"),
    ("anderson_k_sample_test", "Equal distributions"),
];
let o = ClassicalOptions::default();
let trimmed = VarianceCenter::Trimmed { proportion: 0.1 };
let mut groups = vec![vec![0.0; n]; 3];
let mut counts = [0_usize; 12];
let mut methods = vec![String::new(); 12];
for replicate in 0..replicates {
    for value in groups.iter_mut().flatten() {
        *value = match population.as_str() {
            "normal" => normal.sample(&mut rng),
            "exponential" => exponential.sample(&mut rng),
            _ => student.sample(&mut rng),
        }
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    }
    let k: Vec<&[f64]> = groups.iter().map(Vec::as_slice).collect();

    let results = [
        bartlett_test_with_options(&k, o),
        levene_test_with_options(&k, VarianceCenter::Mean, o),
        levene_test_with_options(&k, VarianceCenter::Median, o),
        levene_test_with_options(&k, trimmed, o),
        fligner_test_with_options(&k, VarianceCenter::Median, o),
        mood_scale_test_with_options(x, y, o),
        ansari_bradley_test_with_options(x, y, o),
        median_test_with_options(&k, MedianTies::Below, o).map(|outcome| outcome.test),
        ks_two_sample_test_with_options(x, y, o),
        cramer_von_mises_two_sample_test_with_options(x, y, o),
        epps_singleton_two_sample_test_with_options(x, y, [0.4, 0.8], o),

        counts[t] += usize::from(result.p_value() < alpha);
        if replicate == 0 {
            methods[t] = format!("{:?}", result.method());
        }
    }
}
let mut rows = Vec::new();
for (t, (name, null)) in names.into_iter().enumerate() {
    let check = binomial_test_with_options(counts[t], replicates, alpha, o)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let interval = check.interval().ok_or("Interval is absent")?;
    let rate = counts[t] as f64 / replicates as f64;
    rows.push((
        name,
        null,
        methods[t].clone(),
        rate,
        interval.lower(),
        interval.upper(),
    ));
}

let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Test" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Null hypothesis" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Null distribution" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Proportion rejected [-]", "type": "number", "format": f3 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Lower 95% interval endpoint [-]", "type": "number", "format": f3 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Upper 95% interval endpoint [-]", "type": "number", "format": f3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "sizes" })
```

For normal populations, most test intervals, including Bartlett's, contain 0.05 or lie below it (conservative).
Median-centered Levene and Fligner–Killeen fall to around 0.03 at $n = 15$, and the exact two-sample KS rejection rate is below nominal because its statistic is discrete.
Levene with `Trimmed`, which removes observations at both ends, rejects at more than twice nominal even for normal populations.
This variant also excludes removed observations from deviation calculations and degrees of freedom.
The Brown–Forsythe (1974) trimmed-mean variant uses a trimmed mean only for the center and deviations of all observations; scioxide-stats does not provide that variant.
For exponential and t distributions, Bartlett rejection rates exceed 0.3 (0.32 and 0.39), while mean-centered Levene gives 0.17 and 0.08.
Median-centered Levene and Fligner–Killeen rejection rates remain at or below 0.07 regardless of population shape; rank-based Mood and Ansari–Bradley, median, and equal-distribution tests also remain near or below nominal.

## Summary

ECDF goodness-of-fit null distributions are distribution-free only for continuous, fixed-parameter $F$; after fitting parameters from the sample, use parametric-bootstrap calibration with `goodness_of_fit_test_into` instead.
Shapiro–Wilk and $K^2$ have high power against skewness and heavy tails; Jarque–Bera's asymptotic distribution gives below-nominal rejection rates for samples of a few dozen points.
For two- and k-sample tests, `Auto` switches between exact enumeration and asymptotic approximation according to state count; use permutations or midrank variants for tied data.
Bartlett's variance-homogeneity test overrejects for nonnormal distributions; when normality cannot be established, use median-centered Levene or Fligner–Killeen.
