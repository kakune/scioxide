When several quantities are measured on each individual (multivariate data), analysis concerns how variables move together as well as their means and variances.
The fundamental quantity is the covariance matrix; PCA, CCA, multivariate regression, and discriminant analysis all begin with its estimation.
When variable count $p$ is not small relative to sample size $n$, sample-covariance eigenvalues spread and its inverse becomes unstable.

This page explains four `scioxide-stats` capabilities in the order of definitions, estimation, error and cost, APIs, and experiments.

- Sample covariance matrices and frequency-weighted covariance matrices
- Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage
- Principal component analysis (PCA) and whitening
- Canonical correlation analysis (CCA)

Matrix rows are observations (individuals), and columns are variables.
Write the $n \times p$ data matrix as $X$ and its row $i$ as $x_i^{\mathsf T}$.
Assume rows are independent and identically distributed.
For dependent rows, as in time series, the estimator-variance formulas on this page do not hold.

## Sample covariance matrix

### Definition and centering

With column-$j$ mean $\bar x_j = \frac1n \sum_i x_{ij}$, entries of sample covariance $S$ are

$$
S_{jk} = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)(x_{ik} - \bar x_k)
$$


Divisor $n - 1$ compensates for degrees of freedom lost to mean estimation, giving $E[S] = \Sigma$.
For complex data, conjugate the first factor to make $S$ Hermitian.
$S$ is positive semidefinite with rank at most $\min(n - 1, p)$.
Thus, when $p \ge n$, $S$ is necessarily singular and has no inverse.

A two-pass calculation first computing and subtracting means is more stable than the one-pass sum formula $\sum x^2 - n\bar x^2$; however, subtracting rounded means $\mathrm{fl}(\bar x_j)$ loses low-order deviation digits in columns with large common offsets, such as small fluctuations around $10^{8}$.
`scioxide-stats` computes column means by compensated summation and centers values by subtracting compensated means (sums of two `f64` values), rather than rounded means.
Real products use compensated summation; strongly cancelling entries or entries outside the exponent range of `f64` are recomputed using an extended-exponent-range representation.
Returned mean vectors are rounded to the scalar type, but covariance is unaffected by that rounding.

Time cost is $O(np^2)$ and memory cost is $O(np + p^2)$.

### Frequency weights

Data with repeated identical rows can be represented by distinct rows and occurrence counts $c_i$ (frequency weights).
With $N = \sum_i c_i \ge 2$, the following matrix

$$
\bar x_j = \frac{1}{N} \sum_i c_i\, x_{ij},
\qquad
C_{jk} = \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_i c_i\, (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)(x_{ik} - \bar x_k)
$$

equals the sample covariance of data repeating each row $c_i$ times.
Divisor $N - 1$ is correct only for frequency weights.
Do not use this formula for reliability or precision weights (analytical weights), whose effective sample size differs from $\sum c_i$.
`frequency_covariance(data, weights)` and the memory-limited form `frequency_covariance_with_options(data, weights, options)` accept only `Weights<_, Frequency>` and reject other weight models at compile time.
A weight count differing from the row count, weights unrepresentable as integers, or $N < 2$ returns `InvalidInput`.
Zero-weight rows do not contribute, but their values must still be finite.

## Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage

### Sample covariance in high dimensions

When $p/n$ is not close to 0, eigenvalues of $S$ spread more than those of true covariance $\Sigma$.
For example, with $\Sigma = I$ and $p/n \to c \in (0, 1)$, the eigenvalue distribution of $S$ approaches the Marchenko–Pastur distribution with support $[(1 - \sqrt c)^2, (1 + \sqrt c)^2]$.
Even at $c = 1/2$, eigenvalues spread from 0.09 to 2.9 rather than concentrating around the true value 1.
Largest eigenvalues are too large and smallest too small, so $S$ has an inflated condition number and estimates using $S^{-1}$ (Mahalanobis distances, discriminant analysis, portfolio weights) have larger errors.

### Shrinkage estimator and optimal intensity

Shrinkage pulls $S$ toward a simple target matrix.
Ledoit–Wolf uses a scaled identity target $\mu I$ ($\mu = \mathrm{tr}\,S/p$) and defines

$$
\hat\Sigma = (1 - \alpha)\, S + \alpha\, \mu I,
\qquad 0 \le \alpha \le 1
$$


Eigenvectors match $S$, while each eigenvalue $\lambda$ becomes $(1 - \alpha)\lambda + \alpha\mu$.
Eigenvalues shrink toward their mean $\mu$; if $\alpha > 0$ and $\mu > 0$, $\hat\Sigma$ is positive definite.

Intensity $\alpha$ minimizes expected squared Frobenius error $E\|\hat\Sigma - \Sigma\|_F^2$.
The optimum depends on $\Sigma$, so Ledoit and Wolf (2004) replace it with consistently estimable quantities.
From centered rows $x_i$, `scioxide-stats` computes

$$
S_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i x_i x_i^{\mathsf T},
\quad
\mu = \frac{\mathrm{tr}\,S_n}{p},
\quad
\delta = \|S_n - \mu I\|_F^2,
\quad
\bar\beta = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_i \|x_i x_i^{\mathsf T} - S_n\|_F^2,
\quad
\alpha = \min\!\left(\frac{\bar\beta}{\delta},\, 1\right)
$$

(using $\alpha = 1$ if $\delta = 0$).
$\delta$ measures how far $S$ is from the target, while $\bar\beta$ measures estimation error in $S$ itself; larger estimation error produces stronger shrinkage.
Since $\bar\beta \ge 0$, $\alpha$ is nonnegative, and only its upper side is clipped at 1.
The returned matrix $\frac{n}{n-1}\bigl[(1 - \alpha) S_n + \alpha\mu I\bigr]$ restores divisor $n - 1$, equaling $S$ shrunk with the same $\alpha$.
The estimator assumes no normal distribution, requiring only finite fourth moments.
Constant data or data with equal variance in every direction give $\alpha = 1$.
Fourth-order sums and ratios use an extended exponent range, so scaling all data leaves $\alpha$ unchanged.

The only target is $\mu I$; diagonal or constant-correlation targets, nonlinear shrinkage, Oracle Approximating Shrinkage (OAS), and graphical lasso estimation of sparse precision matrices are not provided.
If intensity is chosen separately, for example by cross-validation, `shrink_covariance(covariance, intensity)` shrinks a known covariance toward $\mu I$.
For outlier-resistant covariance, use `PreparedHuberScatter` (Huber's M-estimator).

## Covariance estimation and shrinkage APIs

| Function | Returned value | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| `sample_covariance(data)` | (Column means, $S$) | $O(np^2)$ |
| `ledoit_wolf_covariance(data)` |  ($\alpha$, $\hat\Sigma$) | $O(np^2)$ |
| `shrink_covariance(covariance, intensity)` | $(1 - \alpha) S + \alpha\mu I$ | $O(p^2)$ |
| `frequency_covariance(data, weights)` | (Weighted means, $C$) | $O(np^2)$ |

`sample_covariance` and `ledoit_wolf_covariance` are one-shot forms accepting `MatrixView`; use `sample_covariance_with_options` and `ledoit_wolf_covariance_with_options` to pass memory limits and numerical-rank policies through [`MultivariateOptions`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.MultivariateOptions.html).
Covariance estimation uses only the memory limit, not the rank policy.
Fewer than two rows, no columns, or row counts not exactly representable by the scalar (above $2^{53}$ for `f64`) return `InvalidInput`; NaN or infinity returns `NonFinite`, overflowing scalar entries `NumericalInstability`, and memory beyond the limit `ResourceLimit`.
`shrink_covariance` returns `InvalidInput` for nonsquare or not exactly Hermitian matrices, negative diagonal entries, or intensity outside $[0, 1]$.
At $\alpha = 0$ it returns the original matrix; at $\alpha = 1$ it returns $\mu I$.

### Shrinkage experiment

The next block defines the model shared by this page's first two cells.
Model $p$ sensors in a line, with stronger correlations between nearer sensors, using a multivariate normal distribution with correlation matrix $\Sigma_{jk} = \rho^{|j-k|}$.
`correlated_rows` generates each row with the across-variable AR(1) recurrence $z_1 = e_1$ and $z_j = \rho z_{j-1} + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\, e_j$ ($e_j$ are independent standard normals), whose covariance is exactly $\rho^{|j-k|}$.
Values use standardized units with variance 1.
`toeplitz` returns the true $\Sigma$.

```rust
//| id: stats-multivariate-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "Model of data with stronger correlations between neighboring variables"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-linalg, scioxide-random]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, RealScalar};
use scioxide_linalg::Matrix;
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Normal, SeedableRng, Xoshiro256StarStar};

/// Draw n rows and p columns from a normal distribution with Σ_jk = ρ^|j−k| (rows are observations).
pub(crate) fn correlated_rows(
    seed: u64,
    n: usize,
    p: usize,
    rho: f64,
) -> Result<Matrix<f64>, SciOxideError> {
    let mut rng = Xoshiro256StarStar::from_seed(seed);
    let normal = Normal::new(0.0, 1.0)?;
    let innovation = RealScalar::sqrt(1.0 - rho * rho);
    let mut data = Vec::with_capacity(n * p);
    for _ in 0..n {
        let mut z = normal.sample(&mut rng)?;
        data.push(z);
        for _ in 1..p {
            z = rho * z + innovation * normal.sample(&mut rng)?;
            data.push(z);
        }
    }
    Matrix::from_vec(n, p, data)
}

/// True covariance Σ_jk = ρ^|j−k|.
pub(crate) fn toeplitz(p: usize, rho: f64) -> Result<Matrix<f64>, SciOxideError> {
    let entries = (0..p * p).map(|i| RealScalar::powi(rho, (i / p).abs_diff(i % p) as i32));
    Matrix::from_vec(p, p, entries.collect())
}
```

let spectrum = |m: &Matrix<f64>| -> Result<Vec<f64>, String> {
    let eigen = HermitianEigenDecomposition::decompose(m, HermitianInput::Exact)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let mut values = eigen.eigenvalues().to_vec();
    values.sort_by(|a, b| b.total_cmp(a));

};

    let squares = a
        .as_slice()
        .iter()
        .zip(b.as_slice())
        .map(|(x, y)| (x - y) * (x - y));

// Independent check 1: compute α = min(β̄/δ, 1) directly from centered rows
let at = |i: usize, j: usize| data.as_slice()[i * p + j];
let means: Vec<f64> = (0..p)
    .map(|j| (0..n).map(|i| at(i, j)).sum::<f64>() / n as f64)
    .collect();
let centered = |i: usize, j: usize| at(i, j) - means[j];

};

Sample covariance $S$ has eigenvalues spread from 0.095 to 3.61 and condition number 38.
Ledoit–Wolf intensity is $\alpha = 0.494$, shrinking eigenvalues to 0.522–2.30 and condition number to 4.4.
Frobenius difference from $\Sigma$ is 2.78 for $S$ versus 2.31 for $\hat\Sigma$, a 17 % error reduction through shrinkage.
The shrunk condition number 4.4 is below the true 8.6, with both endpoint eigenvalues overly shrunk.
Ledoit–Wolf chooses intensity to reduce Frobenius error, so it need not accurately estimate condition numbers or individual eigenvalues.
All three check values are at most $3 \times 10^{-16}$, confirming the $\alpha$ formula, $\hat\Sigma = $ `shrink_covariance(S, α)`, and agreement between frequency weights and replicated rows.

At $\rho = 0$ (`identity`), true $\Sigma$ is the identity, but eigenvalues of $S$ spread from 0.17 to 2.42.
These lie inside the Marchenko–Pastur support $[0.086, 2.91]$ for $c = p/n = 0.5$.
Here $\alpha$ reaches its upper bound 1, giving $\hat\Sigma = \mu I$ ($\mu = 0.974$) and reducing error from 2.90 for $S$ to 0.118.
At $n = 200$ (`large-sample`), intensity falls to $\alpha = 0.147$; compared with $S$ condition number 12.9, $\hat\Sigma$ has 7.96, closer to the true 8.6.
At $p = 40$ and $n = 20$ (`wide`), $S$ has rank at most 19 and a minimum eigenvalue of order $-10^{-15}$, leaving its condition number undefined.
Shrinkage with $\alpha = 0.755$ makes it positive definite, with condition number 3.1.

## Principal component analysis

### Directions maximizing variance

Principal component analysis (PCA) projects centered data $X_c = X - \mathbf 1 \bar x^{\mathsf T}$ onto orthogonal directions ordered by decreasing variance.
The first principal direction $v_1$ maximizes sample variance $v^{\mathsf T} S v$ of projection $X_c v$ over unit vectors and is an eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue of $S$.
Principal component $k$ maximizes variance subject to orthogonality to preceding directions; eigenvectors of $S = V \Lambda V^{\mathsf T}$ ordered by descending eigenvalues give principal directions (loadings).
Component $k$ has variance $\lambda_k$, with $\sum_k \lambda_k = \mathrm{tr}\,S$ (the sum of individual-variable variances).
$\lambda_k/\mathrm{tr}\,S$ is the explained variance ratio.

Reconstruction $\hat X_k = X_c V_k V_k^{\mathsf T}$ from the leading $k$ components of $X_c$ minimizes Frobenius error among rank-$k$ matrices (Eckart–Young theorem), with error

$$
\|X_c - \hat X_k\|_F^2 = (n - 1) \sum_{j > k} \lambda_j
$$


Principal directions have arbitrary signs: both $v_k$ and $-v_k$ are solutions.
PCA depends on variable units; high-variance variables dominate leading components.
To weight variables with different units equally, standardize columns first (PCA of the correlation matrix).

### Computation by singular value decomposition

[`PreparedPca`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.PreparedPca.html) in `scioxide-stats` computes a thin singular value decomposition $X_c = U D V^{\mathsf T}$ of $X_c$ ($D = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots)$, $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$), rather than forming and eigendecomposing $S$.
Principal directions are columns of $V$, and variances are $\lambda_k = \sigma_k^2/(n - 1)$.
Forming $S \propto X_c^{\mathsf T} X_c$ squares the condition number and loses relative accuracy in low-variance components compared with $\lambda_1$.
SVD acts directly on $X_c$ and avoids that loss.
The cost is $O(np\min(n, p))$.

The numerical-rank policy in `MultivariateOptions` (default `RankPolicy::DimensionScaledEpsilon`) determines retained component count $r$ from singular values.
Centered matrices have rank at most $\min(n - 1, p)$, so $p \ge n$ retains at most $n - 1$ components.
A retained component with zero variance returns `NumericalInstability`.
No component-count selection by explained variance is provided; zero unused component scores for reconstruction.

Whitening (`PcaScaling::Whitened`) divides each score by component standard deviation $\sigma_k/\sqrt{n - 1}$, making fitted-data score sample covariance the identity within rounding error.
Whitened values turn Mahalanobis distance $\sqrt{(x - \bar x)^{\mathsf T} S^{+} (x - \bar x)}$ into ordinary Euclidean distance.
This amplifies low-variance components, so discard noisy lower components first.

### PCA API

`PreparedPca::new(data)` and `PreparedPca::with_options(data, options)` fit the model; `PreparedPca::build(data, options, context)` accepts an execution context.
Read fitted values through column means `mean()`, the $p \times r$ direction matrix `components()`, variances `variances()`, and numerical-rank report `rank()`.
`transform(data, scaling)` returns scores $(Z - \mathbf 1 \bar x^{\mathsf T}) V$ for new data; `reconstruct(scores, scaling)` returns original variables $\mathbf 1 \bar x^{\mathsf T} + T V^{\mathsf T}$ from score matrix $T$.
Transformation subtracts compensated means rather than rounded `mean()`.
With every component retained, `reconstruct` is the inverse of `transform` within rounding error.

For repeated transformations of many rows, use workspace-accepting `transform_into(data, scaling, &mut output, &mut ws, context)` and `reconstruct_into`.
`pca.workspace_requirements(rows, columns)` returns [`MultivariateRequirements`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.MultivariateRequirements.html) (`required_bytes()` is scratch bytes excluding output), and `pca.workspace(rows, columns)` constructs [`PcaWorkspace`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.PcaWorkspace.html).
`columns` is the retained component count for `transform_into` and variable count for `reconstruct_into`.
Insufficient workspace returns `ResourceLimit` without allocating.
Passing CCA requirements to `PcaWorkspace::try_reserve` returns `InvalidInput`.

### PCA experiment

## Canonical correlation analysis

### Correlation between two sets of variables

Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) describes the relationship between variable sets $X$ ($p$ columns) and $Y$ ($q$ columns) measured on the same $n$ individuals through correlations of their linear combinations $u = Xa$ and $v = Yb$.
The first canonical correlation $\rho_1$ maximizes $\mathrm{Corr}(Xa, Yb)$; canonical correlation $k$ maximizes it among combinations uncorrelated with preceding canonical variates.
Canonical correlations satisfy $\rho_1 \ge \rho_2 \ge \dots \ge \rho_r \ge 0$ ($r \le \min(p, q)$) and are invariant under nonsingular linear transformations within each variable set.
For $p = q = 1$, $\rho_1$ equals the absolute correlation of two variables; for $q = 1$, it equals the square root of the multiple-regression coefficient of determination.

In covariance notation, $\rho_k^2$ are eigenvalues of $S_{xx}^{-1} S_{xy} S_{yy}^{-1} S_{yx}$.
Forming explicit inverses this way makes squared condition numbers of $S_{xx}$ or $S_{yy}$ affect errors.

### Björck–Golub method

[`PreparedCca`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.PreparedCca.html) obtains canonical correlations (Björck–Golub) from thin singular value decompositions of centered $X$ and $Y$:

$$
X = U_x D_x V_x^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
Y = U_y D_y V_y^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
U_x^{\mathsf T} U_y = P\, C\, Q^{\mathsf T}
$$


$U_x$ and $U_y$ are orthonormal bases of the column spaces; the diagonal of $C$ (cosines of principal angles between the bases) gives canonical correlations.
Weights are $A = \sqrt{n - 1}\, V_x D_x^{-1} P$ and $B = \sqrt{n - 1}\, V_y D_y^{-1} Q$; canonical variates $XA$ and $YB$ have fitted-data sample variances 1 and cross-covariance $\mathrm{diag}(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_r)$.
The SVDs of $X$ and $Y$ are truncated at numerical ranks $r_x$ and $r_y$, retaining $r = \min(r_x, r_y)$ pairs.
Linearly dependent columns are therefore allowed.
The cost is three SVDs (of $X$, $Y$, and an $r_x \times r_y$ product).

Sample canonical correlations have upward bias.
Since $\rho_1$ maximizes over many directions, even zero true correlation gives large values when $n$ is small relative to $p + q$.
Population-zero canonical correlations become positive in samples.
Canonical-correlation significance tests (Bartlett approximation or Wilks' Λ) and regularized CCA are not provided.

### CCA API

`PreparedCca::new(x, y)`, `with_options(x, y, options)`, and `build(x, y, options, context)` fit the model.
Mismatched row counts, fewer than two rows, no columns, or either numerical rank being 0 (such as all-constant columns) returns `InvalidInput`.
`correlations()` returns canonical correlations, `x_weights()` and `y_weights()` return weight matrices of size $p \times r$ and $q \times r$, `ranks()` returns both numerical-rank reports, and `means()` returns both sets of column means.
`scores(x, y)` returns canonical variates $(XA, YB)$ for new data.
Workspace-accepting `scores_into(x, y, &mut x_out, &mut y_out, &mut ws, context)` uses [`CcaWorkspace`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.CcaWorkspace.html) constructed with `cca.workspace_requirements(rows)` and `cca.workspace(rows)`.
Workspace holds two score-shaped matrices; insufficient space returns `ResourceLimit` without allocation.

### CCA experiment

The next cell constructs two variable sets sharing latent variable $z \sim \mathrm{N}(0, 1)$.
Among the three columns of $X$, $x_1 = a z + e_1$; among the two columns of $Y$, $y_1 = a z + f_1$; remaining columns and $e_1$ and $f_1$ are independent standard-normal noise.
Population canonical correlations are $\rho_1 = a^2/(a^2 + 1)$ and $\rho_2 = 0$.

## Summary

`sample_covariance` centers by subtracting compensated means, preserving deviation digits for columns with large common offsets.
When $p/n$ is not small, sample-covariance eigenvalues spread; shrink with `ledoit_wolf_covariance` before using inverses or condition numbers.
An intensity $\alpha$ near 1 indicates that differences between $S$ and target $\mu I$ are hidden by estimation error in $S$.
`PreparedPca` computes principal components by SVD of centered data; check total variance against $\mathrm{tr}\,S$ and leading-$k$ reconstruction error against the discarded-variance sum $(n - 1)\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j$.
Canonical correlations from `PreparedCca` have upward bias; when $n$ is small relative to $p + q$, even population-zero correlations can appear large.
