When planning an experiment, estimate in advance the probability that a test will detect an effect of the assumed size if it actually exists.
This probability is called statistical power.
With too few observations, the test may fail to reject despite an effect, leaving the experiment inconclusive.
Too many observations consume more time and money than needed.
This page derives power for tests of normal population means, computes it with `power` from `scioxide-stats`, and obtains required sample sizes with `sample_size`.
Finally, `simulated_power` estimates power by simulation to check the analytical values and extends the same approach to tests without a formula.

## Power and the two types of error

A test that decides whether to reject a null hypothesis $H_0$ can make two types of error.
Rejecting a true $H_0$ is a type I error; failing to reject a false $H_0$ is a type II error.
The significance level $\alpha$ bounds the type I error probability; write the type II error probability as $\beta$.
Power is the rejection probability under the alternative hypothesis:

$$
\pi = 1-\beta = P_{H_1}(\text{reject})
$$


Viewed as a function of the parameter $\theta$, $\pi(\theta)=P_\theta(\text{reject})$ is the power function, with $\pi(\theta)\le\alpha$ under $H_0$.

Power depends on effect size, observational variability, sample size $n$, significance level $\alpha$, and the direction of the alternative (one-sided or two-sided).
The mean difference $\Delta$ divided by the standard deviation $\sigma$ gives

$$
d = \frac{\Delta}{\sigma}
$$

called the standardized effect size (Cohen's $d$).
Cohen (1988) suggested $d=0.2,\ 0.5,\ 0.8$ as guidelines for small, medium, and large effects.
The effect size to detect must, however, be determined from subject knowledge; these guidelines cannot replace that judgment.

The design values $\Delta$ and $\sigma$ are assumptions based on previous studies or pilot experiments.
Substituting estimates from the collected data to compute post hoc power adds no information: it is merely a monotone function of the p-value (Hoenig and Heisey 2001).
Interpret a nonsignificant result through the effect's confidence interval rather than post hoc power.

## Power for tests of means

Assume independent observations following a normal distribution $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$.
The hypothesis is $H_0:\mu-\mu_0=0$; $\mu_0$ is a known reference for one sample or the control-group mean for two samples.
Let each group have sample size $n$ and write the standard error of the mean (or mean difference) as $\sigma\sqrt{c/n}$.
For one sample, $c=1$; for two independent groups of $n$ observations each, $c=2$.
Treat paired samples (such as before-and-after measurements on the same individuals) as one sample of differences $D_i=X_i-Y_i$, using their standard deviation for $\sigma$.
The mean shift after dividing the test statistic by its standard error is

$$
\delta = \frac{\Delta}{\sigma\sqrt{c/n}} = d\sqrt{\frac{n}{c}}
$$

called the noncentrality parameter.
Multiplying $n$ by 4 doubles $\delta$.

### z test with known variance

When $\sigma$ is known, the test statistic $Z=(\bar X-\mu_0)/(\sigma\sqrt{c/n})$ follows $N(\delta,1)$ under the alternative.
Write the standard normal distribution function as $\Phi$ and its upper-tail $p$ quantile as $z_p=\Phi^{-1}(1-p)$; an upper one-sided test rejects for $Z>z_\alpha$ and has power

$$
\pi_{\text{upper}} = 1-\Phi(z_\alpha-\delta) = \Phi(\delta-z_\alpha)
$$


A two-sided test rejects for $|Z|>z_{\alpha/2}$, so including the opposite tail gives

$$
\pi_{\text{two-sided}} = \Phi(-z_{\alpha/2}-\delta) + 1-\Phi(z_{\alpha/2}-\delta)
$$


For $\delta>0$, the first term quickly becomes negligible, so the sample size $n$ satisfying $\pi=1-\beta$ is approximately

$$
n \approx \frac{c\,(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2}{d^2}
$$

(in a one-sided test, replace $z_{\alpha/2}$ with $z_\alpha$).
For two groups with $d=0.5$, $\alpha=0.05$ (two-sided), and $\pi=0.8$, $2(1.960+0.842)^2/0.25=62.8$ gives 63 observations per group.

### t test with estimated variance

Replacing $\sigma$ with the unbiased sample standard deviation $S$ gives $T=(\bar X-\mu_0)/(S\sqrt{c/n})$, which follows a noncentral t distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom.
The noncentral t distribution is defined from independent $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and a chi-squared variable $W$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom by

$$
T = \frac{Z+\delta}{\sqrt{W/\nu}}
$$


The degrees of freedom are $\nu=n-1$ for one sample and $\nu=2(n-1)$ for the two-group Student t test using pooled variance.
Under $H_0$, $\delta=0$ gives a central t distribution, so the rejection boundary is the upper-tail $\alpha$ quantile $t_{\alpha,\nu}$ of the t distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom.
The power of an upper one-sided test is the noncentral t upper-tail probability

$$
\pi_{\text{upper}} = P(T_{\nu,\delta} > t_{\alpha,\nu})
$$

and for a two-sided test it is $P(T_{\nu,\delta}<-t_{\alpha/2,\nu})+P(T_{\nu,\delta}>t_{\alpha/2,\nu})$.
At the same $n$, variability in the denominator $S$ makes t-test power lower than z-test power; the difference shrinks as $\nu$ grows.
The required t-test sample size is therefore at least the z-test size, and $n$ from the approximate z-test formula provides a starting point for the t-test search.
Exact values agree with the tables in Lehmann and Romano (2005) and Cohen (1988, Chapter 2).
For example, a two-group t test with $d=0.5$, $\alpha=0.05$ (two-sided), and $\pi=0.8$ needs 64 observations per group, one more than the z-test approximation.

These formulas assume normality and independence.
Heavy tails or outliers can make actual t-test rejection rates and power differ from the formulas.
With unequal group variances, the pooled-variance t test's type I error stays near the nominal level for equal sample sizes but can depart substantially for unequal sample sizes.
Estimate power for such designs with the simulation below.

## APIs for power and sample-size searches

`MeanPowerModel` collects a test design in a structure with the following fields.

- `effect` is $\Delta=\mu-\mu_0$, and `standard_deviation` is $\sigma>0$.
  To specify $d$ directly, set $\sigma=1$.
- `design` is `MeanPowerDesign::OneSample` (one sample or paired differences, $c=1$) or `MeanPowerDesign::EqualGroups` (two independent groups of $n$ each, $c=2$).
- `reference` is `MeanPowerReference::KnownVariance` (z test) or `MeanPowerReference::EstimatedVariance` (pooled-variance t test).
- `alternative` specifies the rejection direction: `TestAlternative::Greater`, `Less`, or `TwoSided`.
  A two-sided test allocates half of $\alpha$ to each side and adds both tail probabilities.
- `significance` is a `ConfidenceProbability` containing the significance level $\alpha$ itself.
  Although the type shares its name with the confidence coefficient, pass $\alpha$ here rather than $1-\alpha$.

`power(model, n)` computes power using the formulas above and returns `PowerEstimate`.
The z test uses the standard normal quantile and distribution function; the t test uses the Student t quantile and the upper tail of the noncentral t distribution `NoncentralStudentT`.
Noncentral t tails use the series of Benton and Krishnamoorthy (2003), switching to quadrature when cancellation occurs ([Probability laws of test statistics](../../../scioxide-random/algorithms/test-statistic-laws/)).
Each call evaluates one quantile and one or two tail probabilities, without allocating workspace.
For normal data, the value is exact and has no interval.
The following cases return errors.

- $n<2$, $n>2^{53}$, a nonfinite `effect` or `standard_deviation`, or $\sigma\le0$ gives `InvalidInput`.
- An unrepresentable (overflowing) $\delta$ gives `NumericalInstability` (`NumericalIssue::Breakdown`).
- Distribution-evaluation errors propagate unchanged.

`PowerEstimate` represents both analytical values and simulation estimates.
`probability()` returns power, `interval()` the simulation's Wilson 95% interval, and `replicates()` and `rejections()` the replicate and rejection counts.
For analytical values, `interval()` is `None` and both counts are 0.
Wrap a power value computed yourself with `PowerEstimate::analytic(p)` (nonfinite $p$ gives `NonFinite`, and a value outside $[0,1]$ gives `InvalidInput`).

`sample_size(minimum, maximum, target, evaluate)` increments $n$ by 1 from `minimum`, calls `evaluate(n)`, and returns the first $n$ whose power reaches `target`.
An analytical value passes when $\pi\ge$ `target`; a simulation estimate passes when its lower Wilson bound is at least `target`.
The return type is `Option<SampleSizeResult>`; if no $n$ in the range passes, the result is `None`.
A larger $n$ outside the range may pass, so read `None` as “expand the range.”
`SampleSizeResult` contains the passing `size`, the `estimate` at that $n$, and the number `evaluated` of evaluated $n$ values (including `size`).
The linear search makes at most `maximum − minimum + 1` calls.
Choosing `minimum` from the approximate z-test formula above reduces the t-test search to a few calls.
A `target` outside $(0,1)$ gives `InvalidInput` (`NonFinite` if nonfinite); `minimum < 2` or `maximum < minimum` gives `InvalidInput`; the first error from `evaluate` propagates unchanged.

`scioxide-stats` provides power formulas only for z and pooled-variance t tests of normal population means.
It does not provide power formulas for proportion tests, ANOVA F tests, rank tests, Welch t tests, or two groups with unequal sizes.
When a formula is known, wrap its value with `PowerEstimate::analytic` and pass it to `sample_size`; otherwise use `simulated_power` from the next section.

## Power curves and required sample sizes per group

The next cell computes t-test and z-test power from $n=2$ to 2000 for a design testing the mean difference between two groups of $n$ observations each.
The inputs are standardized effect size $d$, significance level $\alpha$, and the alternative direction; fixing $\sigma=1$ makes `effect` equal to $d$.
The table reports `sample_size` searches for the smallest per-group sample sizes achieving target power 0.8.
The third row checks the result by substituting the normal quantile `Normal::quantile` into the approximate z-test formula and computing it with f64 arithmetic.
The plot shows power curves with a logarithmic horizontal axis; the horizontal line marks the target 0.8, and the vertical line marks the required t-test size.
The dashed z-test curve is an idealized known-variance reference; the t-test curve always lies below it.
This cell passes $d$, $\alpha$, direction, and the required t-test size and power to the simulation cell in the next section.

    "greater" => (TestAlternative::Greater, 1.0),
    _ => (TestAlternative::TwoSided, 2.0),

For the defaults $d=0.5$ and $\alpha=0.05$ (two-sided), the required t-test size is 64 per group (power 0.8015), the z-test size is 63 (0.8013), and the approximation also gives 63.
The value of 64 per group agrees with Cohen's (1988) table.
The t-test search evaluates 63 values of $n$ starting at $n=2$; setting `minimum` to the approximation's 63 completes it in two evaluations.
For $d=0.2$, the t test needs 394 and the z test 393; multiplying the effect size by $1/2.5$ multiplies the required size by approximately $2.5^2=6.25$.
For $d=1.2$, the t test needs 12 and the z test 11.
Smaller $n$ gives heavier tails in the t distribution with $2(n-1)$ degrees of freedom, so the ratio of required sizes increases.
For $\alpha=0.01$, the t-test size rises to 96 and the z-test size to 94; for the upper one-sided test they fall to 51 and 50.
For the one-sided z test, $n=50$ gives $\delta=0.5\sqrt{50/2}=2.5$, giving power $\Phi(2.5-1.6449)=0.80377$ in agreement with the table.
At the plot's left endpoint $n=2$, t-test power is 0.0615: the effect is barely visible, and the rejection probability is close to $\alpha$.

## Power by simulation

For a test without a formula, repeatedly generate data and apply the test, estimating power from the fraction of rejections.
If $r$ of $m$ replicates reject, $r$ follows the binomial distribution $\mathrm{Bin}(m,\pi)$, giving estimate $\hat\pi=r/m$ and standard error $\sqrt{\pi(1-\pi)/m}$.
For $\pi=0.8$ and $m=4000$, the standard error is 0.0063; reading two decimal places therefore requires thousands of replicates.

Use the Wilson score interval:

$$
\frac{\hat\pi+\dfrac{z^2}{2m}\pm z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)}{m}+\dfrac{z^2}{4m^2}}}{1+\dfrac{z^2}{m}},\qquad z=z_{0.025}=1.959964
$$


The Wald interval $\hat\pi\pm z\sqrt{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)/m}$ has coverage far below nominal when $\hat\pi$ is near 0 or 1, and zero width at $r=0$ (Brown, Cai, and DasGupta 2001).
The Wilson interval inverts a score test and retains coverage near nominal even near the boundaries.
Set the lower limit to 0 when $r=0$ and the upper limit to 1 when $r=m$.
This interval describes simulation sampling error, not a rounding-error bound.

`simulated_power(sampler, test, first_size, second_size, significance, replicates, root)` performs this simulation in a single call.

- `sampler(rng, sample)` writes one sample into a `sample` of length `first_size + second_size`.
  `test(x, y)` receives its first portion `x` and second portion `y`, returning a p-value; `p < α` counts as rejection.
- Replicate $k$ draws from a child stream of `root.split(k)`, so results do not depend on execution partitioning or worker count, and `root` itself does not advance.
  Changing only $n$ with the same `root` starts each replicate from the same random stream (common random numbers), reducing variability in estimated differences between $n$ values.
- Zero `first_size` or `replicates < 2` gives `InvalidInput`; a nonfinite sample value gives `NonFinite`; a p-value outside $[0,1]$ gives `InvalidInput`.
  Errors from `sampler` and `test` propagate unchanged.
- The result is a `PowerEstimate` with a Wilson interval.
  Known rejection and replicate counts can produce the same estimate with `PowerEstimate::simulated(rejections, replicates)` (`replicates < 2` or `rejections > replicates` gives `InvalidInput`).

Use the borrowed form `simulated_power_into(sampler, test, first_size, second_size, significance, root, options, &mut output, context)` to retain replicate p-values or set worker and memory limits with `StatisticsOptions`.
The length of `output` determines the replicate count; p-values are written to `output` only after every replicate succeeds.
Workspace contains one sample per worker and the p-value array; exceeding `max_bytes` in total gives `ResourceLimit`.
The computational cost is that of calling `sampler` and `test` once per replicate.

Passing simulation estimates to `sample_size` selects the first $n$ whose Wilson lower limit exceeds the target.
A point-estimate criterion can select too small an $n$ when sampling error happens to push power above the target; the lower-limit criterion avoids this.
In exchange, the selected $n$ tends to exceed the true requirement and approaches it as the replicate count grows.
Because simulation is repeated for every candidate $n$, use a formula to estimate a suitable search range first.

## Checking analytical values by simulation

The next cell receives $d$, $\alpha$, direction, and required t-test size $n$ and its power from the preceding cell, then uses `simulated_power` to generate normal samples for the same design.
The treatment group follows $N(d,1)$ and the control group $N(0,1)$; both pooled-variance and Welch t tests determine rejection (`TTestVariance::Pooled` and `Welch` from `t_test_independent_with_options`).
Both tests use the same `root`, comparing decisions on the same samples.
The table's final column divides the simulation estimate's difference from the analytical value by the analytical standard error $\sqrt{\pi(1-\pi)/m}$; an absolute value roughly at most 2 is consistent with the analytical value.

```rust
//| id: stats-power-simulation
//| ref: stats-power-simulation
//| title: "Check analytical power with simulated t tests and Wilson intervals"
//| run: button
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-random, scioxide-stats]
//| dependsOn:
//|   design: stats-power-sample-size
//| inputs:
//|   replicates: {"type":"integer","label":"Replicates m","min":200,"max":10000,"value":4000}
//|   seed: {"type":"integer","label":"Random seed","min":0,"max":100000,"value":2024}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"few-replicates","label":"m = 200","description":"Fewer replicates widen the Wilson interval.","inputs":{"replicates":200}}
//|   - {"id":"other-seed","label":"Seed = 7","description":"The analytical value remains inside the interval with another random stream.","inputs":{"seed":7}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/0/2","approx":{"value":0.80146,"atol":1e-4}}
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/1/1","equals":3197}
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/1/5","range":{"min":-2.5,"max":2.5}}
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/2/1","equals":3197}
//|   - case: few-replicates
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/1/1","equals":160}
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/1/3","approx":{"value":0.73914,"atol":1e-4}}
//|   - case: other-seed
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/1/1","equals":3213}
//|       - {"artifact":"simulation","path":"/rows/2/1","equals":3212}
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, RealScalar};
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Normal, Pcg64, SeedableRng};
use scioxide_stats::{
    ClassicalOptions, ConfidenceProbability, TTestVariance, TestAlternative, simulated_power,
    t_test_independent_with_options,
};

let effect = design["effect"].as_f64().ok_or("Effect size is absent")?;
let alpha = design["alpha"].as_f64().ok_or("Missing significance level")?;
let size = design["size"].as_u64().ok_or("No required size in the search range")? as usize;
let analytic = design["power"].as_f64().ok_or("Missing analytical power")?;
let tail = match design["alternative"].as_str() {
    Some("greater") => TestAlternative::Greater,
    _ => TestAlternative::TwoSided,
};
let significance = ConfidenceProbability::new(alpha).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let treated = Normal::<f64>::new(effect, 1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let control = Normal::<f64>::new(0.0, 1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let root = Pcg64::from_seed(seed as u64);
// One sample: first n observations in the treatment group, last n in the control group
let sampler = |rng: &mut Pcg64, sample: &mut [f64]| {
    let (x, y) = sample.split_at_mut(size);
    for value in x {
        *value = treated.sample(rng)?;
    }
    for value in y {
        *value = control.sample(rng)?;
    }
    Ok::<(), SciOxideError>(())
};
let m = replicates as usize;
let standard_error = RealScalar::sqrt(analytic * (1.0 - analytic) / m as f64);
let mut rows = vec![("Analytical (noncentral t)", None, analytic, None, None, None)];
for (name, variance) in [
    ("Simulation: pooled-variance t test", TTestVariance::Pooled),
    ("Simulation: Welch t test", TTestVariance::Welch),
] {
    let options = ClassicalOptions::default().with_alternative(tail);
    let test = |x: &[f64], y: &[f64]| {
        Ok::<f64, SciOxideError>(
            t_test_independent_with_options(x, y, variance, options)?.p_value(),
        )
    };
    let estimate = simulated_power(&sampler, test, size, size, significance, m, &root)
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
    let interval = estimate.interval().ok_or("Simulation estimate has no interval")?;
    let p = estimate.probability();
    rows.push((
        name,
        Some(estimate.rejections()),
        p,
        Some(interval.lower()),
        Some(interval.upper()),
        Some((p - analytic) / standard_error),
    ));
}
let fixed4 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 4 });
let fixed2 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Computation" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Rejections r", "type": "integer", "nullText": "-" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Power [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed4 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Wilson lower bound [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed4, "nullText": "-" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Wilson upper bound [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed4, "nullText": "-" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "(π̂ − π)/SE [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2, "nullText": "-" }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "simulation" })
);
```

With defaults $n=64$ and $m=4000$, the pooled-variance t test rejects 3197 times; the Wilson interval $[0.7866, 0.8114]$ for estimate 0.7993 contains the analytical value 0.8015.
The difference from the analytical value is $-0.35$ standard errors, consistent with the noncentral t calculation.
Seed 7 gives 3213 rejections (0.8033, a difference of 0.28 standard errors).
For $m=200$, there are 160 rejections and the interval widens to $[0.7391, 0.8495]$; the simulation supports only one decimal place.

With equal group sizes, Welch and pooled-variance t statistics are identical; only their degrees of freedom differ.
Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom are at most $2(n-1)$, so p-values are slightly larger and there are no more rejections.
At the default seed both rejection counts are 3197; at seed 7 Welch has one fewer.
For unequal group sizes or variances, the tests' powers diverge; change `sampler` to check with the same approach.

## Summary

Power is the probability of rejection when the assumed effect exists, determined by effect size $d$, sample size $n$, significance level $\alpha$, and alternative direction.
For normal-population mean tests, specify the design in `MeanPowerModel` and call `power` to obtain exact power through the normal distribution for z tests or the noncentral t distribution for t tests.
Search sample sizes linearly with `sample_size`, choosing the starting point from the approximate z-test formula $n\approx c(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2/d^2$.
For tests without formulas or designs without normality or equal variances, estimate rejection rates with `simulated_power` and show simulation error with Wilson intervals.
`sample_size` judges simulation estimates by their lower interval limits, avoiding the selection of too small an $n$ due to sampling error.
