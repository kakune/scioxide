A time series is a sequence observed at equally spaced times, such as hourly room temperature, daily sales, or sensor output.
Adjacent time-series values are dependent; the current value often resembles the preceding value.
Directly applying standard errors or tests that assume independent samples ignores this dependence and underestimates uncertainty.
Time-series analysis first measures dependence through autocorrelation, then fits a model of that structure (an ARIMA model) to obtain forecasts and their uncertainty.

This page describes the following three features of `scioxide-stats` through definitions, estimation, APIs, and experiments.

- Sample autocovariance, autocorrelation (ACF), and partial autocorrelation (PACF)
- Spectral description with periodograms
- Exact Gaussian likelihood for ARIMA models, maximum likelihood estimation, forecasts, and prediction intervals

## Stationarity and autocorrelation

### Weakly stationary processes

A time series $\{x_t\}$ is weakly stationary (second-order stationary) if its mean $E[x_t] = \mu$ is independent of $t$ and covariance $\mathrm{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k})$ depends only on the time difference $k$.
Then $\gamma(k) = \mathrm{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k})$ is the autocovariance function and $\rho(k) = \gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$ the autocorrelation function (ACF).
The autocovariance function is positive semidefinite.
That is, the Toeplitz matrix $\Gamma_m = [\gamma(i - j)]_{i,j=1}^{m}$ is positive semidefinite for every $m$.
Series with trends or seasonality are nonstationary; remove these by differencing or regression before measuring autocorrelation.

### Sample autocovariance and the choice of divisor

For observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and center $\bar x$, compute sample autocovariance as

$$
\hat\gamma(k) = \frac{1}{D_k} \sum_{t=k+1}^{n} (x_t - \bar x)(x_{t-k} - \bar x)
$$


There are two choices for divisor $D_k$, selected by [`LagNormalization`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/enum.LagNormalization.html).

- `ObservationCount` (default): use $D_k = n$ at every lag.
  $\hat\gamma(k)$ is biased toward zero, but the sequence $\hat\gamma(0), \hat\gamma(1), \dots$ is positive semidefinite.
  This ensures positive-definite coefficient matrices for Yule–Walker equations and Levinson–Durbin recursion, so use it for model identification (Brockwell–Davis §7.2).
- `PairCount`: divide by the number of pairs $D_k = n - k$ at lag $k$.
  Bias is smaller at large lags, but the sequence need not be positive semidefinite and large-lag estimates have high variance.

Select the center with [`TimeSeriesCentering`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/enum.TimeSeriesCentering.html).
The default `SampleMean` subtracts the sample mean; `KnownZero` subtracts nothing for series with known zero mean (such as differenced series or a model's one-step forecast errors).
Applying `KnownZero` to a nonzero-mean series adds $\mu^2$ to $\hat\gamma(k)$, making autocorrelations near 1 at every lag.

Bartlett's formula approximates the uncertainty of sample autocorrelation $\hat\rho(k) = \hat\gamma(k)/\hat\gamma(0)$.
For white noise ($\rho(k) = 0$ for all $k \ge 1$), $\hat\rho(k)$ is approximately $\mathrm{N}(0, 1/n)$.
This motivates lines at $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{n}$ on ACF plots; these are pointwise 95% limits at each lag, so one of 20 lags is ordinarily expected to exceed them.

### Partial autocorrelation and Levinson–Durbin recursion

$\rho(k)$ includes correlation mediated by the intervening values $x_{t-1}, \dots, x_{t-k+1}$.
Partial autocorrelation $\phi_{kk}$ is the correlation between residuals after linear prediction from intervening values; it equals the final coefficient of the order-$k$ autoregression $x_t = \phi_{k1}x_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_{kk}x_{t-k} + e_t$ fitted using the Yule–Walker equations

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \rho(0) & \cdots & \rho(k-1) \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \rho(k-1) & \cdots & \rho(0) \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \phi_{k1} \\ \vdots \\ \phi_{kk} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \rho(1) \\ \vdots \\ \rho(k) \end{bmatrix}
$$


The coefficient matrix is Toeplitz, so Levinson–Durbin recursion solves successively from order 1.
Construct the order-$k$ solution from the order-$k - 1$ solution using

$$
\phi_{kk} = \frac{\rho(k) - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \phi_{k-1,j}\,\rho(k-j)}{v_{k-1}},
\qquad
\phi_{kj} = \phi_{k-1,j} - \phi_{kk}\,\phi_{k-1,k-j},
\qquad
v_k = v_{k-1}(1 - \phi_{kk}^2)
$$

with $v_0 = 1$; computation through $L$ lags costs $O(L^2)$.
$v_k$ is the standardized prediction-error variance; it decreases while remaining positive as long as $|\phi_{kk}| < 1$.
`scioxide-stats` returns `InvalidInput` when $|\phi_{kk}| \ge 1$ or $v_k$ falls below machine epsilon.
This should not occur with divisor `ObservationCount`; it occurs when `PairCount` produces a sequence that is not positive semidefinite or when the series is nearly deterministic.

### Autocorrelation of autoregressive processes

The first-order autoregressive process AR(1),

$$
x_t - \mu = \phi\,(x_{t-1} - \mu) + \varepsilon_t,
\qquad
\varepsilon_t \sim \mathrm{N}(0, \sigma^2)\ \text{independent},
\qquad
|\phi| < 1
$$

is stationary, with $\gamma(0) = \sigma^2/(1 - \phi^2)$ and $\rho(k) = \phi^k$.
Its partial autocorrelations are $\phi_{11} = \phi$ and $\phi_{kk} = 0$ for $k \ge 2$.
In general, an AR($p$) PACF vanishes beyond order $p$, and an MA($q$) ACF vanishes beyond $q$.
These cutoffs guide model-order selection in the Box–Jenkins method.
For $k > p$, $\hat\phi_{kk}$ is approximately $\mathrm{N}(0, 1/n)$ (Quenouille).

The AR(1) Yule–Walker estimates are $\hat\phi = \hat\rho(1)$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = \hat\gamma(0)(1 - \hat\phi^2)$, with asymptotic variance $\mathrm{Var}\,\hat\phi \approx (1 - \phi^2)/n$.
`scioxide-stats` has no Yule–Walker estimation function; construct estimates from ACF and PACF using the formulas above.

## Computing autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation

[`time_series_summary`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/fn.time_series_summary.html)`(&x, statistic, max_lag)` computes statistic [`TimeSeriesStatistic`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/enum.TimeSeriesStatistic.html) (`Covariance`, `Acf`, or `Pacf`) at lags $0, \dots, \mathrm{max\_lag}$ and returns `max_lag + 1` [`TimeSeriesLag`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.TimeSeriesLag.html) values.
Each element contains the value `value` and the number of pairs `pairs` with both observations present at that lag.
ACF and PACF at lag 0 are 1.
The one-shot form centers by the sample mean and divides by `ObservationCount`.

The detailed form `time_series_summary_with_options(values, validity, statistic, max_lag, summary, options)` accepts missingness indicators `validity`, [`TimeSeriesOptions`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.TimeSeriesOptions.html) for divisor and centering, and `StatisticsOptions` for missing/nonfinite policies and memory limits.
Skipping missing values with `MissingPolicy::Skip` leaves their positions empty on the time grid without shifting later observations.
A missing observation excludes every pair containing it at each lag, reducing `pairs`.
The default `MissingPolicy::Reject` returns `InvalidInput` for missing observations.

In the workspace form [`time_series_summary_into`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/fn.time_series_summary_into.html)`(values, validity, statistic, max_lag, summary, options, &mut output, &mut ws, context)`, the caller owns the output and [`TimeSeriesWorkspace`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.TimeSeriesWorkspace.html).
`TimeSeriesWorkspace::scalar_requirements(samples, max_lag, options)` returns [`TimeSeriesRequirements`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.TimeSeriesRequirements.html), and `workspace(&req)` creates the workspace.
For real series, required bytes `required_bytes()` depend on lag count rather than series length.
Reuse the same workspace for repeated computations with different statistics, such as ACF and PACF.
Use `time_series_summaries_into` to process multiple series (such as multichannel sensors) as records of a `BatchView`; records run in parallel when execution policy permits.
Results are independent of worker count.

Computation costs $O(nL + L^2)$ for $n$ observations and $L = \mathrm{max\_lag} + 1$ lags.
Real series use compensated summation, and lag sums with severe cancellation are recomputed using error-free products.
Empty series, `max_lag` at least as large as the length, any lag with no pairs, or ACF/PACF requests with $\hat\gamma(0) = 0$ produce `InvalidInput`.

### AR(1) autocorrelation experiment

The next block defines the model shared by this page's three cells.
`simulate_ar1` generates $n$ AR(1) points with mean $\mu$, coefficient $\phi$, and innovation (the new error $\varepsilon_t$ at each time) standard deviation $\sigma$.
It draws the first value from the stationary distribution $\mathrm{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/(1 - \phi^2))$, making the series stationary from the start.
The example uses hourly room temperature fluctuating around 22 °C with innovations of $\sigma = 0.5$ °C.

```rust
//| id: stats-time-series-ar1
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "AR(1) model of room temperature"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-random]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, RealScalar};
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Normal, SeedableRng, Xoshiro256StarStar};

/// Mean room temperature μ [°C].
pub(crate) const MEAN: f64 = 22.0;
/// Innovation standard deviation σ [°C].
pub(crate) const SIGMA: f64 = 0.5;

/// n points of x_t − μ = φ (x_{t−1} − μ) + ε_t, ε_t ~ N(0, σ²); draw x_1 from the stationary distribution.
pub(crate) fn simulate_ar1(seed: u64, n: usize, phi: f64) -> Result<Vec<f64>, SciOxideError> {
    let mut rng = Xoshiro256StarStar::from_seed(seed);
    let noise = Normal::new(0.0, SIGMA)?;
    let start = Normal::new(0.0, SIGMA / RealScalar::sqrt(1.0 - phi * phi))?;
    let mut deviation = start.sample(&mut rng)?;
    let mut series = Vec::with_capacity(n);
    for _ in 0..n {
        series.push(MEAN + deviation);
        deviation = phi * deviation + noise.sample(&mut rng)?;
    }
    Ok(series)
}
```

For comparison, compute ACF with divisor `PairCount` and with center `KnownZero` using `time_series_summary_with_options`.
The table compares with theoretical values $\phi^k$; dashed plot lines show theory and the shaded region shows white-noise limits $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{n}$.
The second table shows Yule–Walker estimation, with $z$ equal to $\hat\phi - \phi$ divided by asymptotic standard error $\sqrt{(1 - \phi^2)/n}$.

## Periodograms

### Spectral density and periodograms

Stationary-process autocovariance and spectral density $f(\nu)$ form a Fourier transform pair (the Wiener–Khinchin theorem).
With unit sampling interval and frequency $\nu$ measured in cycles per sample on $[-1/2, 1/2]$, the pair is

$$
f(\nu) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \gamma(k)\, e^{-2\pi i k \nu},
\qquad
\gamma(0) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} f(\nu)\, d\nu
$$


For AR(1), $f(\nu) = \sigma^2/|1 - \phi e^{-2\pi i \nu}|^2 = \sigma^2/(1 - 2\phi\cos 2\pi\nu + \phi^2)$; variance concentrates at low frequencies if $\phi > 0$ and high frequencies if $\phi < 0$.
For a general ARMA process, $f(\nu) = \sigma^2 |\theta(e^{-2\pi i\nu})|^2/|\phi(e^{-2\pi i\nu})|^2$.

The periodogram $I(\nu_j) = |X_j|^2/n$ ($\nu_j = j/n$), constructed from the discrete Fourier transform $X_j$ of a length-$n$ series, is a basic estimator of $f$.
By Parseval's equality, integrating the demeaned series' periodogram over all frequencies gives $\frac{1}{n}\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2 = \hat\gamma(0)$.
However, $I(\nu_j)/f(\nu_j)$ does not approach 1 as $n$ increases; it approximately follows a mean-1 exponential distribution (half a chi-squared variable with two degrees of freedom).
The periodogram is nearly unbiased but inconsistent; at each frequency its standard deviation is of the same order as $f$.
Smooth spectral estimates require averaging adjacent frequencies or segment averaging with Welch's method.

### Computing periodograms

[`periodogram_diagnostics_into`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/fn.periodogram_diagnostics_into.html)`(&plan, &values, &mut bins, &mut ws, context)` computes a single-segment periodogram using the window, detrending, sampling frequency, and frequency ordering specified by `SpectralEstimatePlan` in `scioxide-signal`.
`plan` must use density scaling (`SpectralScaling::Density`, the default), and `values` must have length `plan.segment_len()`.
Default `SpectralEstimateOptions` uses an FFT of window length, constant detrending (demeaning), two-sided frequency ordering (`StftPacking::Full`), and sampling frequency 1.
Results consist of a [`PeriodogramBin`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.PeriodogramBin.html) per frequency (`frequency`, `density`, cumulative fraction `cumulative_power`) and a [`PeriodogramDiagnostics`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.PeriodogramDiagnostics.html) summary.
The summary contains integrated power over all frequencies `integrated_power` $= \sum_j I(\nu_j)\,\Delta\nu$, the frequency of maximum density `peak_frequency` (`None` if total power is zero), and frequency spacing `bin_width` $= f_s/N$.
These are descriptive values, not tests of stationarity or peak significance.

[`PeriodogramWorkspace`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.PeriodogramWorkspace.html)`::new(&plan, options)` returns `InvalidInput` unless both the missing and nonfinite policies in `options` are `Reject`.
Periodograms assume complete, equally spaced records; removing missing values shifts later samples off the time grid.
For incomplete series, fill gaps by interpolation or another method before computing.
`scioxide-signal` provides smooth spectral estimates using Welch's method (segment averaging) and DPSS-window multitapers.

### Periodogram experiment

The next cell computes the preceding cell's series periodogram with `periodogram_diagnostics_into`.
Compute theoretical spectral density with `ArimaModel::spectral_density` on the model returned by passing true parameters $[\phi, \mu, \sigma^2]$ to `PreparedArimaFit::model`.
There are two independent checks.
First, compare `integrated_power` with $\hat\gamma(0)$ from `time_series_summary`; Parseval's equality gives agreement to within rounding error.
Second, compute the largest relative discrepancy between `spectral_density` and the closed form $\sigma^2/(1 - 2\phi\cos 2\pi\nu + \phi^2)$.

## ARIMA models

### Differencing and ARMA processes

A nonseasonal ARIMA($p, d, q$) model assumes that the series made stationary by $d$ differences $(1 - B)^d$ follows an ARMA($p, q$) process.
With backward shift $B$ defined by $B x_t = x_{t-1}$ and deterministic trend $\mu_t$ removed, write the model as

$$
\phi(B)\,(1 - B)^d\,(x_t - \mu_t) = \theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t,
\qquad
\varepsilon_t \sim \mathrm{N}(0, \sigma^2)\ \text{independent}
$$

$$
\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \dots - \phi_p z^p,
\qquad
\theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1 z + \dots + \theta_q z^q
$$


The AR part must be stationary (all roots of $\phi(z)$ outside the unit circle), and the MA part invertible (all roots of $\theta(z)$ outside the unit circle).
Invertibility selects one MA model among those with identical autocorrelation.

Select trend [`ArimaTrend`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/enum.ArimaTrend.html) from the following four forms for the original series' zero-based index $t$.

| `ArimaTrend` | $\mu_t$ | Allowed $d$ |
|---|---|---|
| `None` (default) | $0$ | All |
| `Constant` | $c$ (stationary-series mean) | $d = 0$ |
| `Linear` | $b\,t$ (drift for $d = 1$) | $d \le 1$ |
| `ConstantAndLinear` | $c + b\,t$ | $d = 0$ |

Trends eliminated by differencing (such as constants when $d \ge 1$) are rejected with `InvalidInput`.
Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA), ARIMAX with exogenous variables, multivariate VAR, conditional-variance ARCH/GARCH, unit-root tests (ADF, KPSS), and automatic order selection are not provided.
Handle seasonality by constructing a seasonally differenced series or removing seasonal terms by regression before fitting.

### Exact Gaussian likelihood and innovations

Let $\hat x_t$ be the one-step forecast of $x_t$ from observations through time $t$, its error $v_t = x_t - \hat x_t$ the innovation, and $f_t$ its variance.
The joint density of a Gaussian process factors into prediction-error densities, giving negative log-likelihood

$$
-\ln L = \frac{1}{2} \sum_t \left( \frac{v_t^2}{f_t} + \ln f_t + \ln 2\pi \right)
$$


[`PreparedArimaFit`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.PreparedArimaFit.html) applies a Kalman filter to a state-space representation of dimension $m = p + d + q$ to compute $v_t$ and $f_t$.
The filter starts from the stationary covariance of the ARMA part (the solution of an order-$p + 1$ linear system), yielding the exact ARMA likelihood including the first observation.
For $d > 0$, it conditions on the first $d$ observations, which must not be missing.
Each evaluation costs $O(n\, m^3)$.
At missing positions, the filter advances predictions only, preserving the time grid (Jones's method).
The missing and nonfinite policies of `StatisticsOptions` supplied at construction determine which values count as missing.

For AR(1) estimating $\mu$, the first innovation has $v_1 = x_1 - \mu$ and $f_1 = \sigma^2/(1 - \phi^2)$; subsequent predictions and variances are $\hat x_t = \mu + \phi(x_{t-1} - \mu)$ and $f_t = \sigma^2$.
Unlike conditional least squares, which conditions on and discards the first observation, exact likelihood uses its information too.
The two estimates differ more for small $n$ or $\phi$ near 1.

### Asymptotic variances of maximum likelihood estimates and prediction intervals

AR(1) maximum likelihood estimators are asymptotically normal, with variances (Brockwell–Davis §8.8)

$$
\mathrm{Var}\,\hat\phi \approx \frac{1 - \phi^2}{n},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}\,\hat\mu \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{n(1 - \phi)^2},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}\,\hat\sigma^2 \approx \frac{2\sigma^4}{n}
$$


The variance of $\hat\phi$ equals that of Yule–Walker estimation; both are asymptotically equivalent for AR models.
The variance of $\hat\mu$ is $(1 + \phi)/(1 - \phi)$ times the independent-sample value $\sigma_x^2/n$, a factor of nine at $\phi = 0.8$.
Positive autocorrelation makes a series mean less precise than its apparent sample size suggests.
To estimate this uncertainty without assuming a model, use the moving block bootstrap in [Resampling](../resampling/) (`BootstrapDesign::MovingBlock`, `CircularBlock`).

The forecast mean and error variance $h$ steps after the last observation $x_n$ are

$$
\hat x_{n+h} = \mu + \phi^h (x_n - \mu),
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(x_{n+h} - \hat x_{n+h}) = \sigma^2 \frac{1 - \phi^{2h}}{1 - \phi^2}
$$

and approach mean $\mu$ and variance $\gamma(0)$ as $h \to \infty$.
Assuming normality, the 95% prediction interval is $\hat x_{n+h} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}$.
This variance treats parameters as known and excludes estimation error.
For small $n$ or $\phi$ near 1, intervals tend to be narrower than their nominal level requires.
For $d \ge 1$, integration to reverse differencing adds uncertainty, and forecast variance increases without bound with $h$.

### Checking residual whiteness

If the fitted model is correct, standardized innovations $v_t/\sqrt{f_t}$ are approximately independent $\mathrm{N}(0, 1)$ variables.
The Ljung–Box test constructs the following statistic from their autocorrelations $r_k$:

$$
Q = n(n + 2) \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{r_k^2}{n - k}
$$

and compares it with a chi-squared distribution with $m - p - q$ degrees of freedom.
`scioxide-stats` has no function for the Ljung–Box test (or the Box–Pierce test).
Construct it as in the next cell by computing innovation ACF with `time_series_summary` and its p-value from the upper-tail probability of `ChiSquared` in `scioxide-random`.
Regression standard errors robust to serial correlation (HAC, Newey–West) are also unavailable.
Use the moving block bootstrap above instead to estimate standard errors of statistics from correlated series.

### ARIMA fitting and forecasting APIs

`PreparedArimaFit::new(values, validity, order, trend, options)` constructs the problem.
Specify order as [`ArimaOrder`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.ArimaOrder.html)`{ p, d, q }`; parameter ordering is $[\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_q, \text{trend}, \sigma^2]$ (trend coefficients in order $c$, $b$).
It returns `InvalidInput` when series length is at most $d$, any of the first $d$ observations are missing, or no observations remain after the first $d$.
`PreparedArimaFit::from_view` uses one `BatchView` record without copying it.
Create likelihood and forecast workspace `ArimaWorkspace` with `PreparedArimaFit::workspace()` or the fitted model's `ArimaModel::workspace(horizon)` (for forecasts through `horizon` steps).
Evaluations are independent of prior workspace contents, so reuse the same workspace across different parameter evaluations.

- `evaluate_into(&params, &mut innovations, &mut ws, context)` returns the negative log-likelihood at the parameters as [`ArimaLikelihood`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.ArimaLikelihood.html), writing each time's prediction `prediction`, variance `variance`, and innovation `residual` into a sequence of `ArimaInnovation`.
  `ArimaLikelihood` contains `negative_log_likelihood` (including the $\ln 2\pi$ constant), the innovation count included in the likelihood `observations`, and relative residual `covariance_residual` of the linear system for stationary covariance.
  All three values are `None` for the first $d$ times; at missing times, `residual` is `None`.
  It returns `InvalidInput` for a wrong parameter count, and `InvalidInput` for $\sigma^2 \le 0$, a nonstationary AR part, or a noninvertible MA part.
  Polynomial checks use reflection coefficients (Levinson–Durbin partial autocorrelations), rejecting parameters whose absolute values exceed $1 - 64\varepsilon$.
- `model(&params, &mut ws, context)` returns an `ArimaModel` with fixed parameters and the filter advanced through the final time.
- `fit(&initial, &bounds, &mut solver)` minimizes negative log-likelihood with an injected solver.
  No analytic derivatives are supplied, so the solver must use a derivative-free method or approximate gradients by finite differences.
  `LbfgsBFitSolver` in `scioxide-optimize` approximates unavailable analytic gradients by finite differences according to `DerivativeStrategy` in `LbfgsBOptions`.
  After the solver stops, it reevaluates the likelihood once and constructs a model from that state.
  `fit_scoped` accepts an execution context.

The resulting [`ArimaFitReport`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.ArimaFitReport.html) returns the shared `FitReport` through `report()` (termination `termination()`, final negative log-likelihood `objective()`, iteration and evaluation counts `counts()`) and the fitted `ArimaModel` through `model()`.
`model()` is `None` only when cancellation or an evaluation limit stops execution before the final evaluation.
A model is returned even when the solver stops at its own iteration limit (`BudgetExhausted`), so check termination before using it.

`forecast(h)` and `forecast_into(&mut out, &mut ws, context)` on [`ArimaModel`](https://docs.rs/scioxide-stats/0.1.0/scioxide_stats/struct.ArimaModel.html) return the forecast means and error variances for the next $h$ times as `ArimaForecast` (`mean`, `variance`).
`spectral_density(ν, fs)` returns the ARMA part's two-sided spectral density $(\sigma^2/f_s)\,|\theta(z)|^2/|\phi(z)|^2$ ($z = e^{2\pi i\nu/f_s}$), or `InvalidInput` for $|\nu| > f_s/2$.

## Summary

Compute autocorrelation with `time_series_summary` and use the default divisor $n$, preserving positive semidefiniteness, for model identification.
PACF uses Levinson–Durbin recursion; for AR($p$), values beyond order $p$ within $\pm 1.96/\sqrt n$ guide order selection.
Periodogram scatter at each frequency is of the same order as theoretical density; interpret peak positions and overall shape, and use Welch's method for smooth estimation.
Construct the exact Gaussian ARIMA likelihood with `PreparedArimaFit` and fit with a derivative-free solver or one approximating gradients by differences (such as `LbfgsBFitSolver`); check termination is `Converged` before forecasting.
Prediction intervals treat parameters as known, so remember they tend to be too narrow for short series.
