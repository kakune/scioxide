When hundreds to tens of thousands of hypotheses are tested on the same data, as in a search for differential gene expression, false positives accumulate even if each test uses level $\alpha=0.05$.
Even when all hypotheses are true, an average of 50 out of $m=1000$ independent tests become significant by chance.
Multiple testing corrections describe this accumulation through an error rate for the entire family, and change the decision thresholds to keep that error rate at or below the chosen level.
This page checks the four methods implemented by scioxide-stats' `adjust_pvalues` using a numerical example from the literature and repeated experiments.
The repeated experiments use standard errors and confidence intervals to check that, under independent tests, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate is exactly $\pi_0q$ and the familywise error rate stays below its bound.

## Definitions of the error rates

Suppose $m$ null hypotheses $H_1,\dots,H_m$ are tested simultaneously and $m_0$ are true.
Write the proportion of true null hypotheses as $\pi_0=m_0/m$.
Let $R$ be the number of hypotheses rejected by a procedure, $V$ the number of true null hypotheses incorrectly rejected (false discoveries), and $S=R-V$ the number of false null hypotheses correctly rejected.
$m$ and $m_0$ are fixed numbers, while $R$, $V$, and $S$ are random variables that depend on the data.

| | Not rejected | Rejected | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| True null hypotheses | $m_0-V$ | $V$ | $m_0$ |
| False null hypotheses | $m-m_0-S$ | $S$ | $m-m_0$ |
| Total | $m-R$ | $R$ | $m$ |

The familywise error rate (FWER) is the probability of at least one false discovery:

$$
\mathrm{FWER}=P(V\ge1)
$$


The false discovery rate (FDR) is the expectation of the false discovery proportion (FDP), $Q=V/\max(R,1)$, the proportion of rejected hypotheses that are false discoveries:

$$
\mathrm{FDR}=E\!\left[\frac{V}{\max(R,1)}\right]=E\!\left[\frac{V}{R}\,\middle|\,R>0\right]P(R>0)
$$


Define $Q=0$ when $R=0$.
Since $Q\le\mathbf 1\{V\ge1\}$, $\mathrm{FDR}\le\mathrm{FWER}$ always holds, so a method that controls FWER also controls FDR.
If all null hypotheses are true ($m_0=m$), then $V=R$ and thus $Q=\mathbf 1\{V\ge1\}$, so the two error rates coincide.
Power is measured by $E[S]/(m-m_0)$, the expected proportion of false null hypotheses that are rejected.

FWER control seeks to avoid including any false discoveries and suits a small number of confirmatory tests.
FDR control allows a certain proportion of errors in a list of discoveries and suits exploratory analyses that are narrowed down in subsequent experiments.
A list of 100 discoveries with FDR controlled at $q=0.05$ is interpreted as containing about five false discoveries on average.
This does not mean that each discovery has a 95% probability of being correct.

## Four adjustment methods

Let the p-values for the hypotheses be $p_1,\dots,p_m$, and write them in ascending order as $p_{(1)}\le\dots\le p_{(m)}$.
scioxide-stats returns each method's results as adjusted p-values $\tilde p_i$.
Rejecting hypotheses with $\tilde p_i\le\alpha$ ($\tilde p_i\le q$ for FDR methods) gives the same decisions as the rejection rules in the table below.
An adjusted p-value is the smallest level at which the method can reject hypothesis $i$.

| Method | Adjusted p-value | Rejection rule | Controlled error rate and conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bonferroni | $\tilde p_i=\min(1,\,m\,p_i)$ | $p_i\le\alpha/m$ | $\mathrm{FWER}\le m_0\alpha/m\le\alpha$ under arbitrary dependence |
| Holm | $\tilde p_{(i)}=\min\bigl(1,\max_{j\le i}(m-j+1)\,p_{(j)}\bigr)$ | Check $p_{(i)}\le\alpha/(m-i+1)$ in ascending order and reject up to the position before the first failure (step-down) | $\mathrm{FWER}\le\alpha$ under arbitrary dependence |
| Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) | $\tilde p_{(i)}=\min\bigl(1,\min_{j\ge i}m\,p_{(j)}/j\bigr)$ | Set $k=\max\{i:p_{(i)}\le iq/m\}$ and reject $p_{(1)},\dots,p_{(k)}$ (step-up) | $\mathrm{FDR}\le\pi_0q$ under independence or positive regression dependence (PRDS) |
| Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) | Multiply the BH values by $c(m)=\sum_{k=1}^m1/k$ | Apply BH at level $q/c(m)$ | $\mathrm{FDR}\le\pi_0q$ under arbitrary dependence |

The Bonferroni bound follows from the union bound $P(\bigcup_i\{p_i\le\alpha/m\})\le m_0\alpha/m$.
If the null p-values are independent, FWER is exactly $1-(1-\alpha/m)^{m_0}$, slightly below $m_0\alpha/m$.
Holm's method has the same guarantee as Bonferroni, and its adjusted p-values never exceed the Bonferroni values, so it can reject more hypotheses than Bonferroni but never fewer.

The guarantee for BH assumes that the null p-values are uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$.
If the null p-values are mutually independent and also independent of the alternative p-values, equality $\mathrm{FDR}=\pi_0q$ holds for continuous test statistics (Benjamini–Hochberg 1995; Benjamini–Yekutieli 2001).
This equality also shows that BH controls FDR at $\pi_0q$ rather than $q$.
When $\pi_0$ is substantially below 1, BH is more conservative than the nominal level.
One-sided test statistics with a multivariate normal distribution and nonnegative correlations satisfy PRDS; equality no longer holds, but FDR remains at or below $\pi_0q$.
BH has no guarantee under general dependence that includes negative correlations; use BY in that case.
BY is equivalent to applying BH at level $q/c(m)$, so its FDR under independence is $\pi_0q/c(m)$.
For $m=1000$, $c(m)\approx7.485$, making BY much more conservative than BH.

scioxide-stats does not provide adaptive methods that estimate $\pi_0$ and raise the BH level to $q/\hat\pi_0$ (such as Storey's q-values), or the Hochberg and Hommel methods.
If needed, multiply the BH values from `adjust_pvalues` by $\hat\pi_0$ yourself.
The FDR guarantee then depends on the properties of the estimator of $\hat\pi_0$.

## API and computational cost

`adjust_pvalues(pvalues, method)` adjusts the p-values for one family and returns adjusted p-values in the same order as the input.
`method` is either `PValueAdjustment::Familywise(FamilywiseMethod::{Bonferroni, Holm})` or `PValueAdjustment::FalseDiscovery(FalseDiscoveryMethod::{BenjaminiHochberg, BenjaminiYekutieli})`.
Holm, BH, and BY sort the p-values and therefore cost $O(m\log m)$; Bonferroni costs $O(m)$.
Tied p-values are sorted by their input positions, so the results do not depend on input order.
Empty inputs and inputs containing values outside $[0,1]$ or NaN return `InvalidInput`.
Missing p-values are not accepted, so the caller must first decide whether to exclude untested hypotheses from the family or include them with p-value 1.
`adjust_pvalues_with_options` lets you set a workspace limit with `StatisticsOptions::max_bytes` and returns `ResourceLimit` if it is exceeded.

Use `adjust_pvalues_into(pvalues, width, method, options, output, workspace, context)` to adjust many families of the same size.
Place families (lanes) of width `width` consecutively in `pvalues`; each lane is adjusted as a separate family.
The workspace `PValueAdjustmentWorkspace::new(lanes, width, options, context)` holds sorting indices; reusing it for calls with the same shape avoids allocations on each call.
Lanes are processed in parallel when `options` and `context` permit it.
If `pvalues.len()` is not a multiple of `width`, or the workspace was created for a different width or a smaller number of values, the call returns `InvalidInput`.
The repeated experiment below adjusts 50 experiments in one call, with one lane per experiment.

`combine_pvalues` answers a different question.
It combines $k$ p-values from independent tests of the same null hypothesis into one p-value using Fisher, Pearson, Tippett, Stouffer, or Mudholkar–George, and tests the overall null against the alternative that at least one null hypothesis is false.
It does not identify which hypotheses are false, so use the adjustments above to produce a list of discoveries.

## A numerical example from the literature

Section 4 of Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) applies BH to 15 p-values from a comparison of treatments for myocardial infarction, rejecting four at $q=0.05$.
Bonferroni applied to the same p-values retains only the three at or below the threshold $0.05/15\approx0.00333$.
The following cell adjusts these 15 p-values with all four methods and counts the rejections at the decision level.
The first table shows the adjusted p-values for each hypothesis, and the second shows the rejection count for each method.
The final column of the second table is an independent check that applies the rejection rules above (threshold comparisons and step-down or step-up procedures) directly to the p-values; it should agree with the adjusted p-value decisions.

At the default level of 0.05, BH rejects 4 hypotheses, while Bonferroni, Holm, and BY reject 3; the BH result agrees with the paper's four rejections.
The BH adjusted value of the fourth p-value, 0.0095, is $15\times0.0095/4=0.035625$; it is smaller than every $15p_{(j)}/j$ from the fifth position onward and therefore remains the adjusted value.
BY values are $c(15)\approx3.318$ times the BH values, giving $0.0015\times3.318\approx0.0050$ even for the smallest p-value.
At level 0.10, BH's step-up reaches the ninth p-value, $p_{(9)}=0.0459\le9\times0.10/15=0.06$, and the rejection count jumps from 4 to 9.
The step-up procedure continues checking later p-values even when an intermediate value exceeds its threshold, so a small change in level can produce a large change in the number of rejections.
Holm, Bonferroni, and BY still reject only 3 hypotheses at 0.10.
Holm stops at the fourth p-value because $12\times0.0095=0.114>0.10$, and BY's fourth adjusted value, 0.118, also exceeds the level.
Lowering the level to 0.01 gives 3 rejections for BH and 2 for Bonferroni, Holm, and BY.
At every level, decisions based on adjusted p-values agree with the independent check that applies the rules directly.

## Design of the repeated experiments

An error rate is an expectation across many experiments, so it cannot be checked with a single analysis.
Repeat $R$ experiments under the following model to estimate each method's FDR, FWER, and power.

For each hypothesis $i$, observe the test statistic $Z_i=\mu_i+\varepsilon_i$ and test $H_i:\mu_i=0$ against $\mu_i>0$ with a one-sided z test.
The p-value is $p_i=P(N(0,1)>Z_i)=\bar\Phi(Z_i)$.
Set the first $m_0=\mathrm{round}(\pi_0m)$ means to $\mu_i=0$ (true null hypotheses) and the rest to $\mu_i=\delta$ (effect size $\delta>0$).
The null p-values are exactly uniformly distributed.
Set the errors to $\varepsilon_i=\sqrt\rho\,W+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\xi_i$, with independent $W,\xi_i\sim N(0,1)$.
The tests are independent when $\rho=0$; when $\rho>0$, every pair has correlation $\rho$ (equicorrelation).
Since the one-sided test statistics are normally distributed with nonnegative correlations, PRDS holds even when $\rho>0$.

Let $Q_r$ be the false discovery proportion in experiment $r$; the FDR estimate is the sample mean $\bar Q=\frac1R\sum_rQ_r$, with standard error $s_Q/\sqrt R$, where $s_Q$ is the sample standard deviation of $Q_r$.
The FWER estimate $\hat F$ is the proportion of experiments with $V_r\ge1$, and $R\hat F$ follows the binomial distribution $\mathrm{Bin}(R,\mathrm{FWER})$.
Its 95% confidence interval is the Wilson score interval:

$$
\frac{\hat F+\frac{z^2}{2R}\pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat F(1-\hat F)}{R}+\frac{z^2}{4R^2}}}{1+\frac{z^2}{R}},\qquad z=1.96
$$


The Wilson interval stays within $[0,1]$ even when $\hat F$ is close to 0 or 1, and its coverage is closer to nominal than that of the Wald interval $\hat F\pm z\sqrt{\hat F(1-\hat F)/R}$.
Compare with theory by considering both whether the interval contains the theoretical value and how many standard errors separate the estimate from it.
Even a 95% interval misses the theoretical value one time in twenty, so a few misses among many comparisons are unsurprising.

## Repeated experiments

The following cell places the p-values for 50 experiments in one array and adjusts each lane with `adjust_pvalues_into`, repeating this $R/50$ times.
Allocate the workspace and arrays once at the beginning and reuse them across repetitions.
Evaluate decisions at the 10 levels $q=0.02,0.04,\dots,0.20$, counting decisions for all levels from a single adjustment.
The inputs are the number of hypotheses $m$, true-null proportion $\pi_0$, effect size $\delta$, correlation $\rho$, repetition count $R$, and random seed.
This is a costly calculation, so it runs with a button and reuses results for identical inputs.
The results pass to the following display cell.

```rust
//| id: stats-false-discovery-simulation
//| ref: stats-false-discovery-simulation
//| title: "Repeat many one-sided z tests and make decisions with adjusted p-values"
//| run: button
//| cache: inputs
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-random, scioxide-stats]
//| exports: [levels, summary, tests, nulls, correlation, replicates]
//| inputs:
//|   tests: {"type":"range","label":"Number of hypotheses m","description":"Number of hypotheses tested simultaneously in one experiment.","min":100,"max":2000,"step":100,"value":1000}
//|   null_fraction: {"type":"range","label":"True-null proportion π₀ [-]","description":"Setting this to 1 makes every null hypothesis true, so FDR and FWER coincide.","min":0.5,"max":1.0,"step":0.05,"value":0.9}
//|   effect: {"type":"range","label":"Effect size δ [-]","description":"Mean z statistic for false null hypotheses.","min":1.0,"max":5.0,"step":0.5,"value":3.0}
//|   correlation: {"type":"range","label":"Correlation of test statistics ρ [-]","description":"Nonnegative correlation shared by every pair (equicorrelation).","min":0.0,"max":0.8,"step":0.1,"value":0.0}
//|   replicates: {"type":"range","label":"Replicate count R","description":"Changes the Monte Carlo error of FDR and FWER.","min":100,"max":1000,"step":100,"value":400}
//|   seed: {"type":"range","label":"Random seed","min":1,"max":100,"step":1,"value":1}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"global-null","label":"π₀ = 1","description":"All null hypotheses are true, and BH's FDR equals q itself.","inputs":{"null_fraction":1.0}}
//|   - {"id":"correlated","label":"ρ = 0.5","description":"Equality no longer holds, and the false discovery proportion becomes more variable.","inputs":{"correlation":0.5}}
//|   - {"id":"weak-effect","label":"δ = 1.5","description":"Power decreases, while BH's FDR remains π₀q.","inputs":{"effect":1.5}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"path":"/exports/nulls","equals":900}
//|       - {"path":"/exports/summary/2/curves/1/fdr","approx":{"value":0.0376,"atol":1e-3}}
//|       - {"path":"/exports/summary/3/curves/9/fdr","approx":{"value":0.0257,"atol":1e-3}}
//|       - {"path":"/exports/summary/0/curves/4/fwer","approx":{"value":0.0775,"atol":1e-3}}
//|   - case: global-null
//|     expect:
//|       - {"path":"/exports/summary/2/curves/4/fdr","approx":{"value":0.0925,"atol":1e-3}}
//|       - {"path":"/exports/summary/2/curves/4/power","equals":null}
use scioxide_core::{ExecutionContext, RealScalar};
use scioxide_random::{Distribution, Normal, SeedableRng, SplitMix64, Survival};
use scioxide_stats::{
    FalseDiscoveryMethod, FamilywiseMethod, PValueAdjustment, PValueAdjustmentWorkspace,
    StatisticsOptions, adjust_pvalues_into,
};

let (m, r) = (tests as usize, replicates as usize);
let nulls = (null_fraction * m as f64).round() as usize;
let normal = Normal::new(0.0_f64, 1.0).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mut rng = SplitMix64::from_seed(seed as u64);
let (shared, own) = (
    RealScalar::sqrt(correlation),
    RealScalar::sqrt(1.0 - correlation),
);
let levels: Vec<f64> = (1..=10).map(|j| 0.02 * j as f64).collect();
let methods = [
    (
        "Bonferroni",
        PValueAdjustment::Familywise(FamilywiseMethod::Bonferroni),
    ),
    ("Holm", PValueAdjustment::Familywise(FamilywiseMethod::Holm)),
    (
        "BH",
        PValueAdjustment::FalseDiscovery(FalseDiscoveryMethod::BenjaminiHochberg),
    ),
    (
        "BY",
        PValueAdjustment::FalseDiscovery(FalseDiscoveryMethod::BenjaminiYekutieli),
    ),
];
// Adjust experiments in batches of 50. Lane j contains the m p-values for one experiment;
// the first nulls hypotheses are true null hypotheses.
let lanes = 50;
let options = StatisticsOptions::new();
let context = ExecutionContext::sequential();
let mut workspace =
    PValueAdjustmentWorkspace::new(lanes, m, options, context).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let (mut pvalues, mut adjusted) = (vec![0.0_f64; lanes * m], vec![0.0_f64; lanes * m]);
// totals[method][level] = [sum of Q, sum of Q², count of V ≥ 1, sum of correct rejections, sum of rejections]
let mut totals = vec![vec![[0.0_f64; 5]; levels.len()]; methods.len()];
for _ in 0..r / lanes {
    for lane in pvalues.chunks_mut(m) {
        let common = normal.sample(&mut rng).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        for (i, p) in lane.iter_mut().enumerate() {
            let own_noise = normal.sample(&mut rng).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
            let mean = if i < nulls { 0.0 } else { effect };
            // One-sided z-test p-value P(N(0, 1) > z)
            *p = normal
                .sf(mean + shared * common + own * own_noise)
                .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        }
    }
    for (k, (_, method)) in methods.iter().enumerate() {
        adjust_pvalues_into(
            &pvalues,
            m,
            *method,
            options,
            &mut adjusted,
            &mut workspace,
            context,
        )
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        for (level, &q) in levels.iter().enumerate() {
            for lane in adjusted.chunks(m) {
                let false_hits = lane[..nulls].iter().filter(|&&p| p <= q).count();
                let true_hits = lane[nulls..].iter().filter(|&&p| p <= q).count();
                let total = false_hits + true_hits;
                let proportion = if total == 0 {
                    0.0
                } else {
                    false_hits as f64 / total as f64
                };
                let cell = &mut totals[k][level];
                cell[0] += proportion;
                cell[1] += proportion * proportion;
                cell[2] += f64::from(u8::from(false_hits > 0));
                cell[3] += true_hits as f64;
                cell[4] += total as f64;
            }
        }
    }
}
let mut summary = Vec::with_capacity(methods.len());
for ((name, _), rows) in methods.iter().zip(&totals) {
    let mut curves = Vec::with_capacity(levels.len());
    for (&q, cell) in levels.iter().zip(rows) {
        let fdr = cell[0] / r as f64;
        let spread = ((cell[1] - r as f64 * fdr * fdr) / (r - 1) as f64).max(0.0);
        let power = (nulls < m).then(|| cell[3] / (r * (m - nulls)) as f64);
        curves.push(serde_json::json!({
            "q": q,
            "fdr": fdr,
            "fdr_se": RealScalar::sqrt(spread / r as f64),
            "fwer": cell[2] / r as f64,
            "power": power,
            "discoveries": cell[4] / r as f64
        }));
    }
    summary.push(serde_json::json!({ "method": name, "curves": curves }));
}
export!(levels, levels);
export!(summary, summary);
export!(tests, m);
export!(nulls, nulls);
export!(correlation, correlation);
export!(replicates, r);
```

## Reading results

The display cell uses the preceding results to produce three plots and a table at the selected level $q$.
The first plot compares BH and BY FDR estimates and ±1.96 SE bands with the independent-case theoretical values $\pi_0q$ and $\pi_0q/c(m)$ (dashed).
The second plots Bonferroni and Holm FWER, together with the independent-case Bonferroni FWER $1-(1-q/m)^{m_0}$ (dashed).
The third shows the power of all four methods.

With the default inputs ($m=1000$, $\pi_0=0.9$, $\delta=3$, $\rho=0$, $R=400$, seed 1), the BH FDR estimates agree with $\pi_0q$ within ±1.96 SE at all 10 levels.
At $q=0.1$, the estimate is $0.0916\pm0.0016$ (standard error), compared with the theoretical value 0.09, a difference of about one standard error.
The 10 estimates are correlated because they use the same experiments; all lie slightly above theory, but the largest difference is only 1.6 standard errors.
The BY estimates also follow the dashed $\pi_0q/c(m)$ curve ($c(1000)\approx7.485$), reaching 0.0134 at $q=0.1$ (theory: 0.0120), about 1/7 of BH.
This agreement checks the equality $\mathrm{FDR}=\pi_0q$ under independence, including both the mean and the size of the error.

At $\alpha=0.1$, Bonferroni's estimated FWER is 0.0775 (Wilson interval: 0.055–0.108), and the interval contains the independent-case theoretical value $1-(1-\alpha/m)^{m_0}\approx0.0861$.
The theoretical value lies slightly below the union bound $m_0\alpha/m=0.09$.
Holm's FWER and power are almost identical to Bonferroni's.
After $k$ rejections, Holm's threshold is $\alpha/(m-k)$; with an average of about 24 rejections, this relaxes $\alpha/m$ by only a factor of about 1.02.
BH has the highest power, 0.72 at $q=0.1$, compared with 0.24 for Bonferroni and Holm at the same level.
By allowing false discoveries through FDR control, BH finds about three times as many false null hypotheses as the FWER methods.

At $\pi_0=1$ there are no false null hypotheses, so the power column is empty.
BH's FDR then equals $q$ itself and coincides with FWER (0.0925 at $q=0.1$, standard error 0.0145).
Since $Q=\mathbf 1\{V\ge1\}$, controlling FDR at $q$ is equivalent to controlling FWER at $q$.
At $\rho=0.5$, all test statistics rise and fall together through the common $W$, separating experiments into those with no false discoveries and those with many.
BH's estimated FDR at $q=0.1$ is $0.0808\pm0.0086$, staying below $\pi_0q=0.09$, but the standard error grows to about five times its independent-case value, and the false discovery proportion in one analysis can differ greatly from $q$.
FDR guarantees an expectation; it does not bound the false discovery proportion of each analysis.
At $\delta=1.5$, BH's power falls to 0.037 at $q=0.1$, but its FDR estimate $0.0957\pm0.0082$ agrees with $\pi_0q$ and does not depend on the effect size.

## Summary

FWER is the probability of at least one false discovery, while FDR is the expected proportion of false discoveries among rejected hypotheses; $\mathrm{FDR}\le\mathrm{FWER}$ always holds.
`adjust_pvalues` returns Bonferroni, Holm, BH, and BY adjusted p-values in input order; `adjust_pvalues_into` and `PValueAdjustmentWorkspace` adjust many families lane by lane without allocations.
For independent tests, BH's FDR equals $\pi_0q$ and BY's equals $\pi_0q/c(m)$; the repeated-experiment estimates agree with these values within their standard errors.
With positive correlations, BH's FDR remains at or below $\pi_0q$, but the greater variability in the false discovery proportion allows the result of one analysis to differ from the expectation.
When the direction of dependence is unknown, use BY or an FWER method to obtain a guarantee at the cost of lower power.
