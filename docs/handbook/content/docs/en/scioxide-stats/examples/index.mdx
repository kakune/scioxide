Statistical computation requires separate checks of the algorithm's numerical correctness and the statistical model's validity.
Algorithmic correctness asks whether the defined quantity is computed despite rounding and cancellation.
Model validity asks whether inferential assumptions, such as sample independence and the population distribution, fit the data.
Even an accurate Welford recurrence gives overly narrow mean confidence intervals if autocorrelation is ignored; a histogram that omits out-of-range observations gives incorrect proportions.
The examples in this section compute concrete applications with `scioxide-stats` and independently check results against analytical solutions, counting, published values, or another algorithm.

## How the applications are divided

The first five examples address the correctness of descriptive-statistics calculations themselves.
Sensor series, communication latency, partitioned aggregation, distribution functions, and binning show how baseline cancellation, sparse tail observations, merge order, and the handling of ties and out-of-range values affect computed results.
The confidence-interval coverage example shows that even an accurate algorithm fails to maintain nominal coverage when independence breaks down.

The next three examples cover regression, contingency-table, and survival-time models.
The original regression quartet demonstrates the limits of summary statistics using exact construction identities.
Simpson's paradox illustrates stratification versus pooling, and leukemia remission times illustrate estimation and testing with censoring; those two examples compare with published values.

The remaining five examples cover multiple testing, resampling, time series, sensitivity analysis, and Bayesian inference.
All use repeated experiments or closed forms to compare estimated error rates, coverage, and indices with theoretical values and standard errors or confidence intervals.

## Summary

Each example computes a concrete application with `scioxide-stats` and independently checks it against analytical solutions, counting, published values, or another algorithm.
Check numerical correctness through differences from analytical values or direct counts; check inferential validity by comparing repeated-experiment coverage or error rates with nominal levels.
Varying controls for baselines, tail observations, autocorrelation, and sample sizes quantifies how estimates and intervals change when assumptions fail.
Algorithm pages give the assumptions and derivations; API guides give the type and function contracts.
