When simulation inputs are uncertain, you may want to know how much of the output variability comes from each input.
Variance-based global sensitivity analysis separates output variance into contributions from individual inputs and their interactions, expressing their proportions as Sobol indices.
Monte Carlo estimation gives the index estimates statistical uncertainty.
This page uses the Ishigami function, whose indices have closed forms, to compare scioxide-stats estimates with those values.
It places estimation errors alongside bootstrap intervals and repeated-estimate standard errors, and examines how error decreases as the sample size grows.

## The Ishigami function and variance decomposition

For independent uniform random variables $X_1,X_2,X_3\sim U(-\pi,\pi)$, the Ishigami function (Ishigami–Homma 1990) is defined by

$$
Y=f(X_1,X_2,X_3)=\sin X_1+a\sin^2X_2+b\,X_3^4\sin X_1
$$


The values $a=7$ and $b=0.1$ are commonly used to validate sensitivity analysis (Saltelli et al. 2000).
$X_2$ acts alone, $X_3$ acts only through its product with $X_1$, and $X_1$ acts both alone and through the product.

With independent inputs, a square-integrable $f$ has a unique decomposition into mutually orthogonal components (Sobol or ANOVA decomposition):

$$
f=f_0+\sum_if_i(X_i)+\sum_{i<j}f_{ij}(X_i,X_j)+f_{123}(X_1,X_2,X_3)
$$


Each component is a conditional expectation with lower-order components removed; for example, $f_i=E[Y\mid X_i]-f_0$.
Orthogonality also decomposes the output variance as $V=\sum_iV_i+\sum_{i<j}V_{ij}+V_{123}$.
The first-order index $S_i=V_i/V$ is the proportion contributed by $X_i$ alone; the total-effect index $S_{Ti}$ is the proportion contributed by all terms containing $X_i$:

$$
S_i=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(E[Y\mid X_i])}{V},\qquad S_{Ti}=\frac{E[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X_{\sim i})]}{V}=1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(E[Y\mid X_{\sim i}])}{V}
$$


$X_{\sim i}$ denotes the inputs other than $X_i$.
$S_{Ti}-S_i$ measures the interactions involving $X_i$; if $S_{Ti}=0$, fixing $X_i$ does not change the output variance.

For the Ishigami function, trigonometric orthogonality together with $E[X_3^4]=\pi^4/5$ and $E[X_3^8]=\pi^8/9$ gives the component variances

$$
V_1=\frac12\left(1+\frac{b\pi^4}{5}\right)^2,\qquad V_2=\frac{a^2}{8},\qquad V_{13}=b^2\pi^8\left(\frac1{18}-\frac1{50}\right)=\frac{8b^2\pi^8}{225},
$$

$$
V_3=V_{12}=V_{23}=V_{123}=0,\qquad V=\frac12+\frac{a^2}8+\frac{b\pi^4}5+\frac{b^2\pi^8}{18}
$$


The indices are $S_1=V_1/V$, $S_2=V_2/V$, $S_3=0$, $S_{T1}=(V_1+V_{13})/V$, $S_{T2}=S_2$, and $S_{T3}=V_{13}/V$.
With $a=7$ and $b=0.1$, $V\approx13.845$, giving $S_1\approx0.3139$, $S_2\approx0.4424$, $S_{T1}\approx0.5576$, and $S_{T3}\approx0.2437$.
The first-order index of $X_3$ is 0, but its total effect is 0.24.
The first-order index alone makes $X_3$ appear inactive, yet even after fixing $X_1$ and $X_2$, variation in $X_3$ alone leaves an average of 24% of the output variance.
Simplifying by replacing $X_3$ with a constant is justified only when $S_{T3}$ is small.

## Estimation with the pick-freeze method

The variance of a conditional expectation can be estimated as the covariance of outputs at two points sharing the same $X_i$.
Prepare independent $n\times d$ sample matrices $A$ and $B$, and write $A_B^{(i)}$ for $A$ with only column $i$ replaced by column $i$ of $B$.
Evaluate the model at every row of $A$, $B$, and the $d$ matrices $A_B^{(i)}$, for $n(d+2)$ evaluations.
Let $m$ and $\hat V$ be the mean and variance of the $2n$ outputs from $f(A)$ and $f(B)$; scioxide-stats uses

$$
\hat S_i=\frac1{n\hat V}\sum_{k=1}^n\bigl(f(B)_k-m\bigr)\bigl(f(A_B^{(i)})_k-f(A)_k\bigr),\qquad
\hat S_{Ti}=\frac1{2n\hat V}\sum_{k=1}^n\bigl(f(A)_k-f(A_B^{(i)})_k\bigr)^2
$$


The first-order estimator is due to Saltelli et al. (2010), and the total-effect estimator to Jansen (1999).
$f(A_B^{(i)})_k$ and $f(B)_k$ share only $X_i$, while $f(A_B^{(i)})_k$ and $f(A)_k$ share everything except $X_i$; the former covariance is therefore $V_i$, and half the expected squared difference in the latter is $E[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X_{\sim i})]$.
Because this is a ratio with numerator and denominator estimated from the same sample, it is generally biased, with bias $O(1/n)$.
Estimates are not clipped to $[0,1]$, so $S_3$, whose true value is 0, can be negative.
Sums use extended-exponent accumulation, so adding a constant to the outputs or multiplying them by a constant changes the estimates only within rounding error.

`sobol_indices_monte_carlo(d, n, root, sampler, model)` constructs this design with Monte Carlo sampling.
$A_k$ and $B_k$ in row $k$ are drawn from generator `root.split(k)`, so the results do not depend on execution parallelism.
`sampler(j, rng)` returns one value from the marginal distribution of coordinate $j$ and assumes mutually independent inputs.
The returned `SobolMonteCarloResult` contains the indices `indices()` and all outputs `evaluations()`.
Storing the outputs uses $O(nd)$ memory.

`sobol_bootstrap(evaluations, root, replicates, confidence)` resamples rows with replacement, recomputes the indices, and returns percentile intervals.
The values $f(A)$, $f(B)$, and $f(A_B^{(i)})$ within a row are sampled together.
The cost is $O(Bnd)$, and validity requires independent rows, so do not use it with quasi-Monte Carlo points.

`sobol_indices_qmc(plan, d, points, transform, model)` uses randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (RQMC).
`QmcReplicatePlan` independently scrambles a $2d$-dimensional Sobol sequence $R$ times, using the first $d$ coordinates of each point as a row of $A$ and the remaining coordinates as a row of $B$.
`transform(j, u)` maps coordinates in $[0,1)$ to input coordinates (here, $-\pi+2\pi u$).
The returned indices are the means of the $R$ replicate estimates, and `randomized_standard_error()` returns the replicate standard deviations divided by $\sqrt R$ as standard errors.
The number of evaluations is $R\cdot\text{points}\cdot(d+2)$.
scioxide-random generates and scrambles the sequences ([Quasi-random sequences and Brownian bridges](../../../scioxide-random/algorithms/quasi-random-and-brownian-bridges/)).

For smooth integrands, RQMC error decreases faster than Monte Carlo's $O(n^{-1/2})$.
The actual benefit depends on the integrand and the index; check it in the repeated experiments below.
When model evaluations are expensive, inactive inputs are often screened out first with methods such as Morris.
scioxide-stats does not provide the Morris method, FAST, or second-order Sobol indices $S_{ij}$.
If second-order indices are needed, also evaluate the model on matrices with column $j$ of $B$ replaced by the corresponding column of $A$, and compute the Saltelli (2002) estimator yourself.

The following definitions block provides the model used by the page's two cells.
`ishigami` is the model, `exact_indices` gives the closed-form indices (in the order $S_1,S_2,S_3,S_{T1},S_{T2},S_{T3}$), and `uniform` implements the coordinate map $u\mapsto-\pi+2\pi u$.
`monte_carlo` gives a Monte Carlo estimate with $n$ rows; `randomized` gives an RQMC estimate with eight replicates and $n$ points in total; both evaluate the model $5n$ times.
`ordered` arranges the estimates in an array in the same order as the closed-form values.

```rust
//| id: stats-ishigami-model
//| kind: definitions
//| title: "The Ishigami function, closed-form indices, and two estimators"
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-random, scioxide-stats]
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, RealScalar};
use scioxide_random::{Philox4x64, QmcPlan, QmcReplicateOptions, QmcReplicatePlan, Rng, SeedableRng};
use scioxide_stats::{
    SobolIndices, SobolMonteCarloResult, sobol_indices_monte_carlo, sobol_indices_qmc,
};

/// f(x) = sin x₁ + a sin² x₂ + b x₃⁴ sin x₁
pub(crate) fn ishigami(x: &[f64], a: f64, b: f64) -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
    let s1 = RealScalar::sin(x[0]);
    let s2 = RealScalar::sin(x[1]);
    Ok(s1 + a * s2 * s2 + b * RealScalar::powi(x[2], 4) * s1)
}

/// Closed-form [S₁, S₂, S₃, S_T1, S_T2, S_T3] and total variance V
pub(crate) fn exact_indices(a: f64, b: f64) -> ([f64; 6], f64) {
    let pi4 = RealScalar::powi(core::f64::consts::PI, 4);
    let v1 = 0.5 * RealScalar::powi(1.0 + b * pi4 / 5.0, 2);
    let v2 = a * a / 8.0;
    let v13 = 8.0 * b * b * pi4 * pi4 / 225.0;
    let v = 0.5 + a * a / 8.0 + b * pi4 / 5.0 + b * b * pi4 * pi4 / 18.0;
    ([v1 / v, v2 / v, 0.0, (v1 + v13) / v, v2 / v, v13 / v], v)
}

/// Map a coordinate in [0, 1) to U(−π, π).
pub(crate) fn uniform(u: f64) -> f64 {
    -core::f64::consts::PI + 2.0 * core::f64::consts::PI * u
}

/// Monte Carlo estimate with n rows. Row k is drawn from split k of Philox4x64(seed).
pub(crate) fn monte_carlo(
    rows: usize,
    a: f64,
    b: f64,
    seed: u64,
) -> Result<SobolMonteCarloResult, SciOxideError> {
    let root = Philox4x64::from_seed(seed);
    let sampler = |_: usize, rng: &mut Philox4x64| -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> {
        let u: f64 = rng.next_float()?;
        Ok(uniform(u))
    };
    sobol_indices_monte_carlo(3, rows, &root, sampler, |x: &[f64]| ishigami(x, a, b))
}

/// Mean estimates from eight scramblings of a six-dimensional Sobol sequence, with rows/8 points per replicate.
pub(crate) fn randomized(
    rows: usize,
    a: f64,
    b: f64,
    seed: u64,
) -> Result<SobolIndices, SciOxideError> {
    let root = Philox4x64::from_seed(seed);
    let options = QmcReplicateOptions::default()
        .with_replicates(8)
        .with_points_per_replicate(rows / 8);
    let plan = QmcReplicatePlan::new(&QmcPlan::sobol(6)?, &root, options)?;
    let transform = |_: usize, u: f64| -> Result<f64, SciOxideError> { Ok(uniform(u)) };
    sobol_indices_qmc(&plan, 3, rows / 8, transform, |x: &[f64]| ishigami(x, a, b))
}

/// Arrange in the order [S₁, S₂, S₃, S_T1, S_T2, S_T3].
pub(crate) fn ordered(first: &[f64], total: &[f64]) -> [f64; 6] {
    [first[0], first[1], first[2], total[0], total[1], total[2]]
}
```

## A single estimate

The following cell estimates the indices with the selected method and row count and compares them with the closed-form values.
Monte Carlo uses percentile intervals from 100 `sobol_bootstrap` resamples; RQMC uses the mean of eight replicates $\pm t_{0.975,7}\times$ standard error as a 95% interval ($t_{0.975,7}\approx2.365$).
Both use $5n$ model evaluations, matching the amount of computation.
The table shows each index's closed-form value, estimate, interval, and whether the interval contains the closed-form value.
The bar chart places estimates beside closed-form values; the error-bar plot shows estimate minus closed-form value together with the interval.
If an error bar in the difference plot does not touch the horizontal line at 0, estimation uncertainty alone is unlikely to explain the difference.
The bootstrap costs $O(Bnd)$, so the cell runs with a button.

```rust

//| ref: stats-ishigami-sensitivity-api-1
//| title: "Estimate Sobol indices of the Ishigami function and compare with closed-form values"
//| run: button
//| uses: [stats-ishigami-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-random, scioxide-stats]

//|   method: {"type":"select","label":"Estimation method","options":[{"label":"Monte Carlo (bootstrap intervals)","value":"mc"},{"label":"Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (8 replicates)","value":"rqmc"}],"value":"mc"}

//|       - {"artifact":"indices","path":"/rows/5/1","equals":0}
```

With the default inputs (Monte Carlo, $n=1024$, $b=0.1$, seed 2), all six estimates are within 0.06 of the closed-form values, and bootstrap interval widths range from 0.05 to 0.14.
Five of the six intervals contain the closed-form values; the $S_{T3}$ interval misses because its lower limit, 0.243687, lies just above the closed-form value 0.243684 (both display as 0.2437 at the table's four-digit precision).
With six 95% intervals, it is not unusual for one to miss.
The $S_3$ estimate is negative, $-0.052$, but its interval contains 0.
$S_1+S_2+S_3$ is less than 1; in closed form, the remaining approximately 0.24 is the interaction between $X_1$ and $X_3$.

Switching to RQMC at the same $5n$ evaluations narrows the $S_2$ interval to about 1/5 of its Monte Carlo width, $S_{T2}$ to about 1/2, and $S_{T3}$ to about 0.8, while moving the estimates closer to the closed-form values.
In contrast, the intervals for $S_1$ and $S_{T1}$ become wider.
RQMC intervals use the spread of eight estimates with 128 points per replicate; for indices with large between-replicate variability, this and the $t$ value with 7 degrees of freedom widen the intervals.
The benefit of RQMC varies by index; compare errors in the following repeated experiments rather than ranking methods from a single result.
With $n=256$, Monte Carlo interval widths increase to 0.10–0.37.
At $b=0$, the function becomes additive, $f=\sin X_1+a\sin^2X_2$, giving $S_{T3}=S_3=0$ and $S_1=S_{T1}$.
The estimates of $S_3$ and $S_{T3}$, including their intervals, become exactly 0.
Since $f(A)$ and $f(A_B^{(3)})$ differ only in $X_3$ and $X_3$ does not enter the output, their difference is exactly 0.

## Sample size and error decay

In a single estimate, chance affects whether the interval contains the closed-form value.
For each row count $n=256,1024,4096$, the following cell repeats Monte Carlo and RQMC estimation $R$ times each with different seeds.
It records the sum of squared estimation errors for each index (used to compute root mean square error, RMSE) and the count of RQMC 95% intervals containing the closed-form value.
Monte Carlo bootstrap intervals cost $O(Bnd)$ per estimate, so the repeated experiments compare errors without constructing those intervals.
The default $R=20$ requires about one million model evaluations in total, so the cell runs with a button and reuses results for identical inputs.

```rust
//| id: stats-ishigami-convergence
//| ref: stats-ishigami-convergence
//| title: "Repeat estimates at different row counts and count errors and RQMC interval coverage"
//| run: button
//| cache: inputs
//| uses: [stats-ishigami-model]
//| crates: [scioxide-stats]
//| exports: [sizes, replicates, errors, covered]
//| inputs:
//|   replicates: {"type":"range","label":"Replicate count R","description":"Changes the Monte Carlo error of RMSE and coverage estimates.","min":10,"max":50,"step":10,"value":20}
//|   seed: {"type":"range","label":"Random seed","min":1,"max":100,"step":1,"value":1}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"few","label":"R = 10","description":"The Wilson coverage interval widens.","inputs":{"replicates":10}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"path":"/exports/sizes/2","equals":4096}
use scioxide_stats::ConfidenceProbability;

let (a, b) = (7.0, 0.1);
let (exact, _) = exact_indices(a, b);
let t = ConfidenceProbability::new(0.95)
    .and_then(|c| c.student_t_critical_value(7.0))
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let sizes = [256_usize, 1024, 4096];
// errors[method][row count][index] = sum of squared errors (method 0: Monte Carlo; 1: RQMC)
// covered[row count][index] = count of RQMC intervals containing the closed-form value
let mut errors = [[[0.0_f64; 6]; 3]; 2];
let mut covered = [[0.0_f64; 6]; 3];
for (s, &n) in sizes.iter().enumerate() {
    for j in 0..replicates as usize {
        let replicate_seed = 1_000 * seed as u64 + j as u64;
        let mc = monte_carlo(n, a, b, replicate_seed).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        let mc_values = ordered(mc.indices().first_order(), mc.indices().total_order());
        let qmc = randomized(n, a, b, replicate_seed).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
        let qmc_values = ordered(qmc.first_order(), qmc.total_order());
        let (first, total) = qmc.randomized_standard_error().ok_or("Standard error is absent")?;
        let se = ordered(first, total);
        for k in 0..6 {
            errors[0][s][k] += (mc_values[k] - exact[k]) * (mc_values[k] - exact[k]);
            errors[1][s][k] += (qmc_values[k] - exact[k]) * (qmc_values[k] - exact[k]);
            covered[s][k] += f64::from(u8::from((qmc_values[k] - exact[k]).abs() <= t * se[k]));
        }
    }
}
export!(sizes, sizes);
export!(replicates, replicates as usize);
export!(errors, errors);
export!(covered, covered);
```

The display cell plots the mean RMSE across the six indices against row count on logarithmic axes, comparing it with dashed reference slopes $n^{-1/2}$ and $n^{-1}$.
The reference lines pass through the Monte Carlo point at $n=256$.
For the selected index, the table shows both methods' RMSE by row count, together with RQMC interval coverage and a Wilson 95% interval.

```rust
let sizes: Vec<f64> =
    serde_json::from_value(run["sizes"].clone()).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let r = run["replicates"].as_f64().ok_or("Missing replicate count")?;
let errors: [[[f64; 6]; 3]; 2] =
    serde_json::from_value(run["errors"].clone()).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let covered: [[f64; 6]; 3] =
    serde_json::from_value(run["covered"].clone()).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let names = ["S₁", "S₂", "S₃", "S_T1", "S_T2", "S_T3"];
let k = names
    .iter()
    .position(|&name| name == index)
    .ok_or("Invalid index name")?;
// RMSE = √(sum of squared errors / R)
let rmse = |m: usize, s: usize, i: usize| (errors[m][s][i] / r).sqrt();
let average = |m: usize, s: usize| (0..6).map(|i| rmse(m, s, i)).sum::<f64>() / 6.0;
let curve = |m: usize| -> Vec<[f64; 2]> {
    (0..sizes.len())
        .map(|s| [sizes[s], average(m, s)])
        .collect()
};
let reference = |power: f64| -> Vec<[f64; 2]> {
    sizes
        .iter()
        .map(|&n| [n, average(0, 0) * (sizes[0] / n).powf(power)])
        .collect()
};
let dashed = serde_json::json!({ "lineType": "dashed", "showSymbol": false });
emit_chart!(serde_json::json!({
    "kind": "line",
    "xLabel": "Row count n (5n evaluations) [-]",
    "yLabel": "Mean RMSE across six indices [-]",
    "xType": "log",
    "yType": "log",
    "dataZoom": true,
    "tooltip": true,
    "series": [
        { "name": "Monte Carlo", "points": curve(0) },
        { "name": "RQMC (8 replicates)", "points": curve(1) },
        { "name": "n^(−1/2) reference line", "points": reference(0.5), "style": dashed },
        { "name": "n^(−1) reference line", "points": reference(1.0), "style": dashed }
    ]
}));
// Wilson score interval (95%)
let wilson = |p: f64| {
    let z = 1.96_f64;
    let center = (p + z * z / (2.0 * r)) / (1.0 + z * z / r);
    let radius = z * (p * (1.0 - p) / r + z * z / (4.0 * r * r)).sqrt() / (1.0 + z * z / r);
    (center - radius, center + radius)
};
let rows: Vec<_> = (0..sizes.len())
    .map(|s| {
        let rate = covered[s][k] / r;
        let (low, high) = wilson(rate);
        (
            sizes[s] as usize,
            rmse(0, s, k),
            rmse(1, s, k),
            rate,
            low,
            high,
        )
    })
    .collect();
let fixed3 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 3 });
let sci3 = serde_json::json!({ "notation": "scientific", "significantDigits": 3 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Row count n", "type": "integer" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Monte Carlo RMSE [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "2", "label": "RQMC RMSE [-]", "type": "number", "format": sci3 },
    { "key": "3", "label": "RQMC interval coverage [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed3 },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Wilson interval lower endpoint [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed3 },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Wilson interval upper endpoint [-]", "type": "number", "format": fixed3 }
]);
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "rmse" })
);
```

With the default inputs ($R=20$, seed 1), the mean RMSE across the six indices is 0.052, 0.027, and 0.013 for Monte Carlo, roughly halving whenever $n$ quadruples and following the $n^{-1/2}$ reference line.
RQMC gives 0.048, 0.016, and 0.0069: similar to Monte Carlo at $n=256$, then decreasing faster than $n^{-1/2}$ and reaching about half the Monte Carlo error at $n=4096$.
Decay varies by index: RQMC's $S_2$ RMSE is 0.065, 0.012, and 0.0024, decreasing faster than the $n^{-1}$ reference line, while $S_{T1}$ gives 0.052, 0.021, and 0.016, only 0.5–0.7 times the Monte Carlo values (0.076, 0.046, 0.026), with decay close to $n^{-1/2}$.
RQMC error decay depends on the smoothness and effective dimension of the estimator's integrand, so the same point sequence benefits different indices by different amounts.
RQMC interval coverage is 0.9–1, and every Wilson interval for $R=20$ (lower limits 0.70–0.84) contains 0.95.
$R$ is too small to distinguish coverage differences; this only checks that coverage does not deviate greatly.

## Summary

Sobol indices express output variance as proportions from individual inputs and interactions; for the Ishigami function, $S_{T3}\approx0.24$ despite $S_3=0$, so first-order indices alone can misrepresent an input's importance.
`sobol_indices_monte_carlo` estimates first-order and total-effect indices with $n(d+2)$ pick-freeze evaluations, and `sobol_bootstrap` adds intervals by resampling rows with replacement.
`sobol_indices_qmc` reduces error for many indices at the same evaluation count and returns replicate standard errors `randomized_standard_error`.
Estimates are not clipped to $[0,1]$, so interpret indices near 0 together with their intervals.
Compare differences from closed-form values with interval widths, and do not judge a method solely from an interval that misses in one estimate.
