Most service requests complete quickly, while a few take much longer because of garbage collection or congested queues.
The mean alone hides a few slow requests, and quantiles such as P95 or P99 alone do not show how slow the requests beyond them are.
This page varies congestion strength and observation count, computes quantiles and upper tail means (expected shortfall) with `EmpiricalDistribution`, and examines estimation limits at levels where the tail mass is less than one observation.
The second half examines what frequency and mass weights compute when latencies have been aggregated into bucket counts.

## Quantiles and tail means

For a latency sample of $n$ observations, the level-$p$ quantile $q_p$ is a boundary by which at least a proportion $p$ of requests complete.
Finite-sample quantiles have no unique definition; Hyndman and Fan (1996) classify nine definitions.
Type 1 (`QuantileMethod::NearestRank`) is a step function returning the observation at rank $\lceil np \rceil$ itself; type 7 (`QuantileMethod::Linear`) linearly interpolates between order statistics around the zero-based position $h = (n-1)p$.
The two types differ in tails where adjacent order statistics are far apart.

With quantile function $Q(u)$, the upper tail mean is

$$
\mathrm{ES}_p = \frac{1}{1-p}\int_p^1 Q(u)\,du
$$

It is the mean latency of the proportion $1-p$ of requests beyond the quantile.
Even with identical quantiles, extremely slow requests in the tail increase $\mathrm{ES}_p$.
In a finite sample, the upper mass $m = n(1-p)$ need not be an integer.
`EmpiricalDistribution::tail_mean` evaluates the integral above exactly for the empirical quantile function, including all of the largest $\lfloor m \rfloor$ observations and the fraction $m - \lfloor m \rfloor$ of the next observation ([Apportioning boundary mass](oxref:stats-tail-boundary-mass-apportioning)).
The tail mean therefore varies continuously with $p$.
Observations tied at the boundary are treated as a single boundary group, from which only the required mass is taken.

`tail_mean` returns `TailMeanResult`, containing the mean `mean`, tail mass `tail_mass`, boundary value `boundary_value`, and mass taken from the boundary `boundary_mass`.
Requesting an upper tail at $p = 1$ returns `InvalidInput` because the tail is empty.
`EmpiricalDistribution::new` copies and sorts the sample in $O(n \log n)$; subsequent quantiles cost $O(1)$ and tail means cost $O(n)$.

## Latency sample and experiment

Construct a sample of ordinary responses centered at 24 ms with ±4 ms sinusoidal variation of period 17, adding congestion at four positions.
The congestion delays are 0.55, 1.35, 0.75, and 2.0 times the strength `congestion_scale_ms`, placed at positions $n/6$, $5n/12$, $3n/4$, and $n-3$ relative to observation count $n$.
This is a deterministic sample without random numbers, so the inputs alone determine the results.

The following cell plots type-1 and type-7 quantile curves and shades the upper tail at the selected level.
The upper table shows quantiles and tail means at representative levels; the lower table describes the tail at the selected level.
As an independent check, manually average the largest $\lfloor m \rfloor$ observations plus the fractional observation from the sorted sample, and compute the difference from `tail_mean`.
Also compute the type-7 quantile manually by linear interpolation around position $h$ in the sorted sample, and show the difference.

With the default congestion strength of 120 ms and $n = 64$, type-1 and type-7 P90 both equal 27.98 ms, within the ordinary-response range, while the upper 10% tail mean is 111.35 ms.
All four congestion latencies exceed the ordinary responses and are included in the 6.4 requests in the tail.
At P95, type 1 gives the fourth-slowest request, 87.89 ms, whereas type 7 interpolates toward the ordinary responses to give 78.91 ms; the tail mean is 179.74 ms.
The tail at $p = 0.95$ contains mass $64 \times 0.05 = 3.2$ requests, averaging the largest three observations and 0.2 of the boundary at 87.89 ms.
The difference from the manual tail mean is 0, and the type-7 difference is $2 \times 10^{-13}$ ms, both at the scale of rounding error.

At $p = 0.99$ the tail mass is only 0.64 requests, so the tail mean equals the maximum, 261.89 ms, also matching the type-1 quantile.
The mass taken from the boundary is also 0.64 requests; the estimate at this level depends solely on the slowest observation.
Type 7 interpolates between the maximum and second-largest value to return 213.62 ms.

With congestion set to 0, both quantiles and tail means stay at or below 28 ms.
The period-17 sinusoid repeats values, so all 3.2 requests in the tail at $p = 0.95$ come from tied maxima of 27.98 ms; the boundary mass equals the entire tail mass of 3.2 requests.
At congestion strength 240 ms, the P95 tail mean is 337.61 ms and the maximum is 501.89 ms, growing almost in proportion to congestion strength.

At 256 observations the fraction of four congested requests decreases, so type-1 P95 returns to 27.98 ms; 6.8 of the tail's 12.8 requests come from the tied boundary, and the tail mean is 70.79 ms.
Even when the quantile returns to the ordinary-response range, all four congestion observations remain in the tail mean.
At 32 observations the P95 tail contains only 1.6 requests; its mean of 230.85 ms depends solely on the maximum and 0.6 of the second-largest value, 182.02 ms.

## Tail means of bucketed latency

Observability systems often store counts in latency buckets instead of retaining every request's latency separately.
A sample weighted directly by these counts has the same empirical distribution as a sample repeating each bucket's representative value by its count.
scioxide-stats distinguishes two interpretations through its weight types.

- `Frequency` (`Weights::<_, Frequency>::new(&counts)`, counts of type `u64`) treats counts as observation replication counts.
  Quantiles accept all nine Hyndman–Fan definitions for the replicated sample.
  However, `tail_mean` is defined only for `TailWeights` (`Unweighted` and `Mass`), so it cannot be called on a `Frequency` distribution.
- `Mass` (`Weights::<_, Mass>::new(&masses)`, masses of type `f64`) treats weights as relative probability masses.
  Quantiles accept only the inverse weighted empirical distribution (`QuantileRule::<Mass>::inverse_ecdf()`, corresponding to type 1), while `tail_mean` uses the same apportionment as above.

To compute a tail mean from counts, convert the counts to `f64` masses and construct a `Mass` distribution.
Converting integer counts to masses preserves the empirical distribution, so the tail mean matches the replicated sample's value.
Construction sorts $K$ buckets in $O(K \log K)$, and a tail mean costs $O(K)$, independently of the request count $W$.

For five buckets (representative values 12, 25, 48, 120, and 350 ms; counts 8500, 1200, 250, 45, and 5; total $W = 10000$ requests), the following cell computes quantiles and upper tail means at the selected level through three paths.
The path that handles 10000 replicated observations with `Unweighted` independently checks the treatment of weights.
Also implement a manual calculation that takes the upper mass $(1-p)W$ from the slowest bucket downward, and compare it with the tail mean.
The `Frequency` row's tail mean and the `Mass` row's type-7 quantile display as undefined because the API does not provide them.

```rust
//| id: stats-latency-tail-api-2
//| ref: stats-latency-tail-api-2
//| title: "Compute quantiles and tail means from bucket counts"
//| run: reactive
//| crates: [scioxide-stats]
//| inputs:
//|   bucket_tail_probability: {"type":"range","label":"Tail level p [-]","description":"Changes the size of the upper mass (1−p)W.","min":0.8,"max":0.999,"step":0.001,"value":0.99}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"p999","label":"p = 0.999","description":"The 10 requests in the tail come from the two slowest buckets.","inputs":{"bucket_tail_probability":0.999}}
//|   - {"id":"p90","label":"p = 0.9","description":"The 1000 requests in the tail extend into the 25 ms bucket.","inputs":{"bucket_tail_probability":0.9}}
//|   - {"id":"p85","label":"p = 0.85","description":"The type-7 position falls between two buckets, giving a different value from type 1.","inputs":{"bucket_tail_probability":0.85}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/1/3","approx":{"value":95.5,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/2/3","approx":{"value":95.5,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/3/3","approx":{"value":95.5,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/1/5","approx":{"value":50.0,"rtol":1e-09}}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/0/1","equals":48}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/0/3","equals":null}
//|   - case: p999
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/1/3","approx":{"value":235.0,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/0/1","equals":120}
//|   - case: p90
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/1/3","approx":{"value":36.65,"rtol":1e-12}}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/1/5","approx":{"value":700.0,"rtol":1e-09}}
//|   - case: p85
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/0/1","equals":12}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/0/2","approx":{"value":13.95,"rtol":1e-09}}
//|       - {"artifact":"buckets","path":"/rows/1/5","range":{"min":0,"max":1e-09}}
use scioxide_stats::{
    EmpiricalDistribution, Frequency, Mass, QuantileMethod, QuantileProbability, QuantileRule,
    TailSide, Unweighted, Weights,
};

// Representative latency bucket values [ms] and request counts for each bucket.
let latencies = [12.0_f64, 25.0, 48.0, 120.0, 350.0];
let counts = [8500_u64, 1200, 250, 45, 5];
let p = QuantileProbability::new(bucket_tail_probability).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let (first, seventh) = (QuantileMethod::NearestRank, QuantileMethod::Linear);
// Frequency weights: quantiles accept nine definitions, but tail means are not defined.
let by_count =
    EmpiricalDistribution::new(&latencies, Weights::<_, Frequency>::new(&counts[..]))
        .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let count_q1 = by_count.quantile(p, QuantileRule::new(first));
let count_q1 = count_q1.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?.value();
let count_q7 = by_count.quantile(p, QuantileRule::new(seventh));
let count_q7 = count_q7.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?.value();
// Mass weights: converting counts to f64 masses enables tail means.
let masses: Vec<f64> = counts.iter().map(|&c| c as f64).collect();
let by_mass = EmpiricalDistribution::new(&latencies, Weights::<_, Mass>::new(&masses[..]))
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let mass_q = by_mass.quantile(p, QuantileRule::<Mass>::inverse_ecdf());
let mass_q = mass_q.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?.value();
let mass_tail = by_mass
    .tail_mean(p, TailSide::Upper)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
// Check 1: handle the sample replicated by counts without weights.
let expanded: Vec<f64> = latencies
    .iter()
    .zip(counts)
    .flat_map(|(&x, c)| std::iter::repeat_n(x, c as usize))
    .collect();
let plain = EmpiricalDistribution::new(&expanded, Unweighted).map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
let plain_q1 = plain.quantile(p, QuantileRule::new(first));
let plain_q1 = plain_q1.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?.value();
let plain_q7 = plain.quantile(p, QuantileRule::new(seventh));
let plain_q7 = plain_q7.map_err(|e| e.to_string())?.value();
let plain_tail = plain
    .tail_mean(p, TailSide::Upper)
    .map_err(|e| e.to_string())?;
// Check 2: take upper mass (1 − p)W from the slowest bucket downward.
let mut remaining = (1.0 - bucket_tail_probability) * masses.iter().sum::<f64>();
let tail_weight = remaining;
let mut tail_sum = 0.0;
for (&x, &w) in latencies.iter().zip(&masses).rev() {
    let take = w.min(remaining);
    tail_sum += take * x;
    remaining -= take;
}
let fixed2 = serde_json::json!({ "fractionDigits": 2 });
let columns = serde_json::json!([
    { "key": "0", "label": "Path" },
    { "key": "1", "label": "Type 1 [ms]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "2", "label": "Type 7 [ms]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "3", "label": "Tail mean [ms]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "4", "label": "Boundary value [ms]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2, "nullText": "—" },
    { "key": "5", "label": "Boundary mass [requests]", "type": "number", "format": fixed2, "nullText": "—" }
]);
let rows = vec![
    (
        "Frequency (counts)",
        Some(count_q1),
        Some(count_q7),
        None,
        None,
        None,
    ),
    (
        "Mass (counts converted to mass)",
        Some(mass_q),
        None,
        Some(mass_tail.mean()),
        Some(mass_tail.boundary_value()),
        mass_tail.boundary_mass(),
    ),
    (
        "Replicated sample (Unweighted)",
        Some(plain_q1),
        Some(plain_q7),
        Some(plain_tail.mean()),
        Some(plain_tail.boundary_value()),
        plain_tail.boundary_mass(),
    ),
    (
        "Manual calculation (slowest bucket first)",
        None,
        None,
        Some(tail_sum / tail_weight),
        None,
        None,
    ),
];
emit_table_with_columns!(
    columns,
    rows,
    metadata = serde_json::json!({ "id": "buckets" })
);
```

At the default $p = 0.99$, the tail contains $(1-p)W = 100$ requests: 5 at 350 ms, 45 at 120 ms, and 50 from the 48 ms bucket.
The tail mean is $(5 \times 350 + 45 \times 120 + 50 \times 48)/100 = 95.50$ ms; the `Mass` path, the replicated 10000-observation sample, and the manual calculation agree to the displayed digits.
Type-1 and type-7 P99 both equal 48 ms because the quantile position falls within one bucket.

At $p = 0.999$, the tail contains 10 requests, averaging 5 at 350 ms and 5 at 120 ms to give 235.00 ms; P99.9 is 120 ms.
At $p = 0.9$, the 1000-request tail extends through 700 requests from the 25 ms bucket, giving a tail mean of 36.65 ms.

At $p = 0.85$, the type-7 position $h = 9999 \times 0.85 = 8499.15$ falls between the final observation in the 12 ms bucket and the first in the 25 ms bucket, giving 13.95 ms for type 7 and 12 ms for type 1.
The boundary on the `Mass` path is then 12 ms, with boundary mass $2.2 \times 10^{-13}$ requests.
Binary floating point rounds $1 - 0.85$ to $0.15000000000000002$, making the tail mass slightly greater than 1500 requests; the excess is taken from the 12 ms bucket.
Its effect on the tail mean of 32.77 ms is at the scale of rounding error.

A bucket's representative value replaces latencies that originally span an interval with a single value.
Using the upper endpoint replaces each latency with a larger value, while using the lower endpoint replaces it with a smaller value, biasing the tail mean in the corresponding direction.
When bucket widths are large relative to tail values, also report the effect of the choice of representative value.

## Summary

Quantiles describe where a tail begins, while tail means describe the average magnitude beyond it; a few extremely slow requests affect tail means more strongly than quantiles.
Report the quantile type number, and when type 1 and type 7 differ greatly, check whether the estimate interpolates between observations.
At levels where the tail mass $n(1-p)$ is only a few requests, the tail mean depends on a few near-maximum observations and varies greatly between samples; collect enough observations to place a sufficient number in the tail at that level.
When computing tail means from bucket counts, convert counts to `Mass` masses and report that the result depends on the bucket representative values.
