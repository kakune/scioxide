A small residual $b-A\widetilde x$ of approximation $\widetilde x$ can accompany a large solution error when the matrix is ill-conditioned.
Verified linear computation corrects the residual with an approximate inverse and checks how well this correction bounds the true error.
We derive the method for real square matrices, then explain applicability to interval coefficients, complex values, and sparse matrices.

## Conditions for the proof

Let $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ and $b\in\mathbb R^n$, with $R$ approximating $A^{-1}$.
At this stage, neither nonsingularity of $A$ nor sufficient accuracy of $R$ is assumed.
First enclose $B=I-RA$ with interval arithmetic and obtain the following induced infinity-norm bound:

$$
q\ge\|B\|_\infty=\max_i\sum_j|B_{ij}|
$$


If $q<1$, $RA=I-B$ has an inverse given by Neumann series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B^k$.
Since the square-matrix product $RA$ is nonsingular, both $A$ and $R$ are nonsingular, and $Ax=b$ has a unique solution $x^*$.
This argument is needed to establish nonsingularity without first assuming existence of the desired solution.

### From the error equation to radii

Setting $e=x^*-\widetilde x$ and $z=R(b-A\widetilde x)$ gives

$$
e=z+Be
$$


Taking norms, setting $\|e\|_\infty\le\|z\|_\infty+q\|e\|_\infty$, and moving the right-hand error term to the left gives

$$
\|e\|_\infty\le\rho=\frac{\|z\|_\infty}{1-q}
$$


If componentwise row-sum bounds are $\mathrm{row}_i$, then furthermore $|e_i|\le|z_i|+\mathrm{row}_i\rho$.
SciOxide evaluates $z$, row sums, and division outward, and multiplies the computed radii by $1+4\varepsilon$ to allow for rounding during reverification.
$\varepsilon$ is the scalar type's machine epsilon.

For a user-supplied radius vector $r$, an alternative checks $|z|+|B|r\le r$ componentwise.
This condition says that error box $[-r,r]$ maps into itself under the error equation.
`verify` checks this condition or whether the proposal contains the contraction-map bound above.
An overly narrow proposal yielding `Rejected(EnclosureTooNarrow)` does not prove that the solution lies outside the box.
It means this sufficient condition cannot accept the proposal.

### Preconditioning and condition numbers

As $q$ approaches 1, $1/(1-q)$ grows, widening the certified radius even for a small residual.
Conversely, $q\ge1$ need not imply singularity of $A$; improving $R$ may enable the proof.
For point coefficients, the norm-error bound above is at most approximately $(1+q)/(1-q)$ times the true error, plus rounding and enclosure widths.
This ratio follows by comparing lower bound $\|e\|\ge\|z\|/(1+q)$ and upper bound $\rho$.
Do not directly extend this point-data accuracy bound to uncertainty in interval coefficients.

`bounds()` evaluates $\|A\|$, $\|R\|$, and $q$ and, when $q<1$, also reports $\|A^{-1}\|\le\|R\|/(1-q)$ and a condition-number upper bound.
A report from `solve()` does not necessarily compute all these quantities; `LinearBounds::inverse()` and `condition()` may be `None`.
Residuals, corrections, certified radii, and condition numbers are different quantities; do not combine them in one “error” column.

## API and result interpretation

For a dense point matrix, `solve(&matrix, &rhs)` is the shortest entry point.
It prepares an LU factorization and approximate inverse operator and returns only proved `LinearCertificate` results.
`Singular` from a zero LU pivot is a floating-point factorization failure, not a proof of exact singularity.

For interval coefficients or multiple right-hand sides, use `LinearVerifier` and pass problem and preconditioning operators separately.
`DenseIntervalOperator::new(lower, upper)` borrows two `MatrixView` values containing lower and upper bounds for each entry.
Represent point matrices with `point(view)` and sparse matrices with `SparseIntervalOperator` sharing a CSR structure.
Entries outside the sparse structure are exactly zero; do not omit unknown entries.
`LuIntervalInverse::from_dense` / `from_sparse` prepare approximate inverse operators from the corresponding factorizations.

Pass the right-hand side as an array of `IntervalBox`.
`point_enclosure` encloses the stored scalar value itself; `interval_enclosure` encloses specified real- and imaginary-part ranges.
For real problems, set the imaginary part to exact zero.
The complex-matrix norm uses $\sum_j(|\operatorname{Re}a_{ij}|+|\operatorname{Im}a_{ij}|)$ for each row.
This norm treats real and imaginary parts as separate components and differs from row sums of complex absolute values.

### Enclosing every system with interval coefficients

Consider $A=\operatorname{diag}(a_0,a_1)$, $a_0\in[2-\delta,2+\delta]$, $a_1\in[3-\delta,3+\delta]$, and $b=(2,3)^T$.
For $\delta<2$, every coefficient is positive and the exact solution range is $x_i\in[b_i/(b_i+\delta),b_i/(b_i-\delta)]$.
With preconditioner $R=\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1/3)$, exact arithmetic gives $q=\delta/2$.
At $\delta=2$, the input set contains singular matrices, preventing a nonsingularity proof for the entire set.

The cell's arrays are row-major, with four entries corresponding to $A_{00},A_{01},A_{10},A_{11}$.
Inputs and solutions are dimensionless.
The analytic values in the table are independent checks using ordinary f64 division; guarantees belong to the intervals produced by `LinearVerifier`.
In wasm, only analytic values are displayed.

```rust
//| id: validated-linear-uncertainty
//| ref: validated-linear-uncertainty
//| title: "Interval-coefficient width and nonsingularity proof"
//| run: button
//| crates: [scioxide-core, scioxide-linalg, scioxide-validated]
//| inputs:
//|   delta: {"type":"range","label":"Diagonal-coefficient half-width δ [-]","min":0,"max":2.5,"step":0.25,"value":0.25}
//| cases:
//|   - {"id":"singular-member","label":"δ = 2","inputs":{"delta":2}}
//| tests:
//|   - case: default
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"reference","path":"/rows/0/1","approx":{"value":0.8888888888888888,"atol":1e-14}}
//|   - case: singular-member
//|     expect:
//|       - {"artifact":"reference","path":"/rows/0/1","equals":null}
use scioxide_core::{SciOxideError, SciOxideErrorView, InvalidInputReason};
use serde_json::json;
let unsupported = |error: &SciOxideError| {
    matches!(error.view(),
SciOxideErrorView::InvalidInput { argument, reason: InvalidInputReason::UnsupportedConfiguration, .. }
if argument == scioxide_core::argument!("rounding_environment"))
};
use scioxide_linalg::MatrixView;
use scioxide_validated::{
    DenseIntervalOperator, LinearVerifier, ValidatedOptions, point_enclosure,
};
let reference: Vec<_> = [2.0, 3.0]
    .into_iter()
    .enumerate()
    .map(|(i, a)| {
        (
            i,
            (delta < a).then(|| a / (a + delta)),
            (delta < a).then(|| a / (a - delta)),
        )
    })
    .collect();
emit_table_with_columns!(
    json!([
        {"key":"0","label":"Component","type":"integer"},
        {"key":"1","label":"Analytic lower endpoint (f64 reference)","type":"number","nullText":"Unbounded"},
        {"key":"2","label":"Analytic upper endpoint (f64 reference)","type":"number","nullText":"Unbounded"}
    ]),
    reference,
    metadata = json!({"id":"reference"})
);
let proof = (|| -> Result<_, SciOxideError> {
    let lower = [2.0 - delta, 0.0, 0.0, 3.0 - delta];
    let upper = [2.0 + delta, 0.0, 0.0, 3.0 + delta];
    let r = [0.5, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0 / 3.0];
    let system = DenseIntervalOperator::new(
        MatrixView::from_slice(2, 2, &lower)?,
        MatrixView::from_slice(2, 2, &upper)?,
    )?;
    let inverse = DenseIntervalOperator::point(MatrixView::from_slice(2, 2, &r)?)?;
    let verifier = LinearVerifier::new(&system, &inverse, ValidatedOptions::new())?;
    let (report, certificate) =
        verifier.solve(&[point_enclosure(2.0)?, point_enclosure(3.0)?])?;
    let enclosure = certificate.map(|c| c.enclosure()).transpose()?;
    Ok((report, enclosure))
})();
let outcome = match proof {
    Ok((report, enclosure)) => {
        let rows: Vec<_> = (0..2)
            .map(|i| {
                let bounds = enclosure.as_ref().and_then(|x| x[i].re.bounds());
                (i, bounds.and_then(|b| b.0), bounds.and_then(|b| b.1))
            })
            .collect();
        let fmt = json!({"significantDigits":8});
        emit_table_with_columns!(
            json!([
                {"key":"0","label":"Component","type":"integer"},
                {"key":"1","label":"Verified lower endpoint","type":"number","format":fmt,"nullText":"No proof"},
                {"key":"2","label":"Verified upper endpoint","type":"number","format":fmt,"nullText":"No proof"}
            ]),
            rows,
            metadata = json!({"id":"solution"})
        );
        let q = report.bounds().map(|b| b.defect());
        emit_table_with_columns!(
            ["Upper bound on q", "Correction upper bound"],
            vec![(q, report.correction())],
            metadata = json!({"id":"diagnostics"})
        );
        let result = if report.is_verified() {
            Outcome::satisfied("Verified every system with the interval coefficients")
        } else {
            Outcome::failed("Could not prove nonsingularity with this preconditioner")
        };
        result.status(format!("{:?}", report.status()))
    }
    Err(e) if unsupported(&e) => Outcome::unavailable("Proof computation not executed in this environment"),
    Err(e) => return Err(e.to_string()),
};
emit_outcome!(outcome);
```


Increasing $\delta$ widens both the analytic solution set and the certified radius.
A certified interval is a common box for the entire set, so it need not equal the smallest analytic interval in each component.
When `Inconclusive(Reason(RegularityNotProved))`, reconsider $q$ and the preconditioner.
At $\delta\ge2$ in this experiment there is indeed a singular member, but the same status alone does not establish singularity for general inputs.

## Reusing certificates and workspaces

`LinearCertificate` owns a center and nonnegative radii; `view()` / `view_mut()` return borrows of them.
`propose(rhs, center)` computes certified radii around the user's approximate solution.
`verify(rhs, certificate.view())` reverifies an existing center and radii.
Do not reuse a previous verification status after changing the center, coefficients, or right-hand side.

Reserve `LinearWorkspace` with `workspace(right_hand_sides)` and pass it with an output borrow to `solve_into` or `propose_into`.
`solve_block_into` batches multiple right-hand sides, sharing one scan of the matrix.
Except on `Verified`, linear-system outputs are not updated; do not mistake an old certificate for a successful result from the current call.
In `LinearVerificationReport`, `status()` is the decision, while `LinearInconclusive` and `LinearProofFailure` describe reasons for an undetermined result.

A one-shot dense point-matrix call takes $O(n^3)$ operations and $O(n^2)$ storage.
Operator-based proofs add action costs: dense actions take $O(n^2)$, and sparse actions scale with stored nonzeros.
A sparse matrix alone does not make the entire proof $O(\mathrm{nnz})$.

## Summary

Assess nonsingularity with $q<1$ and solution error from correction $z$ and $1-q$.
Distinguish rejection of an overly narrow proposal from an inconclusive nonsingularity test, and use outputs only after a successful report.
Interval-coefficient guarantees cover every member, so first determine the meaning of the input.
The [Hilbert matrix example](../../examples/hilbert-verified-solve/) further examines coefficient rounding and differences in the object being certified.

Contraction-based verification methods are covered in detail in Neumaier, *Interval Methods for Systems of Equations* (Cambridge University Press, 1990), and Rump's [Verification methods (2010)](https://www.tuhh.de/ti3/rump/intlab/ActaNumerica2010.pdf).
